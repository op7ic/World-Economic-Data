# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1904

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1904 sat near the high-water mark of the first modern globalization. Most leading economies operated inside the classical gold standard, international trade was tied together by steamship, railway, cable, and bill finance, and London remained the core clearing house for global capital. The year was not one of a single synchronized world boom. It was a mixed year in which the United States was still working through the 1903 industrial and financial downturn, Germany continued to deepen its industrial base, Argentina and Australia benefited from export and settlement dynamics, and Japan diverted a large share of fiscal and financial capacity into war.

National cycles diverged. In the Maddison Project Database 2023, real GDP fell 1.26% in the United States and 1.23% in Spain, rose 0.50% in the United Kingdom and 0.73% in France, and grew 4.00% in Germany, 6.72% in Australia and 10.73% in Argentina. U.S. official returns show crude steel output down 4.64% and pig iron down 8.40%, while crude petroleum rose 16.53%; the NBER business-cycle chronology dates the trough of the recession that began in September 1902 to August 1904. In the Federico-Tena world trade series, real world merchandise exports rose 2.11%.

The central geopolitical-economic event was the Russo-Japanese War, which began in February 1904. It mattered not only as a military conflict, but also as a test of global capital markets. Japan financed a major part of the war through foreign loans in London and New York, while Russia drew on European financial connections and domestic fiscal resources. The Japanese cabinet authorized foreign borrowing on 17 February, and the Bank of Japan raised its discount rate on commercial bills from 5.84% to 6.57% on 2 July and to 7.30% on 19 December. The conflict helped prove that a non-Western industrializing state could mobilize modern finance, shipping, manufacturing, and taxation at scale. Its full political meaning would be clearer after 1905, but its capital-market consequences were already visible in 1904.

The ranking of large economies was still dominated by population-heavy agrarian empires and by the industrial North Atlantic. Using Maddison Project style historical estimates, the United States was the largest economy by real purchasing-power-adjusted output, followed by China, the United Kingdom, India, Germany, a Russian Empire proxy based on former-USSR territory, and France. China and India remained enormous in aggregate because of population, but with low output per person. The United States, United Kingdom, Belgium, Australia, and Canada were much richer per person, reflecting industrialization, resource endowments, high capital intensity, and deeper financial systems. The China and Russian-proxy levels, like those for the Poland and Czechoslovakia territories, are log-linear interpolations between Maddison benchmark years rather than annual observations. These 2011-dollar levels are not comparable with the 1990-dollar levels used for the 1903 ranking, so growth cannot be computed from the two years' level tables.

Microeconomically, 1904 was a year of industrial scale, commodity extraction, and infrastructure formation rather than a year of mass consumer technology. Steel, coal, railways, ports, textiles, electrical equipment, chemicals, shipbuilding, and finance were the commanding sectors. Oil was growing quickly, especially in the United States, but coal still dominated energy systems. Corporate capitalism was consolidating in the United States and Germany, zaibatsu and state-directed industrial firms were central in Japan, and colonial export systems shaped India, Indonesia, and large parts of Africa and Asia.

The most important hindsight lesson is that 1904 looked stable because the gold standard imposed predictable exchange rates and because London could intermediate global liquidity. That stability was conditional. The structure depended on confidence, gold flows, short-term credit, imperial security, and political restraint. Within three years the United States would experience the Panic of 1907, and within a decade the classical gold standard would break under the stress of World War I. Those later events should not be read back as inevitable in 1904, but the vulnerabilities were already present.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1904

**Theme 1: Classical gold standard stability with hidden fragility.** The dominant monetary architecture was the classical gold standard. Major currencies were tied to gold at fixed legal or conventional parities, which stabilized exchange rates among gold-standard economies and reduced currency risk in trade finance. Gold convertibility also limited discretionary monetary policy. Central banks and financial authorities defended reserves through discount-rate changes, fiscal caution, and gold-market operations. In 1904, this regime made international trade and capital flows look orderly, but it also transmitted financial stress across countries.

The United Kingdom was the key node. Bank of England Bank Rate moved from 4.00 percent in the first quarter to 3.00 percent in the remaining quarters of 1904. The United States had no central bank, so liquidity was managed through the National Banking System, Treasury operations, private clearing houses, and New York money-market mechanisms. Germany, France, and Japan operated within gold-linked monetary systems, but their official rates moved in different directions: the Reichsbank discount rate averaged 5.0% in December against 4.0% a year earlier, the Banque de France rate stayed at 3.0%, and the Bank of Japan raised its rate twice. The forward linkage is clear: the gold standard survived 1904 but its reliance on confidence and short-term liquidity would become more visible in the 1907 panic and then fatal in 1914.

**Theme 2: War finance as an international capital-market test.** The Russo-Japanese War changed the economic conversation in 1904. Japan had industrialized rapidly after the Meiji Restoration, but war with Russia tested whether that industrialization could be converted into military procurement, shipping, taxation, and international borrowing. Japan raised loans abroad, especially in London and New York, while using domestic fiscal measures and financial institutions to sustain the war. In the Macrohistory Database, Japanese public debt rose from 22.90% to 35.63% of GDP and the current account moved from a deficit of 0.22% to one of 4.44% of GDP; the issue terms and proceeds of the individual foreign loans are Data not available. Russia faced the costs of fighting at long distance, dependent on the Trans-Siberian Railway and European finance, and on 14 February declared a list of contraband goods, several categories depending on enemy destination or use, which raised risks for neutral shipping.

The affected economies were Japan, Russia, the United Kingdom, France, Germany, and the United States. The war linked East Asian strategy to Western capital markets. Its forward linkage was large: Japan's victory in 1905 would change the balance of power in East Asia, while Russia's defeat would contribute to domestic crisis and revolution in 1905.

**Theme 3: Uneven industrialization across the top economies.** The United States, Germany, and the United Kingdom were the central industrial economies, but they were not industrializing in the same way. The United States had scale, railways, steel, petroleum, machinery, and large domestic markets. Germany had advanced chemicals, electrical engineering, coal, steel, and universal banks. The United Kingdom retained shipping, textiles, finance, coal, and imperial trade, but its relative industrial lead was narrowing. In the comparative tables of the 1912 Belgian statistical yearbook, crude steel output rose 8.89% in Germany and 13.48% in France in 1904 but fell 4.65% in the United States and 0.16% in Great Britain and Ireland.

The data show a large gap between aggregate GDP and GDP per capita. China and India were among the top economies by total output, but their per-capita output was far below the industrial economies. This contrast is one of the most important structural facts of 1904. It foreshadowed the twentieth century's divergence between industrial and agrarian economies, and later the difficult transition from agrarian labor absorption to industrial and service-sector productivity growth.

**Theme 4: Commodity, railway, and migration capitalism.** The world economy in 1904 still ran through commodity frontiers. Grain, wool, meat, sugar, rubber, coffee, tea, tobacco, metals, coal, and petroleum shaped capital flows and settlement patterns. Canada, Argentina, Australia, Mexico, and the Netherlands East Indies benefited from commodity exports and foreign capital, though distributional gains were uneven. Railways connected interior production zones to ports, while steamships and refrigerated shipping integrated food and raw-material markets. In British market quotations, Rio coffee rose 23.33%, middling American cotton 9.45% and silver 7.17%, while Newcastle steam coal fell 9.52%.

Migration was a major labor-market mechanism. The United States recorded very large immigrant inflows in 1904, mostly from Europe: 812,870 arrivals in the fiscal year ended 30 June 1904, 5.15% fewer than a year earlier. Argentina, Canada, and Australia also used migration and land settlement as part of their growth models. The forward linkage was the continued expansion of settler economies before World War I, followed by later restrictions on migration and capital flows.

**Theme 5: Corporate scale and managerial capitalism.** 1904 belonged to the era of large-scale corporations and trusts. United States Steel, Standard Oil, major railroads, large banks, and electrical firms demonstrated the corporate scale that had emerged by the turn of the century. Germany's chemicals and electrical sectors were similarly organized around large firms and bank relationships; leading chemical companies formed loose alliances in 1904, more than two decades before the 1925 IG Farben merger. Japan's zaibatsu and state-linked industrial capacity showed a different institutional path. Legal limits also mattered: on 14 March the U.S. Supreme Court upheld the injunction against the Northern Securities railroad holding-company combination.

The year also illustrates early automobile globalization. Ford Motor Company was still young, but in 1904 it formed Ford Motor Company of Canada and built its first international production presence in Ontario; the Canadian company was incorporated on 17 August and began production in late September. Automobiles were not yet a mass-market macro driver, but the organizational and technological direction was visible.

**Theme 6: Colonial and imperial economic structures.** A large share of global production was governed through empires, colonies, protectorates, and semi-colonial treaty-port systems. India was part of the British Empire, Indonesia was the Netherlands East Indies, and China was formally sovereign but constrained by foreign treaty rights, indemnities, and tariff limitations. African, Southeast Asian, and Caribbean production was deeply shaped by imperial trade, plantation systems, mining concessions, and shipping routes.

This matters for measurement. Many historical GDP series are modern-country or modern-territory estimates applied backward. They are useful but not the same as national accounts created by sovereign states. The forward linkage is that decolonization and national statistical systems would later change both economic institutions and the quality of data.

**Theme 7: Institutions changed ahead of measured outcomes.** Several 1904 measures created frameworks whose effects lay mostly in later years. Australia's Commonwealth Conciliation and Arbitration Act of 15 December covered interstate industrial disputes, with explicit exclusions for agriculture and domestic service, rather than setting a universal wage. Sweden's Riksbank took up its note-issue monopoly at the turn of 1903-04 under an 1897 statute. The British Finance Act, assented on 1 August, changed tea and tobacco duties from 20 April. In the Netherlands East Indies, payroll deductions for postal savings by civil and military employees began on 1 September. Forward linkage: wage arbitration, central-bank note monopolies and institutional saving schemes became standard features of twentieth-century economies.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The monetary environment was defined by the classical gold standard, not by modern central-bank inflation targeting. Exchange-rate stability among gold currencies reduced nominal FX uncertainty, but it also meant that gold outflows and reserve pressures could force policy tightening. The Bank of England remained the key global rate-setting institution because of London's role in bills, shipping finance, and capital exports.

The United Kingdom cut or normalized its policy stance in 1904: Bank Rate was 4.00 percent in the first quarter and 3.00 percent in the second, third, and fourth quarters; its December monthly average was 3.0%, against 4.0% in December 1903. The United States had no central bank and no single policy rate. Liquidity was provided or constrained through national banks, New York call money, Treasury balances, and private clearing-house arrangements; the Macrohistory Database money-market rate fell from 3.71% to 1.78%. Germany and France had central banks: in the NBER Macrohistory official-rate series, the Reichsbank discount rate averaged 5.0% in December 1904 against 4.0% in December 1903, while the Banque de France rate averaged 3.0% in both months. The Austro-Hungarian Bank's official discount rate, common to the whole monarchy, was 3.50% in both 1903 and 1904. The Bank of Japan Head Office rate on commercial bills other than collection bills rose from 5.84% to 6.57% on 2 July and to 7.30% on 19 December.

Bretton Woods was not relevant. It would not be created until 1944. The 1904 world used gold convertibility, gold points, bills of exchange, and capital controls only in limited cases compared with later wartime and interwar systems. Many colonial currencies were managed through imperial monetary boards or fixed relationships to metropolitan currencies.

Equity markets were recovering unevenly from earlier stress. In the United States, the Dow Jones Industrial Average rose strongly in 1904 after a large negative return in 1903. The Dow ended 1904 around 69.61 and recorded an annual price return of roughly 41.74 percent. ### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

1904 was a trade-integrated but politically imperial world. Major trade flows were shaped by empire, tariffs, shipping routes, and commodity frontiers. The United Kingdom remained a global trading and financial hub. Germany's industrial exports were rising. The United States was increasingly important as an industrial exporter, while still absorbing immigrants and capital. India, Indonesia, China, and other Asian economies were tied into global markets through colonial administration, treaty-port structures, commodity exports, and imported manufactures.

The main geopolitical disruption was the Russo-Japanese War. It redirected fiscal resources, affected capital-market sentiment, and strengthened the relationship between military capacity and financial credibility. Another important infrastructure event was the transfer of the Panama Canal project to U.S. control and the start of the U.S. canal-building era. The canal convention, signed on 18 November 1903, was ratified and proclaimed on 26 February 1904, and the project was handed over on 4 May. The canal was not yet operational, but the economic rationale was already clear: shorter maritime routes, cheaper intercoastal shipping, and greater strategic control.

Trade policy remained selective rather than liberalizing across the board. The Anglo-French declaration of 8 April settled commercial equality in Egypt and Morocco and Suez passage; it was not a customs union. Brazil reduced duties by 20% on specified U.S. goods from 20 April. In China, an imperial decree of 17 May opened Jinan to foreign trade, a port-specific measure, and Hunan approved provincial mining regulations, reported on 22 March, that restricted share ownership to Chinese holders.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Coal was the dominant industrial energy source. Britain, Germany, the United States, Belgium, and parts of the Habsburg and Russian economic spaces depended heavily on coal for industry, railways, heating, and power. Petroleum was growing but not yet the central fuel of transport and geopolitics. The United States was the leading oil producer, and Pennsylvania, Texas, and other producing regions were increasingly important. Standard Oil remained central to refining and distribution. U.S. crude petroleum output rose from 100.5 to 117.1 million barrels (+16.53%), while its value rose only 6.84%; bituminous coal fell 1.27% to 279.2 million short tons and Pennsylvania anthracite 1.95% to 65.3 million long tons. Metals were central to industrial growth. Steel supported railways, ships, bridges, machinery, and armaments. ### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor markets in 1904 cannot be compared using modern unemployment definitions. Many economies had no official unemployment rate, and agrarian underemployment was not measured consistently. Urban labor markets were increasingly organized through factories, ports, railways, mines, and construction. Wages varied by country, skill, gender, sector, and region, with strong differences between industrial cores and agrarian peripheries. Historical estimates exist for a few economies: U.S. unemployment rose from 2.6% to 4.8% in Lebergott's estimate (5.4% in the Macrohistory Database), and the Macrohistory Database gives 6.0% for the United Kingdom, 2.1% for Germany, 10.2% for France, 3.53% for the Netherlands and 9.4% for Australia, on differing union-based or reconstructed definitions.

Migration was economically important. The United States recorded 812,870 alien arrivals in the fiscal year ended 30 June 1904 according to contemporary immigration reporting, against 857,046 a year earlier. Canada, Argentina, and Australia also absorbed migrants and used settlement as part of agricultural and resource expansion. Urbanization continued in industrial economies, while China and India remained predominantly rural. Demographic size strongly shaped aggregate GDP rankings, but productivity and capital intensity determined GDP per capita.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States remained the world's largest economy in Maddison-style output, but 1904 was still affected by the 1903 industrial and financial downturn. Railroads, steel, securities markets, and credit conditions had weakened in 1903, and recovery was uneven. The absence of a central bank meant the financial system relied on the National Banking System, New York money markets, Treasury balances, and private clearing houses.

The main structural story was scale. The United States had a huge internal market, high immigration, expanding manufacturing, extensive railways, and leading oil and steel sectors. ### 5C. Innovation & technology

The economically significant technologies of 1904 were not digital or electronic in the modern sense. The key technologies were steel production, electrical machinery, chemical processes, railways, steam turbines, internal-combustion engines, telegraphy, telephony, port infrastructure, and shipbuilding. The Panama Canal project, transferred to U.S. control on 4 May and entering the U.S. construction era, was one of the most significant infrastructure commitments of the year. New York's first subway line opened on 27 October.

Automobiles were technologically visible but not yet macroeconomically dominant. Ford's Canadian move mattered because it showed early cross-border production and market strategy. Aviation was experimental. Electrification was more important immediately, especially for factories, cities, tramways, and communication systems. Telephone networks were spreading even in colonial economies: local subscribers in the Netherlands East Indies rose 6.99% to 4,793.

The United States benefited from mass immigration and expanding schooling, though quality varied. Colonial economies often had limited access to broad education, which constrained productivity growth and institutional development.

Unions and labor movements were significant in industrial countries, but cross-country comparable labor-market data are weak. Strikes, workplace regulation, factory legislation, and socialist or labor parties were part of the political economy in Europe, Australia, and North America. Australia's Commonwealth Conciliation and Arbitration Act of 15 December 1904 set up conciliation and arbitration for industrial disputes extending beyond one state, with agriculture and domestic service excluded. Indian factory employment kept growing: average daily employment in cotton mills rose 4.39% to 186,271 and in jute mills 4.18% to 123,869 in the official 1903-04 returns. ### 6C. Currencies

The core exchange-rate system was gold-standard convertibility. Gold fixed the U.S. dollar at $20.67 per troy ounce. Britain fixed sterling to gold, and gold parities stabilized the dollar-sterling relationship. The French franc, German mark, and many other major currencies were also gold-linked. Annual rates in the Macrohistory Database barely moved among gold currencies: sterling at 0.2054 pounds per USD (-0.10%), the French franc at 5.1662 (-0.06%), the guilder at 2.4784 (-0.30%) and the Italian lira at 5.17 (unchanged). The yen, also on gold, averaged 2.0305 per USD in December against 2.0265 a year earlier.

This did not mean every currency was equally stable or every country had the same regime. Silver, colonial currency systems, managed pegs, and local banking arrangements complicated China, India, Mexico, and parts of Asia and Latin America. The Mexican silver peso strengthened from 2.37 to 2.16 per USD (-8.86% in pesos per dollar) while London silver quotations rose 7.17%, and the Spanish peseta weakened from 6.99 to 7.13 (+2.00%). Council bills on India realized an average 16.049d per rupee in fiscal 1903-04. Exact annual average exchange rates for all top 20 economies are Data not available.

### 6D. Alternative assets

Gold was the key alternative asset and monetary reserve. Its official price in the United States was fixed at $20.67 per troy ounce, so it did not behave like a floating market asset in the modern sense. Real estate data for major cities in 1904 are limited. Hedonic asking-price indices built from newspaper listings by the Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia's Historical Housing Prices project show New York asking prices down 1.94% and asking rents up 4.21%, and Boston asking prices up 6.21% with rents up 0.24%; these are asking prices, not transaction prices or returns. Farmland, mines, rail concessions, and commodity inventories were important stores of wealth and sources of collateral.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The largest active crisis was the Russo-Japanese War. It created fiscal stress, capital-market exposure, shipping and insurance risk, and geopolitical uncertainty across East Asia and Europe. At the time, it was clear that the war mattered, but the full political consequences were not yet knowable. With hindsight, the war helped weaken the Russian state and strengthened Japan's regional position. Japanese public debt rose from 22.90% to 35.63% of GDP in a single year, and Russia's contraband declaration widened the risks for neutral shipping.

A second risk was U.S. financial fragility. The recovery in the Dow during 1904 did not mean the financial architecture was stable. The United States lacked a central bank and depended on seasonal liquidity, call-money markets, clearing houses, and Treasury actions. With hindsight, this vulnerability would become visible in the Panic of 1907. The NBER chronology places the end of the 1902-04 recession in August 1904, while unemployment on Lebergott's estimate was still 4.8%.

A third risk was overconfidence in gold-standard stability. Fixed exchange rates made trade and lending easier, but they also tied economies to gold flows and market confidence. The system worked in 1904 because investors expected convertibility, fiscal discipline, and political continuity. It was not designed to absorb a general European war.

A fourth risk was colonial and agrarian vulnerability. India, China, Indonesia, Russia proxy, Mexico, and other economies had large rural populations with limited buffers against crop failure, tax pressure, debt, and price swings. Modern social insurance was minimal or absent in many places. Urban public-health policy could itself trigger unrest: in Brazil, outside the top 20, a vaccination law approved on 31 October was followed, after proposed regulations were published on 9 November, by unrest in Rio de Janeiro from 10 November.

A fifth risk was geopolitical infrastructure competition. Railways, canals, ports, and naval power were economic assets and strategic assets at the same time. The Panama Canal project, the Trans-Siberian Railway, East Asian concessions, and naval construction all show how infrastructure and geopolitics were intertwined.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1904 vs. 1903)

The biggest year-over-year change was the move from financial and industrial weakness in 1903 toward partial recovery in 1904, especially in the United States equity market. The Dow's strong 1904 rebound followed a large 1903 fall: year-end closes rose from 49.11 to 69.61, and the Macrohistory U.S. equity total return swung from -14.04% to +30.29%. Real output for the United States, however, was still negative in the Maddison-derived estimate, showing that financial recovery and real economic recovery did not align perfectly. U.S. growth moved from +4.87% to -1.26% in the Maddison Project Database 2023, consumer-price inflation slowed from 2.92% to 0.94% and the money-market rate halved from 3.71% to 1.78%.

The second major delta was war. In 1903, Japan and Russia were in escalating strategic tension. In 1904, they were at war. This altered fiscal priorities, capital-market flows, shipping risk, and the economic meaning of East Asian infrastructure. Japan's ability to borrow abroad became one of the defining economic facts of the conflict. Japanese growth fell from +10.91% to +0.03%, the Bank of Japan discount rate rose from 5.84% to 7.30%, and the current account moved from -0.22% to -4.44% of GDP.

The third delta was continued industrial divergence. Germany's 1904 growth estimate was stronger than those for Britain and France, reinforcing the trend toward German industrial catch-up and specialization in chemicals, electrical equipment, steel, and machinery. The United States remained largest by output, but its short-run cycle was weaker. German crude steel output rose 8.89% while U.S. output fell 4.65%; British growth turned from -1.18% to +0.50%, and Bank Rate ended the year a point lower than in late 1903 while the Reichsbank rate ended a point higher.

The fourth delta was the continued expansion of commodity frontier economies. Argentina and Australia show strong estimated growth, and Canada remained in a settlement and resource-expansion phase. These economies were tied closely to British capital, migration, railways, and global commodity prices. Argentina's growth slowed from +14.33% to +10.73% and Australia's from +7.89% to +6.72%, while Canada's current-account deficit widened from 6.26% to 8.03% of GDP. Real world merchandise exports rose 2.11% in the Federico-Tena series.

The fifth delta was infrastructure commitment. The U.S. assumption of the Panama Canal project in 1904 did not immediately change trade routes, but it marked a concrete shift in future shipping economics and U.S. strategic capacity. The canal's later opening would matter for interoceanic trade, naval deployment, and logistics. The canal convention signed in November 1903 was proclaimed on 26 February 1904, and the works were handed over on 4 May.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

**Highest-confidence data:** The Maddison Project style population, GDP per capita, and implied GDP estimates are the best available consistent long-run framework for comparing 1904 economies. The Bank of England Bank Rate series, the U.S. gold price parity, the Federal Reserve founding date, U.S. crude oil production, and the Dow Jones annual return are relatively well-documented. Official U.S. mineral and immigration returns, the dated official discount rates of the Bank of Japan, Reichsbank, Banque de France and Austro-Hungarian Bank, and dated legal events such as the Northern Securities decision, the Panama convention, the Anglo-French declaration, the Finance Act and the Australian arbitration act are also well documented.

**Medium-confidence data:** Maddison estimates for industrial economies such as the United States, United Kingdom, Germany, France, Italy, Belgium, Canada, Netherlands, Australia, and Japan are usable for broad comparisons, but they are still reconstructed historical estimates. They are not official 1904 national accounts. Spain and Austria are graded medium-low, and Mexico and Argentina low, consistent with the grades given to these economies, or to Austria-Hungary, for 1903. Retrospective consumer prices, unemployment, debt, current-account, budget, exchange-rate, credit, house-price and return series from the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database are reconstructions with differing perimeters; they are used under the CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 license. The Federico-Tena trade, commodity-price and population series are used under CC BY 4.0.

**Low-confidence data:** China, the Russian Empire proxy, Poland, Czechoslovakia territory, India, Indonesia, and other colonial or imperial territories require extra caution. Some estimates are interpolated. Others refer to modern territories projected backward rather than sovereign states. The Russian Empire proxy is particularly weak because former-USSR territory does not equal exact 1904 imperial borders. The China, Russian proxy, Poland and Czechoslovakia levels, populations, per-capita figures and growth rates are log-linear interpolations between Maddison benchmark years (1900 with 1911, 1913 or 1910); the Maddison Project Database 2023 has no annual 1904 record for any of them, so no observed value replaces them and their growth rates carry no information about the 1904 cycle.

**Unavailable data:** Comparable CPI inflation, unemployment, policy rates, debt-to-GDP, current-account balances, budget balances, housing prices, corporate revenue rankings, total-return equity indexes, credit spreads, and household balance-sheet data are Data not available for most of the interpolated, colonial and non-sovereign units. Where modern-style indicators appear in tables, they are filled mainly for the industrial economies with Macrohistory coverage; elsewhere they are explicitly marked Data not available.

**Conflicting data issues:** GDP rankings can differ depending on whether the analyst uses 1990 international Geary-Khamis dollars, 2011 international dollars, nominal exchange-rate conversion, modern-country territories, historical state borders, or empire aggregates. The ranking uses a consistent Maddison Project style PPP framework, but readers should not treat the numerical ranking as exact to the last digit. Specific conflicts and definitional breaks follow.

**Unit break with 1903:** The 1903 ranking is in 1990 international dollars on historical units (British India, Austria-Hungary, Dutch East Indies, Ottoman Empire), while the 1904 ranking is in 2011 international dollars on Maddison Project Database 2023 territorial records. Levels and ranks cannot be compared across the two years, and growth must not be computed from them; the growth rates shown come from within the 2023 database. For India, Indonesia and Austria the 1903 comparators in the indicator tables are taken from the same database records, because the 1903 table's units differ.

**Growth definition:** The table and indicator rows show the arithmetic percentage change of the database's real GDP, and the log change of the same levels is given in the indicator notes. The two differ noticeably only for fast-growing economies (Argentina 10.73% against 10.19%, Australia 6.72% against 6.51%).

**Spain:** The level and per-capita figures shown (50,673 million and 2,660) differ slightly from the Maddison Project Database 2023 record (50,617 million and 2,657), whose growth (-1.23%) is shown.

**Overlap:** The Germany record uses 1870 frontiers, which include territory also counted in the later-border Poland estimate, and the United Kingdom record includes the whole of Ireland. The 3.112 trillion top-20 sum therefore double-counts part of Poland's territory.

**U.S. unemployment:** Lebergott's estimate (4.8%) is shown; the Macrohistory Database gives 5.4%. Both are reconstructions, and the comparator for 1903 (2.6%) is Lebergott's.

**Equity series:** The Dow figures are year-end closes (49.11 and 69.61), while the 1903 assessment used the NBER Dow series, whose monthly figures are midpoints of each month's high and low (46.90 in December 1903); the two are different measurements and should not be chained.

**Belgium:** The Macrohistory consumer-price index implies a 12.36% fall in 1904, which no other series corroborates, so no Belgian CPI is shown; its current-account field is a merchandise balance; its exchange-rate quote is inconsistent with the French rate under the Latin Monetary Union.

**Germany:** The Macrohistory exchange rate is stored in later currency units, so no 1904 mark rate is shown; the German current-account ratio uses a nominal GDP denominator linked through net-national-product growth.

**Japan:** The general-account budget balance omits the extraordinary war account, so no war-year budget ratio is shown.

**Australia:** The Macrohistory exchange rate is quoted in later Australian dollars; the pound figure shown divides it by two, a conversion the source itself does not assert.

**Crude oil:** The Bureau of Transportation Statistics compilation gives about 117.081 million barrels and the calendar-year returns in the Statistical Abstract 117,063,421 barrels; the $0.86 per barrel figure is production value divided by quantity, not a market price.

**Immigration:** The 812,870 arrivals refer to the fiscal year ended 30 June 1904, not the calendar year.

**Weakest claims:** Any claim about sectoral growth rates outside the major industrial economies is weak unless backed by national historical statistics not reproduced here. Any claim about unemployment is especially weak because definitions varied or did not exist. Any claim about corporate rankings by revenue or market capitalization is weak because globally comparable 1904 corporate datasets are not available. The issue terms and proceeds of Japan's 1904 foreign loans, a consolidated Russian war budget and a world capital-flow balance are not established.
