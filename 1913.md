# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1913

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1913 was the final full year of the pre-First World War world economy. It sat at the peak of the classical gold-standard and first-globalization order: large cross-border capital flows, high migration, fast transoceanic shipping, dense railway networks, and a trade share of world output near its prewar high. In Maddison-style historical purchasing-power estimates, the world economy was still dominated by a small group of industrial powers, but the largest population economies, China, the Russian Empire, and British India, remained among the biggest by total GDP even though their per capita income was far lower than that of the United States, Britain, Germany, France, Canada, Argentina, or Australia.

The dominant macroeconomic regime was not Bretton Woods, managed floating, or modern inflation targeting. It was the classical gold standard, with sterling and London at the center of international finance and with gold parities anchoring major currencies. This created confidence for cross-border trade and lending but also transmitted monetary tightening and balance-of-payments pressure across countries. There was no IMF, World Bank, OPEC, BIS, EEC, ASEAN, or modern central bank policy framework. Central banks and quasi-central banks defended convertibility, managed discount rates, and protected gold reserves. The United States did not yet have an operating central bank; the Federal Reserve Act was signed only on December 23, 1913, and the Federal Reserve Banks opened on November 16, 1914.

The year was economically mixed rather than uniformly buoyant. The world was highly integrated, but parts of the North Atlantic economy were already slowing. Canada's wheat boom weakened as international wheat prices fell; the English Gazette wheat quotation fell 8.63%. The United States experienced a cyclical downturn and financial-market unease, even as it made a major institutional turn through the Federal Reserve Act and the Revenue Act of 1913 (the Underwood-Simmons tariff and income tax). Germany remained the most dynamic European industrial challenger, Britain retained its financial and maritime primacy, France remained a major capital exporter, and Japan continued to consolidate Meiji-era industrialization. Russia, China, India, Indonesia, Brazil, and Mexico were large economies in total output because of population and land, but their statistical systems and national income measurement are much less reliable than those of Western Europe and North America.

Annual measures show the mix. In the Maddison Project Database 2023, real GDP grew 3.33% in the United States, 4.49% in Germany and 3.75% in the United Kingdom, but fell 0.57% in France and 6.02% in revolutionary Mexico. The Federico-Tena reconstruction shows world merchandise-export volume up 4.48% and world population at about 1,795.2 million, up 1.01%. Equity prices fell in most markets with long-run return data: the reconstructed US price index fell 14.29% and the UK common-equity price index 8.75% between Decembers.

Microeconomically, 1913 was a year of mechanism, not only a year of macro aggregates. Mass production reached a milestone when Ford introduced the moving assembly line at Highland Park. BASF opened its synthetic-ammonia plant at Oppau. Railway and steamship networks structured global grain, meat, coal, textile, and metal markets. Telephones, telegraphs, electric power, chemicals, steel, and internal-combustion transport were changing firm scale and labor organization. Agriculture was still the largest employer in much of the world, while heavy industry, finance, and urban consumer goods were the leading engines of structural change in the richest economies.

The most important hindsight point is stark: many strengths of 1913 were also vulnerabilities. Gold convertibility, high capital mobility, food import dependence, imperial trade routes, and dense financial connections raised efficiency in peace but made the global economy fragile when geopolitical risk became real. The war of 1914 did not simply interrupt a self-contained national economy; it broke an integrated international system.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1913

**Theme 1: Peak first globalization before systemic rupture.** The central theme of 1913 was the maturity of the first modern globalization. International trade relative to world output was near its prewar high. Transport-cost declines, steam shipping, railways, telegraphy, and gold-standard currency stability supported an integrated market for grain, meat, coal, metals, textiles, and capital. The major beneficiaries were Britain, Germany, France, the United States, Canada, Argentina, Australia, Japan, and the colonial port economies of Asia.

The significance is that 1913 became a benchmark year in economic history. Many later studies compare 1929, 1950, 1973, or 2000 with 1913 to measure how far globalization collapsed and later recovered; the Federico-Tena world export volume index itself uses 1913 = 100. In 1913, the global trade system still appeared resilient, but that was partly because geopolitical risk was underpriced. The forward link is direct: 1914-1918 destroyed gold convertibility in practice, redirected shipping, subordinated markets to war finance, and reduced the automaticity of the prewar international order.

**Theme 2: Classical gold standard at its high point.** The monetary regime of 1913 was anchored by gold, not by discretionary central bank inflation targets. Britain, Germany, France, the United States, Japan, Canada, Australia, and several Latin American economies operated in or near gold-standard arrangements. Sterling remained the most important international financial currency because London was the leading money market, insurance center, shipping finance center, and international bill market.

The economic significance is two-sided. Gold convertibility reduced exchange-rate uncertainty among participating countries and supported long-distance contracting: annual dollar exchange rates of the major gold currencies moved by less than 1% in 1913. It also created a deflationary and contractionary bias when gold reserves, external deficits, or banking stress forced central banks to tighten. Countries most affected included Britain, France, Germany, the United States, Japan, Argentina, and Canada. The forward link is that wartime suspension and interwar restoration attempts became major sources of economic instability.

**Theme 3: Institutional reform in the United States.** The United States entered 1913 as the world's largest economy by Maddison total-GDP estimates, but it had no operating central bank. Its monetary system relied on national banks, seasonal currency mechanisms, New York money-market liquidity, and emergency private coordination. The Federal Reserve Act, signed in December, was a structural break, even though the Fed was not yet operational during most of the year. The Underwood-Simmons tariff, enacted as the Revenue Act of October 3, 1913, also shifted the fiscal base by lowering tariff rates and introducing a federal income tax after the Sixteenth Amendment.

This theme mattered because the United States combined industrial scale with financial under-institutionalization. Its large domestic market, railways, steel, oil, machinery, and mass manufacturing were world-leading, but periodic money-market stress remained a weakness. The forward link is that the United States became more central to global finance after 1914, especially as European capital markets were strained by war.

**Theme 4: Industrial rivalry and uneven modernization.** Germany, the United States, Britain, France, and Japan were the key industrial powers, but their sectoral profiles differed. The United States led in scale manufacturing, railways, oil, steel, machinery, and mass-market consumer durables. Germany was strong in chemicals, electrical equipment, steel, machine tools, and scientific industry. Britain led in finance, shipping, coal, textiles, and imperial trade networks, while France combined agriculture, luxury goods, metallurgy, and capital exports. Japan was industrializing quickly, with textiles, shipbuilding, railways, and state-guided industrial policy.

The significance is that 1913 was not a simple story of one hegemon. Britain still dominated finance and shipping, Germany was the key European industrial challenger, and the United States was the largest economy but not yet the undisputed organizer of the world monetary system. The forward link is that war mobilization and postwar debt flows changed relative power more quickly than peacetime trend growth would have done.

**Theme 5: Population size versus productivity.** China, British India, and the Russian Empire ranked very high by total GDP, but their GDP per capita was far below that of Western Europe, North America, Argentina, and Australia. This gap reflected differences in industrialization, urbanization, literacy, infrastructure, state capacity, health, technology adoption, and agricultural productivity. These economies were not economically small, but they were less monetized, less industrialized, and statistically harder to measure.

The theme is significant because total GDP and GDP per capita told different stories. China and India mattered enormously as production and consumption systems, but their global role was constrained by low per capita output, colonial or semi-colonial constraints, and weak fiscal capacity. The forward link is that the twentieth century became partly a story of whether large population economies could close productivity gaps.

**Theme 6: Agriculture, migration, and frontier capitalism.** Agricultural exports from Canada, Argentina, Australia, Russia, the United States, India, and parts of Southeast Asia formed a large share of world trade. Migration and land settlement shaped labor supply and capital demand in the Americas and Oceania. Canada's prairie wheat boom weakened in 1913 as wheat prices fell, while Argentina remained a major exporter of beef, wheat, maize, and wool.

The significance is that global agriculture was not a backward residue; it was a central part of international capitalism. Railways, refrigerated shipping, grain elevators, port finance, futures markets, and land credit were the microeconomic infrastructure of the food system. The forward link is that war disrupted trade routes and changed demand for grain, meat, and raw materials.

**Theme 7: Corporate scale, technology, and labor organization.** Ford's moving assembly line in 1913 symbolized a shift toward standardized, high-throughput mass production. In steel, chemicals, electrical machinery, oil, shipping, finance, and railways, large firms and cartels were already central. Labor organization, unionization, and wage bargaining were uneven but increasingly important in industrial economies.

This theme matters because productivity growth came through firm-level organization as much as through national policy. The countries most affected were the United States, Germany, Britain, France, Japan, Canada, and Belgium. The forward link is that mass production, scientific management, and organized labor became defining features of twentieth-century industrial capitalism.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

**World GDP:** The Maddison 2007 historical table gives world GDP in 1913 as approximately 2,733,365 million 1990 international dollars; the Maddison 2010 horizontal workbook gives 2,733,190 million in the same units. The Maddison Project Database 2023 and Our World in Data provide more recent Maddison-style data in 2011 international dollars, adjusted for inflation and living-cost differences, but the ranking table in section 4 relies on the older 1990 international-dollar series because it provides directly comparable historical 1913 country and region totals. The 2023 release has 68 country records for 1913, of which only 39 form a matched panel with 1912, so summing its records does not measure world growth, and the 2010 workbook has no matched 1912 world row. **World GDP growth rate: Data not available.**

**Global trade volume:** NBER work on world trade estimates the ratio of world trade to output at roughly 21 percent in 1913, with a related table reporting trade near 39,762 million current 1913 US dollars and world GDP near 178,280 million current 1913 US dollars. The exact value differs by definition, especially whether trade is measured as exports alone or exports plus imports, but the direction is clear: 1913 was one of the high points of prewar trade globalization. In the Federico-Tena World Trade Historical Database, the world real merchandise-export index rose from 95.71 in 1912 to 100 in 1913 (1913 = 100), a 4.48% volume increase, and nominal world exports at current borders rose from $17,805 million to $18,736 million (+5.23%) across 130 trading polities against 129. These are merchandise exports only; the nominal change is not a volume measure.

**Reserve currency dynamics:** Sterling and the London money market were central to world finance. Gold convertibility and gold points created relatively stable exchange rates among gold-standard countries. The US dollar was important because the United States was the largest economy, but the US did not yet have an operating central bank and New York had not displaced London as the leading international money market. **Global inflation trends:** A single global CPI series for 1913 does not exist. Under the classical gold standard, long-run price-level expectations were constrained by gold convertibility, but national price levels still moved with harvests, commodity prices, credit cycles, and gold flows. Among the ranked economies covered by the Jorda-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database, retrospective consumer prices ranged from -4.76% in Belgium and -1.30% in Australia to +3.07% in Canada and +3.95% in the Netherlands, with the United States at +1.71% and the United Kingdom at -0.40%. US CPI data begin in official BLS back-estimates for 1913, but comparable CPI data for many countries are absent or inconsistent. **Global inflation: Data not available as a single comparable figure.**

**Key commodity prices:** Gold was fixed at the US official gold price of $20.67 per troy ounce under the pre-1933 US gold standard, with sterling gold parity implying a stable dollar-sterling relationship. **World population:** About 1,795.2 million in 1913 against 1,777.4 million in 1912 (+1.01%, or 17.9 million) in the January 2026 Federico-Tena reconstruction on 1991 borders. This is an author-published reconstruction, not a census.

**International capital flows:** Capital generally flowed from mature European saving centers, especially Britain and France, and also Germany and the Netherlands, toward railways, utilities, mines, government debt, ports, and land development in the Americas, Russia, colonies, and semi-peripheral economies. Argentina, Canada, Russia, and parts of Asia were important recipients. ### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

**United States:** No operating central bank existed during 1913. Monetary conditions depended on national banks, seasonal currency demand, gold flows, private clearinghouse arrangements, and New York money-market liquidity. The Federal Reserve Act was signed on December 23, 1913, but the Reserve Banks opened on November 16, 1914, so there was no Fed policy rate in 1913. The Macrohistory Database money-market short-term rate fell from 3.52% to 3.22%. **Policy rate: Data not available because no operating central bank policy rate existed.**

**United Kingdom:** The Bank of England Bank Rate series shows a relatively tight environment: 5.00 percent in 1913 Q1, 4.50 percent in Q2 and Q3, and 5.00 percent in Q4. The Bank of England millennium dataset dates the moves: 5% carried from 1912, 4.5% from 17 April and 5% from 1 October, an annual average of 4.77%. This is consistent with a gold-standard system in which the Bank Rate was used to defend reserves and influence short-term capital flows. London remained the center of global discounting, trade finance, and sterling bills.

**Germany:** Germany remained on the gold standard and the Reichsbank managed discount policy. An official 1913 Reichsbank discount rate is Data not available in a verified form; the Macrohistory Database market short-term rate rose from 4.22% to 4.98%.

**France:** The Banque de France operated under the gold standard, with a reputation for comparatively stable discount policy and large gold reserves. The official 1913 discount rate is Data not available in a verified form; the Macrohistory Database short-term rate rose from 3.12% to 3.82%.

**Japan:** The Bank of Japan historical statistics provide official discount-rate series covering 1882-1941, and Japan had been on gold since the late 1890s. The Bank of Japan commercial-bill discount rate stood at 6.57% throughout 1913, unchanged from its last change on 14 November 1912 until 6 July 1914. The Macrohistory Database Japan discount rate of 8.58% differs from this named Bank of Japan facility and is not reconciled with it.

**British India:** The Bank of Bengal's rate for demand loans secured on government paper stood at 7% in December 1913, as in December 1912; monthly entries during the year ranged from 3% to 8%. This is an institution-specific lending rate, not a national policy rate.

**Exchange-rate regime context:** Bretton Woods did not exist. The IMF did not exist. Floating exchange-rate markets in the modern sense were not the dominant system. Major participating currencies were tied to gold, while some economies operated silver, paper, managed, or gold-exchange arrangements. Exchange controls were not a normal peacetime feature for the major gold-standard powers, but colonies and dependent territories often had administrative currency arrangements.

**Stock and bond markets:** London, New York, Paris, Berlin, Amsterdam, Brussels, Vienna, Tokyo, and other exchanges were active. Reconstructed annual equity price indices fell in the United States (-14.29%), Italy (-9.00%), Belgium (-8.05%), the United Kingdom (-7.41%) and Japan (-7.18%), and rose in Germany (+1.01%) and Australia (+3.80%) (section 6A). Bond markets were deep in Britain, France, Germany, the United States, and the Netherlands; long-term government yields rose in most countries with data, for example from 3.28% to 3.39% in the United Kingdom and from 3.91% to 4.09% in Germany (section 6B). ### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

1913 was a high-trade year but not a free-trade utopia. Tariffs remained significant in the United States, Germany, parts of Latin America, and other economies. The United States enacted the Underwood-Simmons tariff, lowering average tariff rates and introducing a federal income tax. The Revenue Act of October 3 set a 1% normal income tax with additional rates up to 6%, a $3,000 exemption ($4,000 for married couples), and a first taxable period from March 1 to December 31, 1913, with returns due on March 1, 1914 and payment by June 30, 1914; 1913 tax liabilities were therefore not 1913 receipts. This represented a domestic fiscal and trade-policy shift in the world's largest economy.

There was no Cold War, no EEC, no ASEAN, and no GATT/WTO system. Trade rules were embedded in bilateral treaties, imperial preferences, tariffs, gold-standard convertibility, shipping networks, colonial administration, and informal commercial conventions. Geopolitically, the Balkan Wars and rising great-power rivalry increased background risk, but the full economic implications were not yet priced into global markets in a modern risk-management sense. France's law of August 7, 1913 extended active military service to three years.

Trade tensions existed around tariffs, migration restrictions, imperial preferences, colonial markets, and access to raw materials. The most important bilateral economic networks were not necessarily the same as later twentieth-century alliances. Britain's empire and sterling zone, French overseas lending, German industrial trade, US domestic scale and tariff policy, Russian grain exports, Indian and Chinese commodity flows, and Japanese East Asian trade all mattered.

International finance and diplomacy intersected in two cases. The Chinese Reorganization Loan was reported signed on April 27: 25 million pounds face value, a 5% coupon and a 47-year term, with a minimum issue price of 90 to the public and 84 to the Chinese government; an immediate advance of 500,000 pounds was still unpaid in early May, so the face value is not a measure of capital actually transferred. On April 22 the US State Department communicated the Attorney General's instruction to withdraw the antitrust suit against the Brazilian coffee valorization stock, without assurance against future action.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Coal was the dominant industrial energy source. Britain, Germany, the United States, France, Belgium, Russia, and Japan were major users, and coal output underpinned steel, railways, shipping, electricity, and heavy industry. In the Belgian official comparative tables, coal output rose 8.71% in Germany to 190.1 million metric tonnes and 6.64% in the United States (including lignite) to 517.1 million, but fell 0.57% in Belgium; lignite coverage differs between the national series, so the levels are not like-for-like. The Board of Trade's provisional world pig-iron estimate rose from 72 to 77 million long tons (+6.94%). Oil was growing, especially in the United States, Russia, the Dutch East Indies, and parts of the Middle East, but oil had not yet become the central global energy commodity it became later in the twentieth century. Gold was both a commodity and a monetary anchor. Its monetary role was more important than day-to-day commodity speculation in shaping global macro conditions. The fixed official US gold price and gold convertibility in major economies stabilized nominal exchange rates among participating currencies but constrained domestic monetary policy.

Agricultural commodities were central. Grain, meat, wool, cotton, sugar, tea, rubber, coffee, and jute linked farms, plantations, colonial economies, steamships, railways, warehouses, and metropolitan consumers. Canada's wheat-price weakness in 1913 shows how frontier economies were exposed to international commodity cycles. Rubber from Southeast Asia, coffee from Brazil, beef and grain from Argentina, cotton from the United States and India, and rice from Asia were important trade channels. ### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Global labor markets in 1913 were shaped by agriculture, migration, industrialization, and empire. Agriculture still absorbed most employment in China, India, Russia, Indonesia, Mexico, Brazil, and much of Southern and Eastern Europe. Industrial labor was concentrated in the United States, Britain, Germany, France, Belgium, northern Italy, Japan, and selected ports and mining zones.

Migration was economically significant. Europeans migrated to the United States, Canada, Argentina, Brazil, Australia, and other settler economies. Canada recorded 418,909 immigrant arrivals in calendar 1913 (156,984 from the United Kingdom, 115,751 from the United States and 146,174 from other countries); its separately reported fiscal year ended March 31, 1913 gives 402,432. Asian migration was more constrained by law and empire but remained important in Southeast Asia and parts of the Pacific. Remittances, labor supply, land settlement, wage competition, and urban growth all depended on migration.

Unemployment data are not comparable across countries. Many countries had no modern labor-force surveys, and unemployment in agricultural economies often appeared as underemployment, seasonal labor slack, or distress migration rather than a measured rate. Historical national estimates in the Macrohistory Database give 4.3% for the United States, 2.9% for Germany, 2.1% for the United Kingdom, 4.7% for France and 5.0% for Australia, but definitions differ. Wage growth versus productivity cannot be stated as a consistent global figure. Social insurance was widening: the National Health Insurance Fund for England received 15,055,127 pounds in contributions in the 52 weeks to January 11, 1914, and Sweden's pension-insurance law of June 30, 1913 took effect on January 1, 1914. **Global unemployment rate: Data not available. **Economic narrative:**

The United States was the largest economy in 1913 and the leading example of scale-driven industrial capitalism. Railways, steel, oil, machinery, farm equipment, meatpacking, electricity, and urban consumer goods formed the productive base. The country combined huge natural resources, a large internal market, high immigration, and rapid technological change. Yet the financial system still had structural weaknesses: no central bank, seasonal currency stress, and reliance on private coordination in crises.

The two major policy events were the Federal Reserve Act and the Underwood-Simmons tariff. The Federal Reserve Act was not a current-year monetary-policy lever because the system did not open until November 1914, but it was a decisive institutional response to repeated banking panics. The tariff reform lowered average tariff rates and introduced a federal income tax, shifting the revenue base away from tariffs; the first taxable period ran from March 1 to December 31, 1913.

Growth slowed from 4.07% to 3.33% in the 2023 release, and asset prices weakened: the reconstructed equity price index fell 14.29% and the total return was -9.17%. The St. Louis and San Francisco Railroad went into receivership on May 27, a specific case of rail-finance distress rather than a measure of national bankruptcies.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Ford introduced the moving assembly line at Highland Park in 1913, a milestone in mass production; the five-dollar day followed in January 1914. US Steel, Standard Oil successor companies, railroads, meatpackers, farm-machinery firms, and electrical manufacturers were central corporate actors. Consumer credit was still early by later standards. ### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Labor markets were segmented by skill, gender, migration status, race, colony, and region. Industrial economies had growing factory workforces, skilled trades, unions, and urban wage labor. Agrarian economies had seasonal underemployment, tenancy, household labor, and informal credit obligations that do not fit modern unemployment measures.

Education and human capital varied sharply. Germany, Britain, the United States, France, Sweden, Belgium, and Japan had strong or improving primary education and technical systems. China, India, Indonesia, Brazil, Mexico, and Russia had lower literacy and more uneven access, although urban and elite education systems were expanding.

Labor movements were visible in Britain, the United States, Germany, France, Italy, Belgium, Canada, and Australia. Unions mattered most in mining, railways, docks, textiles, and heavy industry. Ford's high turnover before the 1914 Five Dollar Day shows that even advanced firms faced labor-discipline and retention problems. US Steel raised wages on February 1 for about 75% of its employees, by roughly 12.5% for those earning under $2 a day, with an overall payroll effect of about 6%, and the Bell System's benefit plan paid more than $1 million in 16,054 cases. Social insurance spread: England's National Health Insurance Fund paid sickness, maternity and medical benefits, and Sweden legislated pension insurance. Nominal wage indices in the Macrohistory Database rose 6.07% in the Netherlands, 4.47% in Australia, 3.82% in the United States, 3.03% in Germany and 0.18% in the United Kingdom, but comparable wage growth by country and sector is otherwise Data not available. **Global wage growth: Data not available.**

### 6C. Currencies

The key currency fact was gold-standard stability among major participating currencies. The dollar, pound, mark, franc, yen, and several other currencies were tied to gold, directly or through gold-exchange arrangements. The pound sterling was the principal international commercial and financial currency.

Major pair movements were limited among gold-standard currencies because gold points and convertibility constrained exchange-rate fluctuations: annual Macrohistory rates were 0.2054 pounds, 4.2107 marks, 5.1840 French francs, 5.25 Belgian francs, 2.4925 guilders and 3.7444 kronor per dollar, each within 0.8% of the 1912 rate, and the Bank of Japan's December quotation of USD 49.44 per 100 yen implies 2.0227 yen per dollar. The peseta moved to 5.54 per dollar and the Mexican peso moved from 2.08 to 2.13 per dollar. British India's Council bills realized 16.058d per rupee in FY 1912-13. Countries outside the core gold system, or with silver, paper, colonial, or managed arrangements, had less straightforward exchange rates. **Full 1913 currency-pair table: Data not available.**

Capital controls were not the defining feature of the core prewar system. The system relied more on convertibility, discount-rate policy, gold flows, and market discipline. Colonial monetary arrangements were administratively controlled in ways that are not comparable to modern capital controls.

### 6D. Alternative assets

Gold was the central alternative asset and monetary anchor. The official US price was $20.67 per troy ounce. Gold was not merely a portfolio hedge; it was the standard of settlement and the reserve asset behind monetary systems. Silver eased 1.71% in the Federico-Tena series.

Real estate was important in London, New York, Paris, Berlin, Vienna, Buenos Aires, Toronto, Montreal, Sydney, Melbourne, Shanghai, Bombay, Calcutta, and other cities, but comparable city-level price indices for 1913 are limited. Quality-adjusted newspaper asking-price indices compiled by the Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia fell 1.13% in New York City and 0.82% in Boston, while asking rents fell 7.59% in New York and rose 0.55% in Boston; these are asking prices sampled mostly in May, not completed sales. Macrohistory nominal house-price indices rose 7.00% in Australia, 2.38% in Belgium, 1.34% in the United States, 1.00% in Sweden and 0.94% in France, and fell 5.83% in the United Kingdom and 0.76% in the Netherlands. **Comparable major-city real estate prices: Data not available.** Land values in frontier economies such as Canada, Argentina, Australia, and the US West were closely tied to railways, crop prices, migration, and credit.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The most important active risk in 1913 was geopolitical, but its full economic impact was not yet visible to contemporaries. The Balkan Wars had shown instability in southeastern Europe, while naval competition, alliance systems, and great-power rivalry increased systemic risk; France's three-year service law of August 7 was one sign of mobilization pressure. Hindsight makes the danger clear, but markets and policymakers did not fully price the possibility of a prolonged world war.

Financial vulnerabilities were also present. The gold standard created credibility, but it required adjustment through gold flows, interest rates, credit conditions, wages, and prices. Countries with external deficits or reliance on short-term capital were vulnerable to sudden tightening; Canada's current-account deficit was 15.60% of GDP. The United States still had no operating central bank, although reform had been legislated. Equity prices fell in most major markets, and the receivership of the St. Louis and San Francisco Railroad showed rail-finance distress. Canada and Argentina were exposed to commodity prices and foreign capital. Russia depended partly on foreign finance and grain exports. Colonial economies depended on metropolitan trade routes and finance. China's Reorganization Loan added a large foreign borrowing commitment to a weak fiscal base.

A major policy mistake visible in hindsight was overconfidence in the automatic stability of the gold-standard world. The system worked best under peace, credible convertibility, and functioning international markets. It was not designed for mass mobilization, capital-market closures, shipping disruption, and fiscal expansion on the scale that arrived in 1914.

Tail risks that did not fully materialize in 1913 included a global banking panic, a collapse of gold convertibility, or a synchronized sovereign-debt crisis. Tail risks that materialized soon after included war, suspension of normal financial markets, shipping disruption, inflationary war finance, and the breakdown of the first-globalization trade order.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1913 vs. 1912)

The major structural change from 1912 to 1913 was US institutional reform. The Federal Reserve Act created the legal basis for a central banking system, though not yet an operating one. The Underwood-Simmons tariff lowered tariff rates and introduced a federal income tax. These two reforms changed the architecture of US finance and fiscal policy more than they changed immediate 1913 output.

Global trade remained near its prewar high, with world merchandise-export volume up 4.48%, but the environment became more fragile. Geopolitical risk increased after the Balkan Wars, and great-power tensions were more visible. The gold standard still functioned, but the conditions for its breakdown were building outside the purely economic domain.

Credit conditions in parts of the North Atlantic economy were tighter than the easy expansion phases of the previous decade. Britain's Bank Rate was 5.00 percent at the start and end of 1913, with an interval at 4.5% between April and October, and market short-term rates rose in Germany, France, the Netherlands and Sweden. Canada's wheat boom weakened as international wheat prices fell, shifting a previously expansionary frontier economy toward recessionary pressure: its growth slowed from 6.59% to 4.93%. The US economy also experienced cyclical weakness, with growth slowing from 4.07% to 3.33% and equity prices falling 14.29%. France's growth swung from 8.26% to -0.57% and Argentina's from 8.21% to 0.99%, while Spain, Italy and the United Kingdom accelerated. Consumer-price inflation fell in most countries with data.

Industrial trends accelerated in microeconomic form. Ford's moving assembly line was not a macro event by itself, but it marked a turning point in production organization. Germany's chemical and electrical industries, Japan's textile and heavy-industrial expansion, and US mass-production systems showed that productivity growth was increasingly tied to firm organization, technical knowledge, and scale.

Shifts in economic power were incremental, not sudden. The United States was already the largest total-GDP economy. Germany remained the leading European industrial challenger. Britain remained the financial center. China and India remained enormous by population but low-income. The dramatic reordering would come after 1914, not during 1913 itself.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

The highest-confidence countries are the United States, United Kingdom, Germany, France, Italy, Belgium, Netherlands, Sweden, and to a somewhat lesser extent Japan, Canada, Australia, and Argentina. These economies had better statistical systems, more developed historical national-account reconstructions, and more consistent monetary and financial records. Dated statutes, official production returns, colonial administrative returns and company reports are also relatively high confidence for what they measure, though not as national aggregates.

Medium-confidence estimates include China, British India, Mexico, Spain, and Dutch East Indies/Indonesia. These economies have historical estimates, but national accounts are reconstructed from partial evidence, and boundaries, colonial administration, silver or mixed monetary systems, and informal production complicate measurement.

The Russian Empire/former-USSR estimate is medium-low confidence. Maddison-style estimates exist, but boundaries, data quality, agricultural statistics, services, and later political discontinuities create major uncertainty. For Russia and other pre-1990 non-market or later Soviet-bloc territories, a rough uncertainty band of plus or minus 20-30 percent is appropriate. The Maddison Project Database 2023 former-USSR record has no 1912 value, and its 1913-1928 growth path is based on Markevich and Harrison's national-income estimates, which distinguish imperial and interwar territories.

Austria-Hungary is medium-low confidence because the imperial economy does not map cleanly onto later Maddison country units. This estimate is a specialist imperial historical estimate rather than a simple modern-country total; the 2023 release reconstructs Austria and Hungary only on their smaller later territories, so their growth rates are not the empire's. Brazil is low-medium confidence because the top-20 placement uses a derived estimate rather than a direct Maddison country total.

**Vintage and unit conflicts:** The levels in section 4 are Maddison 1990-dollar estimates, while the growth rates are Maddison Project Database 2023 rates in 2011 dollars, and the 1912 assessment shows 2011-dollar levels from the 2020 release; the levels are not directly comparable across the two years. In 2011 dollars the 2023 release ranks Germany and the United Kingdom slightly above the former USSR, and Mexico, Brazil and Sweden fall to 21st-24th behind modern-territory Poland, Czechoslovakia, Austria and Switzerland. The 2023 release's India record covers undivided India, dated to 1 October fiscal-year midpoints; its Indonesia record includes Timor, so its growth rate is shown only in Notes. World GDP is 2,733,365 million 1990 dollars in the Maddison 2007 table and 2,733,190 million in the 2010 workbook; both are the same lineage. Spain's 1912 growth is -3.05% in the 2020 release and -3.08% in the 2023 release, so the YoY change uses the latter.

**Source conflicts:** For the United Kingdom, the Bank of England millennium dataset and the Macrohistory Database give different consumer-price inflation (+0.64% against -0.40%), unemployment (3.63% against 2.1%), debt (28.27% against 27.90% of GDP) and current-account (9.37% against 9.77% of GDP) values; the Macrohistory values are shown and the sources are not blended. Japan's Bank of Japan commercial-bill rate (6.57%) differs from the Macrohistory Japan discount rate (8.58%); the two are not reconciled. Belgium's Macrohistory short-term series changes instrument in 1913. The German house-price series changes geography in 1913. The Netherlands appears among the capital-exporting saving centers in section 3A, while the Macrohistory Database shows a 9.68% current-account deficit; its numerator covers merchandise, services and primary income without separately established transfers, and the two are not reconciled. Canada's fiscal-year 1913 immigration total is 402,432 in the 1913 Year Book but 402,232 in the 1912 edition, whose components do not add up; calendar and fiscal totals are not mixed. The Belgian tables and the Board of Trade returns use different units (metric tonnes against tons of 2,240 lb) and vintages, and the Belgian German pig-iron figure for 1912 is suspect, so only the Board of Trade Germany-including-Luxembourg series is used. The Belgian tables revise Sweden's 1912 coal output from 366 to 360 thousand tonnes. The Dutch colonial report prints 45% growth for interlocal telephone calls although its own counts give 42.99%, and its sugar ton conversions change between years, so only the weighted pikol series is used. The China loan's signing date is April 27 in a contemporaneous telegram and May 4 in a later dispatch.

**What does not exist in modern form:** The most important data gaps are world real GDP growth, a global CPI, unemployment outside the countries with historical estimates, budget balance/GDP, official discount rates for most European central banks, exchange-level stock indices, credit spreads, corporate revenue and market capitalization rankings, M&A, IPOs, bankruptcy tables, global wage growth, global R&D spending, world oil output and comparable real-estate prices. Budget balances relative to GDP are not shown for any economy because fiscal years and government coverage are not reconciled with calendar GDP. These data either did not exist in modern form or were not collected consistently. Values derived from the Jorda-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database and its rate-of-return extension are used under the CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 license; Federico-Tena commodity-price data are used under CC BY 4.0.

The GDP rankings are strongest at the top and weakest around ranks 11-20. Boundary choices can change the list. If the Ottoman Empire were estimated as a single imperial economy using a reliable total, it could plausibly enter the lower top 20, but a reliable 1913 total is Data not available. Switzerland, separate Austria, South Africa, Korea, Cuba, and other economies may be close to the lower ranks depending on definitions. Therefore, the bottom of the table should be treated as an informed Maddison-based reconstruction, not a precise official ranking.

A final limitation is unit consistency. Maddison 2007 tables use 1990 international dollars, while Maddison Project Database 2023 and Our World in Data often present 2011 international dollars. The top-20 table uses 1990 international dollars because the historical country and region values were available in that unit. Figures in 2011 international dollars are not directly interchangeable with 1990 international-dollar values.
