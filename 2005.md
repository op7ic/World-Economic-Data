# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 2005

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

2005 was a late-cycle expansion year, a description that is a retrospective interpretation rather than a contemporaneous prediction that a crisis was imminent. The world economy remained strong after the unusually rapid rebound of 2004, but the mix changed. Growth was still led by the United States, China, India, commodity exporters, and recovering Japan, while the euro area remained comparatively weak. World GDP in current U.S. dollars was about $47.9 trillion in World Bank data. IMF and World Bank growth measures differ because of weighting methods, but both point to a broad expansion rather than recession: the IMF World Economic Outlook database of October 2026 puts world real growth at 4.713 percent with PPP weights and 3.575 percent with market-exchange-rate weights. The big macro question of the year was not whether the world economy was growing, but whether it was growing on a stable foundation.

Three forces defined the year. First, oil and metals prices rose sharply, benefiting Russia, Brazil, Canada, Australia, Saudi Arabia, and other resource exporters while raising inflation pressure in oil-importing economies; the EIA annual Brent spot average rose 42.63 percent to US$54.57 per barrel. Second, the U.S. Federal Reserve continued its tightening cycle, taking the federal funds target to 4.25 percent by December, while Japan remained under quantitative easing and the euro area only began to lift rates at year-end. Third, global current-account imbalances widened: the United States absorbed savings from Asia and oil exporters, while China, Japan, Germany, Russia, Switzerland, and oil exporters accumulated surpluses. In the BIS's June 2006 tables the U.S. deficit widened from US$668 billion to US$805 billion and the oil exporters' surplus rose from US$239 billion to US$417 billion.

The most important structural event was China's July 2005 renminbi reform. The immediate revaluation was small, about 2.1 percent against the U.S. dollar, but the move away from the decade-old tight dollar peg signaled a new phase in Asian exchange-rate management. It did not close global imbalances, but it changed the policy debate. 2005 was also the first year of the European Union Emissions Trading System, a major microeconomic policy innovation that created a tradable carbon allowance market and altered cost structures in power and energy-intensive industry.

With hindsight, 2005 also sits close to the peak of the pre-crisis credit and housing cycle. Household leverage, structured credit, low long-term yields, and U.S. housing finance looked manageable to many observers at the time, although housing and mortgage vulnerabilities were already debated: the U.S. federal financial regulators' proposed guidance of 20 December explicitly addressed repayment and underwriting risks in nontraditional mortgages. A later systemic collapse was not a contemporaneously established outcome. In retrospect, these were major vulnerabilities. The year therefore belongs to two histories at once: the high-growth globalization cycle of the mid-2000s and the build-up to the 2007-2008 financial crisis.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 2005

**1. Expansion with late-cycle inflation pressure.** Growth stayed broad, but the composition became less comfortable. Oil, metals, and construction costs rose, putting pressure on real incomes and margins in fuel-importing economies. Advanced economy core inflation was still contained, which allowed central banks outside the United States and some commodity-linked economies to remain cautious. Several emerging economies tightened hard: Bank Indonesia's new BI rate rose from 8.50 percent at its July launch to 12.75 percent in December, and the Bank of Thailand raised its policy rate six times to 4.00 percent.

**2. Global imbalances became a central policy problem.** The United States ran a very large external deficit, funded by capital inflows from surplus economies. China, Japan, Germany, Switzerland, Russia, and oil exporters were the major surplus poles. At the time, policy debates focused on exchange-rate flexibility, U.S. fiscal saving, Asian reserve accumulation, and the recycling of oil revenues. The forward link is direct: these imbalances shaped the international policy debate through 2006 and interacted with the credit boom before the financial crisis. A saving-investment identity does not by itself show that a particular surplus country financed a particular mortgage; the distribution, intermediation and maturity of gross claims also mattered.

**3. The commodity super-cycle accelerated.** Copper, iron ore, zinc, energy, and gold rose on strong Asian demand, low inventories, and supply constraints. The strongest beneficiaries among the top 20 economies were Russia, Brazil, Australia, Canada, and to a lesser degree Mexico. The forward linkage was the investment boom in mining, oilfield services, shipping, and resource-linked infrastructure that continued into 2007-2008.

**4. China moved from fixed peg to managed flexibility.** China's July 2005 currency reform was modest in magnitude but large in policy meaning. It changed expectations for Asian currencies and became part of a broader argument over trade balances, reserve accumulation, and export-led growth. China also remained an enormous source of demand for metals, energy, shipping, machinery, and electronics supply chains.

**5. Japan's recovery became more credible.** Japan remained under deflationary pressure and the Bank of Japan kept quantitative easing in place, but corporate profits, bank balance-sheet repair, equity prices, and investment all improved. With hindsight, 2005 was the last full year before the Bank of Japan exited quantitative easing in 2006.

**6. Housing, credit, and leverage supported demand while attracting explicit warnings.** In the United States, Spain, the United Kingdom, Australia, and parts of Europe, property markets and household credit were major demand supports. At the time, low long-term rates and financial innovation were widely treated as signs of market depth, but contemporary supervisory concern, including the December U.S. interagency proposal on nontraditional mortgages, means the risks were not wholly invisible. Hindsight shows that in the United States, the same conditions helped build the mortgage-credit vulnerabilities that surfaced in 2007.

**7. The microeconomics of globalization deepened.** Cross-border M&A, global supply chains, outsourcing, container trade, emerging-market consumer growth, and Web 2.0 platforms all advanced. P&G-Gillette, Sprint-Nextel, eBay-Skype, Lenovo's purchase of IBM's personal computing division, Baidu's U.S. listing, the growth of Google services, and the founding of YouTube illustrate the corporate and technology layer underneath the macro story.

The current WDI release gives world nominal GDP of US$47.938 trillion. The IMF World Economic Outlook database of October 2026 gives world nominal GDP of US$48,134.2 billion (US$44,427.5 billion in 2004) and current-PPP GDP of 67,558.1 billion international dollars. The BIS's June 2006 annual report gave 4.3 percent growth for its selected-country total, weighted with 2000 GDP at PPP; its inflation footnote mixes harmonised annual prices for the euro area and United Kingdom with end-year prices for Latin America, so it is not a uniform world CPI series.

The BIS's contemporary current-account table put the U.S. deficit at US$805 billion (6.4 percent of GDP), China's surplus at US$161 billion (7.2 percent of GDP) and the oil exporters' surplus at US$417 billion, against US$239 billion in 2004. These source-vintage values differ from later WDI and WEO figures. Current accounts cover goods, services, income and transfers; they are not customs goods balances, reserve changes or gross capital inflows, and reserve accumulation can include valuation effects.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The United States tightened steadily. The Federal Reserve raised the federal funds target to 4.25 percent by December 2005, continuing the "measured pace" cycle that started in 2004; the final increase of the year was decided on 13 December. Long-term yields rose much less than short rates, producing the famous yield-curve "conundrum." Credit stayed easy enough to support corporate borrowing, private equity, housing finance, and structured credit growth.

The euro area remained accommodative for most of the year. The ECB kept the main refinancing rate at 2.0 percent until December, then raised it to 2.25 percent: the decision was announced on 1 December, and the 2.25 percent minimum bid rate applied from the operation settled on 6 December, when the deposit facility rate of 1.25 percent and marginal lending rate of 3.25 percent also took effect. The Bank of England moved in the opposite direction, cutting Bank Rate from 4.75 percent to 4.50 percent on 4 August after softer consumption and housing signals. The Bank of Japan maintained quantitative easing and near-zero short-term rates, targeting current-account balances at the central bank of JPY 30-35 trillion; in May it added a proviso allowing balances to fall below the target when liquidity demand was exceptionally weak, which was not an exit from quantitative easing.

Other central banks also moved. The Reserve Bank of Australia's Board decided on 1 March to raise the cash rate target to 5.50 percent, implemented on 2 March. The Bank of Canada raised its overnight target to 3.25 percent on 6 December. The Bank of Korea raised its overnight call-rate target from 3.50 to 3.75 percent on 8 December. The Reserve Bank of India raised the repo rate to 6.25 percent and the reverse repo rate to 5.25 percent effective 26 October. The Bank of Russia cut its refinancing rate from 13 to 12 percent, announced on 23 December and effective 26 December. Bank Indonesia introduced the BI rate framework in July at 8.50 percent and raised it to 12.75 percent by 6 December, while the Bank of Thailand's 14-day repurchase rate rose by 2.00 percentage points to 4.00 percent in six steps, the last on 14 December.

Equity markets outside the United States were strong. Japan's Nikkei 225 rose about 40 percent, Germany's DAX about 27 percent, and the FTSE 100 about 17 percent. The U.S. market was much more subdued, with the broad market posting only a small positive return. Bond markets were shaped by low term premia, foreign official demand, pension demand, and credibility of inflation-targeting central banks.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The WTO Doha Round remained unresolved. Trade growth stayed strong, but the multilateral system showed signs of strain over agriculture, industrial tariffs, and services. China's WTO accession effects continued to reshape manufacturing supply chains, especially in electronics, textiles, machinery, and consumer goods. 2005 was also the first full year after the end of the WTO Agreement on Textiles and Clothing quota system on 1 January, intensifying pressure on textile producers in some developing and advanced economies. Saudi Arabia's WTO accession was accepted on 11 November and took effect with membership on 11 December 2005; it changed the trade-policy framework, while sector commitments and transition periods phased in under individual schedules.

Geopolitics affected oil, defense budgets, and risk premia. The Iraq war continued, tensions around Iran affected oil-risk pricing, and Hurricane Katrina exposed the vulnerability of U.S. Gulf energy infrastructure. Sanctions and embargoes were not the dominant global growth driver, but energy geopolitics was a major price driver.

Regional integration advanced unevenly. The European Union absorbed the 2004 enlargement economically, while its constitutional treaty suffered political rejection in France and the Netherlands. ASEAN and China continued deepening trade linkages. In the Americas, NAFTA supply chains remained mature, while broader hemispheric trade liberalization was politically constrained.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil was the defining commodity. WTI and Brent moved into price ranges that were high by early-2000s standards, with mid-year and late-summer spikes around refinery constraints, strong demand, and Hurricane Katrina. OPEC operated in a context of reduced spare capacity and high uncertainty about non-OPEC supply growth.

Metals were the second major story. Chinese fixed-asset investment and global manufacturing demand pushed copper, zinc, iron ore, nickel, and aluminum markets higher. Mining exporters benefited through income, tax receipts, investment, and currency appreciation pressure. Agricultural staples were less explosive than energy and metals, though sugar and some soft commodities strengthened. The broad food crisis of 2007-2008 had not yet arrived.

The EIA's U.S. crude oil first-purchase price, a domestic acquisition-stage measure rather than a spot benchmark, averaged US$50.28 per barrel against US$36.77 in 2004 (+36.742 percent). U.S. crude production including lease condensate fell from 1,991,394 thousand barrels to 1,892,095 thousand barrels (-4.986 percent), a calendar annual flow that excludes other petroleum liquids. The 2005 Gulf hurricanes hit refining as well as production. The EIA's June 2006 assessment found up to 4.9 million barrels per day of refinery capacity shut during 22-25 September 2005, approximately 29 percent of U.S. capacity; this was idle downstream capacity, not crude lost each day through the year. Its cumulative Gulf of Mexico shut-in production of more than 162 million barrels of crude oil and 784 billion cubic feet of natural gas runs through 1 June 2006 and cannot be assigned entirely to calendar 2005. Upstream production, refinery throughput, pipeline damage and fuel availability were distinct bottlenecks.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor conditions improved in the United States, Japan, Germany, and several emerging economies, but unemployment definitions were not comparable across countries. Europe's unemployment remained high in Germany, France, Spain, Belgium, and Italy by U.S. or Japanese standards. China's official urban registered unemployment rate understated migrant and informal labor-market dynamics. India's labor-market data were not directly comparable with OECD unemployment statistics.

Demographics were already economically important. Japan's working-age population decline was an explicit policy concern. Europe faced aging and pension sustainability problems. China still benefited from a large working-age population, but rural-to-urban migration and household registration rules shaped labor supply. India's demographic dividend was visible in services, construction, and domestic demand, but job quality and informality remained major constraints.

**Economic narrative:**

The U.S. economy expanded solidly, but the year was increasingly shaped by the tension between Fed tightening and resilient credit creation. Household consumption, housing wealth, mortgage credit, and business spending supported activity. Corporate profits were strong, and the labor market continued to heal from the early-2000s downturn.

The federal financial regulators' proposed guidance on nontraditional mortgages, issued on 20 December, identified interest-only repayment, payment-option adjustable loans, negative amortization, reduced documentation and simultaneous second liens as risk features. Lower introductory payments could postpone rather than eliminate debt service, and principal could rise; qualification at the fully indexed rate was specifically debated. This documents contemporary supervisory concern rather than knowledge of the later losses, and the proposal was not final guidance in 2005.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Housing finance, retail, energy, health care, and financial services were major demand centers. Hurricane Katrina disrupted Gulf energy and logistics, and up to 4.9 million barrels per day of refinery capacity was shut in late September. P&G announced and completed the Gillette acquisition, and U.S. technology platforms continued to scale. Mortgage underwriting deterioration was already the subject of explicit supervisory concern in December, although later loss magnitudes were not known. U.S. crude production fell 4.986 percent to 1,892,095 thousand barrels.

### 5B. Corporate landscape

The largest companies by revenue were still dominated by retail, oil, autos, and industrial conglomerates. Fortune's 2005 Global 500 ranking placed Walmart first, followed by BP, Exxon Mobil, Shell, General Motors, DaimlerChrysler, Toyota, Ford, General Electric, and Total. This is a publication-year list that generally reflects the preceding fiscal year's revenues rather than calendar-2005 revenue. The prominence of the oil companies is consistent with higher crude prices lifting revenue even where output growth was modest; the list's revenue figures are not given.

Major M&A included Procter & Gamble's acquisition of Gillette, valued around $56.9 billion, one of the largest consumer-products deals of the period. Sprint and Nextel combined in telecommunications. eBay announced its purchase of Skype in September at a headline value of about $2.6 billion and completed it on 14 October. eBay's SEC-filed closing release valued the upfront consideration at approximately EUR 2.1 billion, or US$2.5 billion at the 12 October rate of 1.20 dollars per euro, comprising about US$1.3 billion in cash and 32.8 million eBay shares, plus a contingent earn-out of up to EUR 1.2 billion (US$1.4 billion) expected to be paid in 2008-2009; the contingent maximum was not cash paid in 2005. With hindsight the deal became a cautionary example of strategic overreach, but it also signaled the perceived value of internet communications networks. Banking, telecoms, lodging, and consumer goods all saw deal activity supported by low financing costs.

Cross-border technology deals and listings also marked the year. Lenovo completed its acquisition of IBM's personal computing division on 1 May 2005; closing consideration comprised US$650 million in cash and US$600 million in Lenovo stock, plus approximately US$500 million of assumed net balance-sheet liabilities, and IBM initially held 18.9 percent of Lenovo. ### 5C. Innovation & technology

2005 was a foundational year for platform internet. YouTube was founded, Google Maps launched, broadband adoption expanded, and online advertising became a more mature business model. These innovations were not yet large enough to dominate macro aggregates, but they changed the microeconomics of media, advertising, search, mapping, and user-generated content.

Semiconductors, mobile handsets, LCD displays, broadband equipment, and enterprise software were the core technology value chains. Korea, Japan, Taiwan, China, the United States, India, Sweden, and Germany were the main nodes. India's IT services and business-process outsourcing sectors grew as firms globalized back-office functions.

The EU ETS was an institutional innovation. Its first phase created carbon allowances for power and industrial installations. Early design problems, especially over-allocation, later became clear, but 2005 marked the beginning of carbon pricing as a large-scale market mechanism.

### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

The global labor market was defined by skill polarization, migration, and informality. China's migrant labor force powered factories and construction. India's high-skill services exports grew rapidly, but the majority of workers remained informal. In advanced economies, high-skill finance, technology, health care, and professional services gained income share, while lower-skill tradable manufacturing faced pressure.

Unions remained important in continental Europe, Korea, public sectors, and autos, but bargaining power was uneven. Germany's labor reforms, Italy's competitiveness problems, and France's unemployment showed the policy tension between social protection and labor-market flexibility. Migration was economically significant in Spain, the United Kingdom, the United States, Gulf economies outside the top 20, and parts of Europe. Quantitative household aggregates do not establish distribution across income, skill or migrant groups.

### 6B. Fixed income

The fixed-income story was low long-term yields despite rising short rates. U.S. 10-year Treasury yields averaged roughly the low-to-mid 4 percent range, while the Fed funds target moved up steadily. Sovereign debt stress was not a global theme in 2005, but there were important legacies. Argentina completed a major debt restructuring after its 2001 default. ### 6C. Currencies

The U.S. dollar strengthened against the euro and yen during much of 2005 as interest-rate differentials moved in its favor. China's renminbi reform was the key structural FX event. Asian currencies were watched for follow-on appreciation, but most moves were gradual.

Commodity currencies such as the Canadian dollar and Australian dollar were supported by high resource prices. Brazil's real strengthened on external improvement and high interest rates. Turkey's new lira symbolized stabilization but the economy remained externally exposed.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The most visible crisis was Hurricane Katrina. It caused large human and economic disruption, damaged Gulf Coast infrastructure, affected energy supply chains, and exposed weaknesses in disaster response and insurance systems. It was a macro-relevant shock because U.S. Gulf energy infrastructure was central to refining and distribution. The EIA's later assessment found up to 4.9 million barrels per day of refinery capacity, about 29 percent of the U.S. total, shut during 22-25 September.

The most important non-crisis risk was the U.S. housing and credit boom. At the time, the risk was debated but not fully understood. With hindsight, lax underwriting, securitization incentives, leverage, and reliance on rising house prices were systemic vulnerabilities.

Global imbalances were an active risk, not merely a hindsight risk. Economists disagreed on whether they would unwind gradually through exchange-rate changes and saving shifts or abruptly through dollar and bond-market stress. The abrupt adjustment did not occur in 2005, but the imbalance widened.

Oil-price risk materialized. High energy prices did not cause a global recession in 2005, partly because monetary credibility was stronger than in the 1970s and because energy intensity had declined. However, oil prices redistributed income toward exporters and squeezed real purchasing power in importers.

Other vulnerabilities included euro-area political fragmentation, Japanese deflation risk, banking and public-sector contingent liabilities in China, external deficits in Turkey and Spain, and commodity-dependence in Russia and Brazil.

Outside the ranked group, Indonesia and Thailand faced oil-cost and other pressures. Bank Indonesia reported fuel-price increases in March and October, inflation pressure and weaker purchasing power; December CPI inflation reached 17.11 percent year on year (an end-year rate, against 10.464 percent annual-average inflation in the IMF series), oil-and-gas output fell 3.26 percent, and the BI rate rose to 12.75 percent. The Bank of Thailand identified tsunami-related tourism weakness, drought and oil costs; private consumption grew 4.0 percent and private investment 11.0 percent, down from 16.3 percent in 2004, while the policy rate rose to 4.00 percent. Finance-company credit in Thailand fell partly because companies converted into banks, so the fall is not by itself evidence of a collapse in private credit.

The boundary between contemporary knowledge and hindsight matters. The 20 December mortgage proposal establishes awareness of repayment and underwriting risks, not the magnitude or timing of the later crisis. Hurricane loss counts published in June 2006 are retrospective observations with a stated endpoint, and the BIS's 2006 assessment of global imbalances is an ex-post view of 2005.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (2005 vs. 2004)

2005 was slower than 2004 but still strong. Trade growth decelerated from the exceptional pace of 2004, and world output growth eased, but the expansion remained broad. In the IMF's retrospective data, PPP-weighted world growth slowed from 5.266 to 4.713 percent and goods-and-services trade volume growth from 10.850 to 7.807 percent. The most important shift was that the expansion became more visibly late-cycle: oil was higher, inflation pressure rose, and central banks became less uniformly accommodative.

The United States moved from early tightening to sustained tightening. The Fed's rate path became central to global markets. In contrast, Japan stayed at zero and the ECB waited until December, widening policy divergence. Long-term yields stayed surprisingly low, increasing confidence at the time but raising concern in hindsight.

China's nominal GDP moved ahead of France in the World Bank current-dollar ranking. This was partly real growth, partly exchange-rate and price dynamics, but symbolically important. Brazil moved ahead of India in current dollars because of currency appreciation and commodities, even though India's real growth was faster. Australia moved ahead of the Netherlands on commodity and exchange-rate strength. On the MPD 2023 measure in 2011 international dollars, the top-twenty membership did not change from 2004; current-dollar rank movements do not by themselves show a change in real purchasing-power size.

The commodity boom moved from theme to macro driver. Resource exporters saw better fiscal and external positions. Importers saw margin and household-income pressure. Corporate revenue rankings shifted toward oil majors. The BIS's matched-vintage tables show the U.S. current-account deficit widening by US$137 billion, from US$668 billion to US$805 billion, and oil-exporter surpluses rising by US$178 billion; these are nominal flows, not additions to world GDP. U.S. crude production fell 4.986 percent, a national annual change that is not the same as the hurricane shut-in estimate, which extends into 2006.

New risks emerged or intensified: U.S. mortgage-credit quality, Spain's construction imbalance, global imbalances, oil dependence, and the possibility that low long-term yields were encouraging leverage. Risks that eased included Japanese banking distress, some emerging-market external fragility, and Turkey's post-2001 stabilization credibility.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

**High-confidence data.** Nominal GDP rankings, world GDP, GDP per capita, population, and broad GDP growth are high-confidence for World Bank WDI countries. U.S., euro-area, Japan, U.K., Canada, Australia, Sweden, Switzerland, and Korea data are generally reliable and well documented.

**Medium-confidence data.** Fiscal balances, government debt, current accounts, policy-rate comparisons, and exchange rates are generally reliable for advanced economies but definitions vary. Debt can mean gross public debt, net public debt, central government debt, or general government debt. Budget balances can be central, federal, or general government. Current-account data are more comparable than fiscal data but still subject to later revision. The IMF fiscal series map India's April-March fiscal year to 2005, Australia's figures are calendar-year conversions of fiscal-year data, and Mexico's coverage includes public corporations.

**Lower-confidence or non-comparable data.** Unemployment is the weakest cross-country table item. China's registered urban unemployment is not comparable with U.S. U-3 or OECD harmonized unemployment. India's labor-market informality makes a single unemployment rate misleading. Mexico, Brazil, Turkey, and Russia have definitional and informality issues. Rates from different definitions are therefore flagged rather than treated as identical measures.

**Measures and units.** The ranking levels are World Bank WDI GDP in current U.S. dollars at market exchange rates. The MPD 2023 levels in the country headers are real GDP in 2011 international dollars, and the IMF WEO PPP aggregates are in current international dollars; the three are different price bases and are not mixed. Government ratios use their own nominal denominators and are never computed from the PPP levels. The October 2026 IMF WEO and MPD 2023 are later retrospective series, not the information available in 2005. Policy rates are dated central-bank decisions where available (United States, euro area, United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, Korea, India and Russia), approximate values otherwise (China, Mexico, Brazil, Turkey, Switzerland and Sweden); Japan's setting was a quantity target. For the Netherlands, Switzerland, Sweden and Belgium, and for Korea's debt and fiscal balance, the indicator values are approximate and not taken from the IMF WEO.

**Historical ranking caveats.** Taiwan is excluded from the World Bank country ranking because World Bank WDI does not provide standard country data for Taiwan. UNCTAD reports Taiwan GDP at about $374.1 billion in 2005, below Belgium and above Saudi Arabia, so the top 20 sovereign-country list would not change if Taiwan were added as an economy, but the next-ranked positions would. On the MPD 2023 measure, Indonesia, Iran, Saudi Arabia and Thailand would be in the top twenty instead of the Netherlands, Switzerland, Sweden and Belgium.

**Conflicting data.** Real growth estimates differ materially by source and vintage: China 9.858 percent (MPD), 11.437 percent (IMF WEO) and 9.9 percent (NBS preliminary, January 2006); Germany 1.756 percent (MPD) against 0.886 percent (IMF); Italy 1.752 against 0.762 percent; Russia 8.738 against 6.397 percent; Mexico 3.939 against 2.113 percent; Brazil 4.453 against 3.202 percent; Spain 4.575 against 3.551 percent; Korea 3.209 against 4.358 percent; India 7.371 percent (MPD) against 7.923 percent (IMF, fiscal year). Bank Indonesia's 2005 report gives annual growth of 5.60 percent in its Table 1.1 but 5.1 percent in nearby text, against 5.693 percent in the IMF series and 6.301 percent in MPD. The BIS's contemporary current-account ratios differ from the current IMF series: United States -6.4 percent against -5.642 percent, China +7.2 percent against +5.710 percent, and Saudi Arabia 29.5 percent (US$91 billion) against 28.146 percent. World nominal GDP is US$47.938 trillion in the October 2026 WDI release and US$48,134.2 billion in the IMF WEO. World CPI inflation is about 4.1 percent in WDI, a median of reporting economies, and 3.985 percent in the IMF WEO aggregate. World trade growth is about 6 percent for WTO merchandise export volume and 7.807 percent for IMF goods-and-services volume, which are different measures. Population levels in the ranking table follow the WDI-based ranking source and differ from MPD populations (India 1,145 million against 1,104.8 million in MPD; China 1,304 million against 1,298.2 million; Mexico 108.0 million against 104.5 million; Turkey 68.0 million against 70.2 million; Brazil 186.1 million against 188.3 million). The JST equity capital gain for Japan (14.15 percent) and the Nikkei 225 gain of about 40.2 percent have not been reconciled. The EIA WTI spot average (US$56.64 per barrel) and the EIA U.S. first-purchase price (US$50.28) are different stages of the market, as are the IMF copper benchmark and the USGS U.S. unit value. Agreement between WDI and IMF series is not independent corroboration, since both draw on national sources, and MPD and WEO can share national inputs.
