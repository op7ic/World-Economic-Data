# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1902

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1902 sat inside the late classical gold-standard era and the first modern wave of globalization. The dominant economic pattern was not a single crisis but a dense mix of industrial expansion, imperial finance, commodity dependence, and rising cross-border flows of capital, goods, and migrants. The United States had become the largest economy in the world in the Maddison long-run GDP series, with a 1902 GDP estimate of about 690.13 billion constant 2011 international dollars in the Maddison Project Database 2023 as processed by Our World in Data. China and India remained very large by total output because of population scale, even though per-capita output was far below the North Atlantic industrial core. Western Europe, especially the United Kingdom, Germany, and France, still dominated global finance, shipping, and colonial trade networks.

National cycles diverged sharply. In the Maddison Project Database 2023 reconstruction, real GDP rose 9.77% in Canada, 8.42% in the undivided-India record and 4.33% in the Netherlands, but only 1.03% in the United States after 11.26% in 1901, and it fell 5.65% in Japan, 4.39% in Spain and 7.23% in Mexico. In the Federico-Tena world trade series, the real world export index rose 5.53%, after 1.93% in 1901, and nominal world merchandise exports rose 4.03% to USD 10,151.5 million. British market quotations for Rio coffee, Chilean copper and silver fell by 31.11%, 19.70% and 11.30%.

The year was also a stress test for industrial capitalism. The U.S. anthracite coal strike of 1902 disrupted a strategic energy input and became a landmark in federal mediation between labor and capital; official returns show Pennsylvania anthracite output falling 38.86% while bituminous output rose 15.23%. In Britain, the South African War ended through the Peace of Vereeniging, reducing one major military expenditure shock but leaving reconstruction and imperial administration costs. In Japan, the post-1897 gold standard regime continued to integrate the yen into international finance and supported a state-led industrial and military buildup before the Russo-Japanese War. In China, the Qing state was still absorbing the fiscal and monetary consequences of the Boxer settlement and foreign indemnities.

Financial conditions were shaped by the gold standard, not by modern discretionary central banking. There was no Federal Reserve in the United States. The Bank of England remained the pivot of sterling liquidity, with the official Bank Rate moving from about 3.17 percent in early 1902 to 4 percent by the fourth quarter according to FRED's Bank of England historical rate series. The Reichsbank followed a similar path, from 3.0% in the spring and summer back to 4.0% in December. The Bank of Japan cut its official discount rate four times, from 8.76% to 6.21%, and the Austro-Hungarian Bank cut its rate from 4% to 3.5% on 5 February. Japan was on gold. Many silver-using and bimetallic economies faced exchange-rate and debt-service pressures because international trade and finance were increasingly gold-centered.

The top-economy ranking uses Maddison Project Database 2023 levels as processed by Our World in Data, in constant 2011 international dollars, where exact country-year values exist. Several historically important political economies, especially the Russian Empire and Austria-Hungary, do not map cleanly onto modern country series. Their exact 1902 GDP entries are therefore marked "Data not available" rather than guessed. The ranking should be treated as provisional: high confidence for countries with MPD 2023 1902 values, lower confidence for empires and partitioned territories where Maddison benchmark treatment differs across publications. These 2011-dollar levels are not comparable with the 1990-dollar levels used for the 1901 ranking, so growth cannot be computed from the two years' level tables.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1902

**1. Classical gold standard integration and monetary discipline.** The classical gold standard was the central international monetary architecture. It lowered exchange-rate risk among gold-standard countries and supported large capital exports from Britain, France, Germany, and the Netherlands. Its importance in 1902 was that monetary credibility helped bind global trade and debt markets, but it also constrained domestic stabilization policy. Countries on silver or with weak fiscal credibility faced higher volatility in exchange and capital access; the silver-based Mexican peso depreciated 13.27% against the dollar while the gold-linked mark, franc and guilder moved by less than 0.4%. Forward linkage: the same architecture would transmit stress during later crises, especially the 1907 panic and the 1914 breakdown of prewar globalization.

**2. U.S. industrial dominance with labor conflict.** The United States combined rapid industrial output, rail integration, steel, coal, finance, and mass consumer markets. Crude steel output rose 10.94% and pig iron 12.24% in the official returns. The 1902 anthracite coal strike showed that scale brought coordination problems: fuel shortages threatened cities and industry, and federal mediation became politically necessary. The strike mattered because it foreshadowed a more interventionist state role in labor disputes without yet creating modern labor law. Forward linkage: the Progressive Era would deepen antitrust, labor, food safety, and financial regulation debates; the Biologics Control Act of 1 July 1902 was an early federal product-safety statute.

**3. Britain still financial hegemon, but no longer the unrivaled producer.** London remained the world's premier money market, sterling remained the leading trade and settlement currency, and the Bank of England remained central to liquidity under gold. Yet the British industrial lead was visibly challenged by the United States and Germany. The end of the South African War relieved a major conflict, but the war had exposed imperial costs; the Finance Act 1902 set income tax at 1s 3d per pound and added a grain duty. Forward linkage: Britain would remain the financial core until 1914, while relative industrial power kept shifting toward the U.S. and Germany.

**4. German and Japanese state-backed industrialization.** Germany was a leading heavy-industrial economy, built on coal, steel, chemicals, electrical engineering, and universal banking. Japan was smaller but strategically important, combining textile exports, state infrastructure, and a gold-standard monetary framework. In 1902 Japan also concluded the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, with economic consequences through credibility, security, and borrowing capacity. Germany's new tariff law, enacted on 25 December 1902, was scheduled to take effect only from 1906 after new commercial treaties. Forward linkage: both economies would become central to pre-1914 military and industrial competition.

**5. Imperial and colonial commodity chains.** India, Indonesia, Egypt, Latin America, and parts of Africa were deeply tied to export crops, railways, ports, mines, plantation systems, and colonial fiscal regimes. Growth was often real but uneven, with surplus extraction and vulnerability to terms-of-trade shifts. The end of the South African War affected gold-mining, reconstruction, and imperial finance. The original Aswan dam on the Nile, inaugurated on 10 December 1902 with a design storage of about 1 billion cubic meters, created a large storage reservoir for Egyptian irrigation. Forward linkage: commodity dependence would remain a major source of volatility in later debt and exchange-rate crises.

**6. Trade-policy commitments with delayed effects.** Several 1902 agreements changed future rather than current trade conditions. The Brussels Sugar Convention, signed on 5 March 1902 to curb sugar export bounties, took effect only on 1 September 1903. The British-Chinese commercial treaty of 5 September 1902 tied tariff and transit-tax reform to conditions with a target start of 1 January 1904. Forward linkage: their economic effects belong to 1903-1906 rather than to 1902.

**7. Statistical asymmetry.** 1902 is data-rich for some North Atlantic economies and data-thin for many colonies, empires, and non-market or semi-market economies. GDP, unemployment, debt, current-account, and CPI data are not comparable across many countries. Forward linkage: even twentieth-century economic history remains shaped by the quality and territorial definition of statistics.

### 3A. Global Aggregates

The database has 1902 records for 37 country territories, with no entry for China, the Russian Empire or the Ottoman Empire, so summing the available records would not measure world output. World population was about 1,628.1 million in 1902 against 1,613.9 million in 1901 (+0.88%) in the January 2026 Federico-Tena reconstruction on 1991 borders. This is an author-published reconstruction adjusted to mid-year where possible, not a census count.

Global trade volume was rising, and the period belonged to the first globalization, with falling trade costs, expanding steamship and rail networks, and rising capital flows before 1914. In the Federico-Tena World Trade Historical Database, the real world export index (1913 = 100) rose from 62.26 to 65.71, or 5.53%, while nominal world merchandise exports at current borders rose from USD 9,758.6 million to USD 10,151.5 million (+4.03%). These are merchandise exports only, not services or total trade.

Reserve currency dynamics: sterling was the leading trade, reserve, and bill-of-exchange currency. The U.S. dollar was tied to gold but the United States did not yet have a central bank and was not yet the central reserve-currency issuer it would become after the world wars. French francs, German marks, Dutch guilders, and other gold-standard currencies played important regional and financial roles. Silver-linked economies remained vulnerable to gold/silver movements.

Global inflation trends: comparable global CPI data are Data not available. The gold-standard core generally experienced low long-run inflation compared with fiat eras, but individual countries experienced local price movements from harvest conditions, credit conditions, transport costs, and wars. Retrospective consumer-price reconstructions in the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database range from declines of 2.27% in Spain and 2.20% in Belgium to rises of 3.87% in Japan and 6.78% in Australia, with zero measured change in the United Kingdom and France; no population-weighted world inflation rate exists. Any precise cross-country inflation comparison for 1902 is low confidence because national series differ in coverage and construction.

Key commodity prices: gold was fixed under the U.S. gold standard at about $20.67 per troy ounce. Oil was not yet an OPEC-era commodity system; OPEC did not exist. The key energy input for industrial economies was coal, and coal supply disruptions were economically material, as shown by the U.S. anthracite strike.

International capital flows: capital generally flowed from Britain and other European financial centers toward the Americas, Russia, colonies, railways, mines, ports, sovereign debt, and utilities. The gold standard reduced exchange-rate risk for credible borrowers, while colonial status could both lower borrowing costs through imperial guarantees and intensify extractive fiscal structures. ### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

United States: no Federal Reserve existed. Monetary conditions were shaped by the gold standard, Treasury operations, national banks, trust companies, New York call money, and seasonal agricultural demand for currency. A recession began in September 1902 under the NBER chronology and continued into 1904, with the trough dated August 1904, so late-1902 financial conditions were more fragile than early-year industrial optimism suggested. Policy/key interest rate: Data not available because no modern policy rate existed; the Macrohistory Database market short-term rate rose from 4.00% to 5.15%.

United Kingdom: the Bank of England was the central institution of the global gold-standard system. FRED's Bank of England policy-rate series reports quarterly values for 1902 of 3.17 percent, 3.00 percent, 3.00 percent, and 4.00 percent. The mean of the twelve monthly averages of Bank Rate in the NBER Macrohistory series was 3.33%, against 3.73% in 1901.

Germany: the Reichsbank managed gold convertibility and domestic credit within a fast-growing industrial system. Its official discount rate, in the NBER Macrohistory monthly series, eased from 4.0% in December 1901 to 3.8% in January and 3.0% from March to September, then rose to 3.9% in October and 4.0% in November and December. Germany's banks remained closely tied to industrial finance, especially in steel, chemicals, electrical engineering, and heavy industry.

France and Austria-Hungary: the Banque de France discount rate stayed at 3.0% in every month of 1901 and 1902. The Austro-Hungarian Bank, whose official rate applied uniformly across the monarchy, cut its discount rate from 4% to 3.5% on 5 February 1902.

Japan: Japan had adopted the gold standard in 1897. In 1902, the Bank of Japan operated inside that regime, with sterling as an important reference in international finance. The Bank of Japan Head Office discount rate on commercial bills was cut on 19 March (to 8.03%), 27 June (7.30%), 3 October (6.57%) and 9 December (6.21%), from 8.76% at the end of 1901. The monetary framework mattered because it improved Japan's access to foreign capital and supported state-led industrialization.

Exchange-rate regime context: Bretton Woods did not exist. The relevant framework was the classical gold standard, bimetallism or silver standards in some economies, colonial currency boards or pegs in colonies, and capital controls that varied widely. Exchange rates among gold-standard currencies were anchored by gold parities, while silver and weak-credit countries faced wider instability.

Stock markets and bonds: official exchange-wide annual index returns for the New York Stock Exchange, London Stock Exchange, Paris Bourse, Berlin Bourse, Tokyo, and other exchanges in 1902 are Data not available; selected country-basket total returns and long-term yields from the Macrohistory Database and its rate-of-return extension are given in 6A and 6B. Bond markets were more central than equities for international finance, with sovereign, railway, municipal, mining, and utility securities widely held in London and Paris.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The global trade system remained imperial and tariff-fragmented. There was no WTO, no IMF, no World Bank, and no Bretton Woods institutions. Britain leaned toward free-trade ideology, although imperial preference debates were growing; the Finance Act 1902 introduced a grain duty of 3d per hundredweight on wheat from 15 April. The United States had protective tariffs and a rapidly expanding industrial export base. Germany combined industrial exports with cartels and universal-bank finance, and enacted a higher tariff on 25 December 1902 with effect only from 1906. Colonial trade channels structured flows from India, Indonesia, Egypt, Africa, and Southeast Asia.

Major geopolitical events included the Peace of Vereeniging ending the South African War on May 31, 1902, and the Anglo-Japanese Alliance. The former affected British public finance, South African mining, and imperial reconstruction; under the treaty Britain committed a grant of GBP 3 million for reconstruction and loans free of interest for two years and at 3% thereafter. The latter improved Japan's strategic standing and helped shape regional capital confidence. China's Boxer indemnity obligations remained a major fiscal issue. Argentina and Chile signed naval-limitation and arbitration agreements on 28 May 1902, and on 20 December Germany proclaimed a blockade of the Venezuelan ports of Puerto Cabello and Maracaibo.

Regional integration was limited by modern standards. The European Economic Community, ASEAN, NAFTA, IMF, BIS, and World Bank did not exist. Integration happened through gold parities, shipping networks, railways, migration chains, colonial administrations, and private financial markets rather than through post-1945 institutions. The Brussels Sugar Convention of 5 March 1902 was a multilateral commodity agreement that took effect on 1 September 1903.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Coal was the main industrial energy source. The U.S. anthracite strike directly exposed how energy, labor, railroads, households, and urban heating were interlinked: Pennsylvania anthracite output fell from 60.4 to 36.9 million long tons (-38.86%), while bituminous output rose from 225.8 to 260.2 million short tons (+15.23%). Oil was rising but still regionally concentrated, notably in the United States and the Russian Empire's Baku region; U.S. crude petroleum output rose from 69.4 to 88.8 million barrels of 42 gallons (+27.93%), while its value rose only 7.17%. OPEC context: not applicable.

Metals and minerals: steel, iron, coal, copper, gold, and silver underpinned industrial and financial systems. South African gold mining was strategically important to British imperial finance and global gold supply. U.S. copper output rose 9.54% to 659.5 million pounds. In the 1912 Belgian statistical yearbook's comparative tables, 1902 coal output excluding lignite was 230.7 million metric tons in Great Britain and Ireland, 107.5 million in Germany, 29.4 million in France and 21.4 million in Belgium (net production).

Agricultural commodities: wheat, rice, cotton, sugar, coffee, tea, jute, and rubber shaped trade balances and household welfare. Climatic and harvest shocks could move prices and wages; the Federation drought in Australia caused multi-year pastoral losses. ### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Global employment was still overwhelmingly agricultural, especially in Asia, Africa, Latin America, and Eastern Europe. Industrial employment was concentrated in the United States, Britain, Germany, France, Belgium, northern Italy, parts of the Russian Empire, and Japan's expanding textile and heavy-industry centers. Modern unemployment statistics were not standardized, and cross-country unemployment rates for 1902 are generally Data not available. Historical estimates exist for a few economies: 2.7% in the United States (Lebergott), 2.9% in Germany, 3.84% in the Netherlands, 4.0% in the United Kingdom, 4.8% in Australia and 9.9% in France in the Macrohistory Database, on differing union-based or reconstructed definitions.

Migration remained economically significant. Europeans continued moving to North America, Argentina, Brazil, Australia, and other destinations; immigrant arrivals in the United States rose from 487,918 in the year to June 1901 to 648,743 in the year to June 1902 (+32.96%). Asian labor migration was heavily shaped by colonial rules and racial restrictions. Urbanization continued as railways, ports, factories, and mines pulled labor into cities. **Economic narrative:**

The United States was the largest economy in the Maddison series in 1902. Its core strengths were manufacturing scale, railroads, steel, coal, oil, machinery, agriculture, finance, and an increasingly integrated domestic market. But 1902 was not just expansion. The NBER chronology places the start of a recession in September 1902, meaning the late-year economy was moving into a contraction after a strong turn-of-century expansion; reconstructed real GDP growth slowed to 1.03% from 11.26%.

The defining microeconomic event was the anthracite coal strike. Energy supply, railway ownership, mine labor, and urban households all collided in one dispute, and Pennsylvania anthracite output fell 38.86% for the year. Federal mediation under Theodore Roosevelt did not create a modern welfare state, but it marked a shift in the perceived public interest in industrial disputes.

Outside anthracite, the physical record shows continued growth. The Statistical Abstract of the United States records crude steel output rising from 13.47 to 14.95 million long tons (+10.94%), pig iron from 15.88 to 17.82 million long tons (+12.24%), bituminous coal from 225.8 to 260.2 million short tons (+15.23%) and crude petroleum from 69.4 to 88.8 million barrels (+27.93%). Consumer prices rose 0.98% and the Lebergott unemployment estimate edged up to 2.7%.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Steel and rail equipment remained central; coal mining exposed labor risk; oil was rising but not yet the dominant energy system; finance was powerful but structurally fragile because trust companies and seasonal liquidity pressures operated without a central bank. International Harvester was incorporated on 12 August 1902, combining five harvesting-machinery companies, and Minnesota Mining and Manufacturing (3M) was founded in 1902. Willis Carrier's humidity-control design for a printing plant dates from 17 July 1902. Savings banks reported deposits of USD 2,650.1 million in June 1902, up 5.22%, with real-estate loans up 4.51%; total bank loans in the Macrohistory Database rose 10.08%. The Macrohistory nominal house-price index rose 14.59%, and newspaper asking-price indices rose 8.52% in New York City but fell 7.81% in Boston, with asking rents up 5.46% and 3.04%. Real consumption per person in the Barro-Ursúa reconstruction rose 0.33%. The nominal equity total return in the rate-of-return reconstruction was 5.41%, after 20.38% in 1901.

The main economic story was institutional weakness under external pressure. Foreign-controlled customs, treaty ports, and indemnity payments constrained fiscal sovereignty. Domestic markets remained large, but infrastructure, banking, and modern manufacturing were uneven and concentrated in specific coastal or treaty-port nodes. The British-Chinese commercial treaty signed on 5 September 1902 tied customs-tariff changes to the abolition of internal transit taxes (likin), conditional on the agreement of other treaty powers, with a target start of 1 January 1904.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

Active crises in 1902 included the U.S. anthracite coal strike, the onset of the U.S. recession in September, and the fiscal aftermath of the South African War, in which the Vereeniging terms committed Britain to a GBP 3 million reconstruction grant and to loans interest-free for two years and at 3% thereafter. China faced ongoing indemnity and sovereignty pressures after the Boxer settlement. Commodity-dependent economies faced exposure to price movements, harvest shocks, and external credit conditions: Rio coffee fell 31.11%, Australia suffered the Federation drought, and Mexico absorbed a 13.27% peso depreciation and a 14.25% rise in Mexico City prices. The German blockade of Venezuelan ports in December added a sovereign-dispute risk in Latin America.

Near-misses and vulnerabilities included the absence of a U.S. central bank, seasonal liquidity stress, trust-company fragility, and reliance on gold flows. These risks did not fully materialize in 1902, but with hindsight they foreshadowed the Panic of 1907.

Hindsight label: the pre-1914 global economy looked stable to many contemporaries because trade, migration, and capital flowed widely. Retrospectively, it was vulnerable to geopolitical shocks, imperial rivalries, bank liquidity crises, and social conflict. The gold standard was credible but not flexible.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1902 vs. 1901)

The clearest U.S. shift was from expansion to recession: the NBER chronology places a U.S. peak in September 1902, after which contraction began. Reconstructed U.S. real GDP growth slowed from 11.26% to 1.03%, and the anthracite output fall of 38.86% contrasted with continued gains in steel and bituminous coal. Labor conflict intensified through the coal strike, making the energy-labor nexus more visible than in 1901.

Globally, the South African War ended in 1902, changing Britain's fiscal and imperial context. Japan's strategic position strengthened through the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, reinforcing the credibility of a gold-standard, rapidly modernizing Asian power. China moved further into the post-Boxer indemnity regime. In the same MPD 2023 series, growth accelerated in the United Kingdom (from -0.11% to +2.45%), Germany (from -2.36% to +2.39%) and Australia (from -2.97% to +1.03%), but reversed in Spain (from +8.36% to -4.39%), Mexico (from +8.58% to -7.23%) and Japan (from +3.09% to -5.65%). Real world merchandise exports grew 5.53% after 1.93%. Official discount rates ended the year 2.55 pp lower in Japan and 0.50 pp lower in Austria-Hungary, and at the same December level in Germany and France.

Structural breaks were limited at the global level: this was still the same gold-standard, first-globalization regime as 1901. The delta was a change in stress signals rather than a full regime change. New risks emerged in U.S. finance and labor markets; war-related risks eased in southern Africa; imperial and credit risks persisted. New commitments in 1902, including the Brussels Sugar Convention, the British-Chinese commercial treaty, the German tariff law, the Italian labor law and the U.S. Biologics Control Act, took full effect only in later years.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

Highest confidence: Maddison Project Database 2023 GDP values for countries with exact 1902 records, namely the United States, United Kingdom, India, Germany, France, Italy, Japan, Spain, Indonesia, Belgium, Canada, Netherlands, Mexico, Australia, Argentina and Brazil, in the limited sense that the figures are the database's published values; as reconstructions they carry wide error margins, especially for India, Indonesia, Mexico, Argentina and Brazil, which is why the overall confidence column grades several of them low. Bank of England quarterly policy-rate values are also relatively high confidence because they come from the Bank of England/FRED historical series, as are the dated discount-rate changes of the Reichsbank, Banque de France, Bank of Japan and Austro-Hungarian Bank, statutory and treaty dates, and official physical-output tables. Retrospective price, consumption, credit and equity-return reconstructions are moderate in quality; early property-price indices are weaker.

Medium confidence: broad themes about the gold standard, first globalization, U.S. coal strike, South African War, and Japanese gold-standard regime. These are well supported by economic-history sources, but exact annual quantitative transmission channels are not always measurable.

Low confidence or explicitly incomplete: exact 1902 GDP ranks and GDP figures for the Russian Empire, Austria-Hungary and Ottoman/Turkey, and China's level, which is a 1900 benchmark. The issue is not that these economies were unimportant; it is that the Maddison Project Database 2023 has no 1902 record matching these historical empires and partitioned territories. Rankings involving those entries should be treated as approximate. The ranking is also uncertain around ranks 15-20: in the same database, Switzerland (22.39 billion 2011 international dollars) exceeds Argentina, and Switzerland and Sweden (17.44 billion) exceed Brazil, while the record for the later Austrian republic (28.63 billion) is not a measure of Austria-Hungary.

The levels in this ranking are in 2011 international dollars from the Maddison Project Database 2023, while the 1901 ranking used Maddison-style levels in 1990 international dollars. The implied change between the two years' level tables therefore mixes a change of unit and vintage with real growth and must not be read as growth; the growth rates shown come from within the 2023 database. Populations differ for the same reason: the 1901 table shows 275.0 million for British India and 39.5 million for the Dutch East Indies, against 288.0 million and 46.06 million in the database records for undivided India and modern Indonesia used here. For India and Indonesia, growth, population and per-capita GDP are taken from the same records as the GDP levels; the 1901 table, built on British India and Dutch East Indies units, showed no growth for them.

Several indicators rest on conflicting or narrowly defined sources. U.S. unemployment is 2.7% in Lebergott's estimate but 3.7% in the Mitchell-based Macrohistory series; the lower figure is shown, as in 1901, and the difference is unresolved. The Mexican peso was 2.39 per dollar in the INEGI historical statistics but 2.42 in the Gómez-Galvarriato-Musacchio price study. German and British budget ratios are withheld because the Macrohistory revenue and expenditure series do not match a reconciled government perimeter; the Australian ratio is withheld because Federation-era accounts are not consolidated and the series has no 1901 value. The Belgian current-account field in the Macrohistory Database is a merchandise trade balance, and its Belgian franc rate differs from its French franc rate although both currencies shared one gold parity, so neither is shown. For Italy, Spain, Belgium and the Netherlands only market short-term rates are available, so the policy-rate rows show them in the notes rather than as official rates. The British policy-rate row shows the fourth-quarter average of Bank Rate, without a year-on-year change on that basis, because the 1901 table used a December average; the change in annual means (-0.40 pp) is given in the notes. The German and Australian exchange rates are rescaled from the Macrohistory Database's normalized currency units to marks and pounds. Large reconstructed swings in real consumption per person for Spain, Argentina and Mexico are not reported because they could not be traced to original components.

Unavailable or not comparable: unemployment rates outside a few economies, CPI inflation for colonies and empires, government debt and budget ratios without compatible GDP denominators, current-account ratios outside the Macrohistory Database economies, official policy rates for most countries without central banks, exchange-wide stock-index returns, corporate revenue and market-capitalization rankings, and city real-estate transaction prices. For 1902, many official statistical systems either did not collect modern measures or used definitions that are not comparable across countries. Data not available means that no observation with the stated definition and territory was found for 1902, not that no historical record exists.

Most important data conflict: Maddison-style GDP estimates differ across versions, benchmark dollars, territorial definitions, and treatment of empires. MPD/OWID 2023 expresses values in constant 2011 international dollars and derives GDP by multiplying GDP per capita by population. Older Maddison tables often use 1990 international dollars and sometimes treat territories such as Russia/Soviet Union, India, Germany, and the United Kingdom differently. Exact database values are therefore kept separate from benchmark cross-checks.
