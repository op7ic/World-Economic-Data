# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 2004

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

2004 was the strongest global expansion year of the early twenty-first century. IMF reporting in April 2005 described world output growth at about 5.1 percent, the fastest pace in nearly three decades, while the World Bank WITS/WDI current-dollar world GDP measure put global output at about US$44.18 trillion. In the IMF's October 2026 World Economic Outlook database, PPP-weighted world real growth is 5.266 percent, up from 3.802 percent in 2003, and market-exchange-rate-weighted growth is 4.024 percent; these are later retrospective estimates with different weights, not figures known in 2004. The expansion was broad: the United States accelerated from the post-dot-com recovery into a credit- and housing-supported upswing; China continued double-digit industrialization; India grew rapidly in services, construction and manufacturing; commodity exporters benefited from higher prices; and Japan continued its uneven recovery from deflation and bank balance-sheet stress.

The year was defined by a rare combination of synchronized demand, easy-to-tightening financial conditions and rising commodity scarcity. The Federal Reserve began lifting the federal funds target from 1 percent in June and, in five 25-basis-point steps, reached 2.25 percent by December, with the discount rate at 3.25 percent; long-term rates nevertheless stayed low by historical standards. The ECB held its main refinancing (minimum bid) rate at 2 percent, the Bank of Japan remained at the zero-rate and quantitative-easing boundary and raised its current-account-balance target in January, and the Bank of England tightened to 4.75 percent. China combined credit and investment restraint with an October increase in its commercial lending and deposit benchmarks. Financial markets therefore faced a transition from emergency accommodation toward normalization, without yet pricing the full leverage and housing risks that would become visible later.

Trade was the second defining force. The WTO's April 2005 release estimated real merchandise trade volume growth of about 9 percent in 2004, about twice the pace of 2003; that figure does not cover services trade or nominal export earnings. China, Korea, Singapore and Japan were central to the Asian export cycle; Germany and the Netherlands benefited from global capital-goods demand and intra-European trade; and commodity exporters from Russia to Brazil to Australia gained from energy, metals and agricultural demand. The U.S. current account deficit widened, while Asia and oil exporters accumulated surpluses, reinforcing the global imbalance pattern of the mid-2000s.

The microeconomic story was equally important. Google went public, the mobile-telecom industry consolidated, JPMorgan Chase completed the Bank One merger, Lenovo agreed to buy IBM's personal-computer business, and oil, autos, retail, finance and telecoms dominated global corporate rankings. Supply chains became more China-centered, European integration deepened with the May 2004 EU enlargement, and household balance sheets in several advanced economies became more exposed to real estate and credit cycles. The Indian Ocean tsunami of 26 December inflicted catastrophic local damage whose reconstruction and output effects mainly unfolded in 2005. With hindsight, 2004 looks like both a high-growth year and an incubation year for later vulnerabilities: global imbalances, low risk premia, U.S. housing leverage, European competitiveness divergence and commodity dependence; that later outcome was not certain in 2004.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 2004

**Theme 1: Synchronized global expansion with emerging-market acceleration.** 2004 was historically significant because growth was strong in both advanced and emerging economies. The United States grew close to 4 percent, China above 10 percent on revised series, India near 8 percent, Russia above 7 percent and Brazil close to 6 percent. This made 2004 a bridge between the post-2001 recovery and the pre-crisis boom of 2005-2007. The countries most affected were China, India, Russia, Brazil, Korea, the United States and Germany. The forward linkage was clear: high growth encouraged risk-taking, cross-border lending and capital inflows, while also creating bottlenecks in oil, metals, electricity, shipping and skilled labor.

**Theme 2: Monetary policy normalization began, but global liquidity stayed abundant.** The Federal Reserve started a "measured pace" tightening cycle in June 2004, but policy rates remained low relative to nominal GDP growth and credit appetite. The Bank of England tightened earlier, the ECB stayed on hold, and Japan remained under quantitative easing. This mix mattered because it allowed global credit conditions to remain supportive even as short rates rose; low long yields and housing collateral could offset some of the short-rate transmission. The United States, United Kingdom, Australia, Canada and several emerging markets faced housing or credit expansion pressures. With hindsight, 2004 was not yet a crisis year, but it was a year in which cheap funding, search for yield and structured-credit growth became more embedded; contemporary central banks still judged aggregate financial conditions supportive.

**Theme 3: China moved from export engine to macro-management challenge.** China's economy continued rapid integration into global production networks. The problem in 2004 was not weak demand, but overheating: fixed-asset investment, energy shortages, transport bottlenecks and rising inflation pressures led authorities to tighten credit and raise benchmark lending rates. The NBS preliminary release put fixed-asset investment growth at 25.8 percent, and the People's Bank of China raised the one-year lending benchmark from 5.31 to 5.58 percent effective 29 October. China's expansion affected Japan, Korea, Germany, Australia, Brazil, Russia and oil exporters through trade and commodity channels. The forward linkage was China's growing role as the marginal buyer of industrial commodities and as a driver of global manufacturing relocation.

**Theme 4: Commodity exporters regained macro leverage.** Oil prices rose sharply, with Brent averaging about US$38 per barrel and monthly averages moving much higher in the second half. Metals prices were also strong: the IMF's LME copper benchmark rose 60.9 percent. Russia, Brazil, Australia, Canada and parts of the Middle East and Africa gained fiscal and external room. The significance is that commodity-exporting economies entered the mid-2000s with stronger current accounts, reserves and public finances than in the late 1990s. The forward linkage was the commodity supercycle, which strengthened through 2007 and reshaped capital spending in mining, energy, ports and rail.

**Theme 5: Europe deepened integration but exposed divergence.** On 1 May 2004, ten countries joined the European Union: Cyprus, Czechia, Estonia, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Malta, Poland, Slovakia and Slovenia. This was economically important because it expanded the single market eastward, increased labor mobility over time and reinforced foreign direct investment into Central and Eastern Europe. EU membership was not simultaneous euro adoption, and the accession changed the institutional setting rather than the physical output of each entrant at the accession date. At the same time, the euro area core still had weak domestic demand and fiscal-rule tensions. Germany relied heavily on exports and wage restraint; France and Italy struggled with reform and competitiveness; Spain boomed on construction and credit. The forward linkage was the widening divergence inside Europe that later mattered for the euro-area crisis.

**Theme 6: Corporate scale, platforms and consolidation advanced.** The largest global companies were still dominated by retail, oil, autos and industrial conglomerates, with Walmart, BP, Exxon Mobil, Shell, GM, Ford, DaimlerChrysler, Toyota, GE and Total among the largest by revenue. Yet 2004 also marked a platform turning point through Google's IPO and the scaling of internet advertising. Telecom consolidation accelerated, including Cingular's acquisition of AT&T Wireless, while U.S. banking consolidation continued with JPMorgan Chase and Bank One. IPO receipts, revenue, profit and market capitalization are different measures, and transactions announced in 2004 for completion in 2005, such as Lenovo-IBM, remained announcements at year-end. The forward linkage was a shift toward platform economics, data-driven advertising, mobile scale and universal banking.

**Theme 7: Global imbalances became a central macro risk.** The United States ran a current account deficit above 5 percent of GDP, while Japan, Germany, China, Russia, Switzerland, Sweden, the Netherlands and oil exporters accumulated surpluses. The immediate 2004 interpretation was that capital flowed willingly into dollar assets and U.S. consumption supported world demand. With hindsight, the same imbalance pattern interacted with low yields, mortgage credit, securitization and reserve accumulation, contributing to systemic fragility.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

World GDP in current U.S. dollars was about US$44.18 trillion on the World Bank WITS/WDI measure; the current WDI release gives US$44.26 trillion with real growth of 4.495 percent. IMF communications in April 2005 put real global growth at about 5.1 percent, the strongest in decades. World trade expanded faster than output: the WTO's 14 April 2005 release estimated merchandise trade volume growth of about 9 percent and a 31 percent developing-economy share of merchandise trade, with Asia, South and Central America, the Commonwealth of Independent States and commodity-exporting developing economies performing especially strongly. The 6.5 percent figure in the same release was a 2005 forecast, not 2004 growth. Merchandise volume, goods-and-services volume and current-dollar values measure different things.

Reserve-currency dynamics remained dollar-centered, but the dollar was under pressure. The euro traded much stronger than in 2001-2002, averaging EUR 0.804 per dollar (about US$1.24 per euro) in WDI official data, the Canadian dollar and several commodity currencies appreciated, and Asian reserve accumulation remained substantial. China's renminbi stayed effectively pegged near 8.28 per U.S. dollar. Japan's yen averaged about 108.2 per dollar. The international monetary system was a managed-float system dominated by the dollar, not a Bretton Woods fixed-rate system. Commodity prices tightened materially. Brent crude averaged about US$38.26 per barrel in EIA spot data, up from roughly US$28.83 in 2003, and monthly Brent prices moved toward US$50 in October; WTI averaged US$41.51. Gold averaged around US$410 per troy ounce, supported by dollar weakness and investor demand. Copper prices rose strongly as China-led industrial demand met constrained mine supply. Agricultural staples were mixed: grains were not in a generalized food crisis, but freight, energy and input costs were rising.

International capital flowed toward the United States and other deficit economies through bond markets, banking flows and portfolio investment, while emerging Asia and oil exporters accumulated reserves. Foreign direct investment continued to favor China, Central and Eastern Europe, and selected service and resource sectors. The broad direction was surplus-country saving into dollar and euro financial assets, plus multinational investment into supply-chain and commodity capacity.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The United States began the year with the federal funds target at 1 percent and ended it at 2.25 percent after a sequence of 25 basis-point increases. The 30 June statement raised the target to 1.25 percent and approved a discount rate of 2.25 percent; further target increases on 10 August, 21 September, 10 November and 14 December took it to 1.50, 1.75, 2.00 and 2.25 percent, and the December action set the discount rate at 3.25 percent. Neither is an annual-average effective market rate. From the December meeting, FOMC minutes were released earlier, with that meeting's minutes published on 4 January 2005. The Federal Reserve judged that emergency accommodation was no longer necessary, but inflation remained contained enough to tighten gradually. Long-term Treasury yields did not rise proportionately, supporting mortgages, credit and equity valuations.

The euro area was more subdued. The ECB held the main refinancing (minimum bid) rate at 2 percent throughout 2004, with the deposit facility at 1 percent and marginal lending at 3 percent. Germany, France and Italy had weak domestic demand relative to the global expansion, while Spain and Ireland were more credit-sensitive. The United Kingdom was stronger: the Bank of England raised its repo rate by 25 basis points to 4.75 percent on 5 August, reflecting housing and demand pressures, although the accompanying statement already noted signs of easing housing momentum.

Japan remained exceptional. Short-term rates were held near zero under quantitative easing, and the Bank of Japan's current-account-balance target remained the main operating framework; on 20 January it raised the target from around JPY 27-32 trillion to JPY 30-35 trillion. Deflation pressures were diminishing but not gone. Japanese banks and corporations were healthier than earlier in the decade, but the recovery still depended heavily on exports and corporate balance-sheet repair.

Elsewhere, tightening was selective. The People's Bank of China's notice of 28 October, effective 29 October, raised the one-year deposit benchmark from 1.98 to 2.25 percent and the one-year lending benchmark from 5.31 to 5.58 percent, leaving central-bank lending and reserve-remuneration rates unchanged. The Reserve Bank of India raised its liquidity-absorption (LAF) rate by 25 basis points to 4.75 percent effective 27 October. The Bank of Thailand raised its 14-day repurchase target three times, on 25 August, 20 October and 15 December, from 1.25 to 2 percent. Brazil's Selic target reached 17.75 percent in December. In contrast, the Bank of Korea cut its overnight call-rate target to 3.25 percent on 11 November, and the Reserve Bank of Australia held its cash rate at 5.25 percent throughout the year. These instruments are not interchangeable.

Equity markets rose moderately. U.S. equities posted positive returns, with the S&P 500 total return around 10.9 percent. Germany's DAX rose about 7.3 percent, Britain's FTSE 100 about 7.5 percent and Japan's Nikkei 225 about 7.6 percent. Bond markets remained supported by low inflation expectations and strong demand for safe assets. Credit conditions were favorable in most advanced economies and improving in emerging markets.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The WTO Doha Round remained unresolved, but trade volumes grew rapidly despite political frictions. The EU enlargement of 2004 was the major regional-integration event, expanding the single market from 15 to 25 members and accelerating investment links between Western Europe and Central and Eastern Europe. Later comparison series built on EU27 or EU14 groupings do not describe the actual 2004 membership. NAFTA continued to shape North American trade, especially for Mexico and Canada. Asian production networks deepened around China, Japan, Korea, Taiwan and ASEAN economies.

Geopolitical risks were present but did not derail global growth. The Iraq war continued to affect oil risk premia and fiscal pressures. Russia's Yukos affair raised concerns about property rights and state influence in strategic sectors, even as oil revenues strengthened Russia's macro position. China's WTO-era integration continued, but trade tensions around textiles, technology and exchange-rate policy were building; the expiry of textile quotas at the start of 2005 was a forward linkage rather than a 2004 event. ### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil was the central commodity story. Demand growth from China, the United States and broader global recovery met limited spare capacity, geopolitical risk and refining constraints. The BIS reported global oil-demand growth of roughly 3.5 percent in 2004, with China accounting for about one-third of the increase; its discussion of OPEC spare capacity of 1 to 2 million barrels per day, excluding Iraq, referred to recent months near its mid-2005 publication rather than a 2004 average. Refinery constraints and light/heavy grade spreads complicated a single-price narrative. OPEC remained important, but the market increasingly focused on capacity limits and the ability of non-OPEC supply to respond. Russia, Canada, Brazil, Australia and Middle Eastern producers gained from higher export revenues. An IMF notice of January 2005 expected Saudi Arabia's oil and non-oil real growth to be around 5 percent and real income to rise about 17 percent including terms-of-trade gains; these were expectations, not the later revised growth series.

Metals benefited from industrial construction, infrastructure and manufacturing demand. Copper, aluminum, iron ore and steel markets reflected Chinese fixed-asset investment and global capital-goods orders. Mining investment increased, but supply responses were slow because projects required long lead times and infrastructure.

Agriculture did not experience a 2007-2008 style price shock, but input costs, freight rates and weather remained important. Brazil expanded agribusiness exports, India had stronger rural demand after better agricultural conditions, and China's grain policy remained important for domestic stability. The EIA Brent and WTI averages are spot benchmarks, not posted Arabian Light prices or every exporter's realized revenue, and the gold figure of about US$410 is an approximate annual average rather than a reconciled fixing-calendar mean.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor markets improved in the United States, Canada, Australia and parts of Asia, while unemployment remained high in France, Germany, Italy, Spain and Turkey. Definitions varied widely: China's preliminary release reported end-year urban registered unemployment of 4.2 percent, which understated labor-market slack and is not an all-resident survey measure; India lacked a directly comparable annual unemployment rate; and informal work was large in emerging economies. Country tables in section 4 use ILO modeled unemployment estimates, which differ from national registered rates. Growth in output did not necessarily produce equal gains in wages, participation or household consumption.

Demographically, advanced economies continued aging, especially Japan and Germany. China still had a large working-age expansion but was moving toward later demographic pressure. India's young labor force was becoming a structural growth advantage, provided education, infrastructure and job creation kept pace. Migration mattered in Europe and North America, particularly in construction, services and agriculture. **Economic narrative:**

The United States was the main demand engine of the world economy. Growth was supported by household consumption, residential investment, business equipment spending and defense-related federal outlays. The output gap narrowed, payrolls recovered and corporate profits improved, but growth was accompanied by a larger current-account deficit and heavy reliance on foreign capital inflows.

Policy shifted from crisis support toward normalization. The Federal Reserve began raising short-term rates in June, but the housing market and credit channels remained powerful because long-term yields stayed low. At the micro level, autos faced intense competition, energy companies benefited from oil prices, finance expanded mortgage and structured-credit activities, and technology sentiment improved as internet advertising became investable again after the dot-com collapse. The Federal Reserve's February 2005 Monetary Policy Report described increasingly popular adjustable-rate and hybrid mortgages; its household-obligations measure for the fourth quarter of 2004 was a projection, and low aggregate debt service did not establish uniform borrower resilience.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Google's IPO signaled the rise of search advertising as a large-scale business model; JPMorgan Chase completed the Bank One merger; Cingular's AT&T Wireless acquisition reshaped U.S. mobile telecom; household debt and mortgage exposure increased; retail scale remained symbolized by Walmart; real estate was already a major growth mechanism and a future vulnerability. In the BIS's residential table, nominal house prices rose 11.2 percent over the year to end-period and mortgage debt rose 3.9 percentage points of GDP. FDIC data show insured banks and thrifts earned US$31.8 billion in the fourth quarter alone, with a quarterly return on assets of 1.28 percent, but only 48.4 percent of institutions reached a 1 percent return on assets, so profitability was uneven.

### 5B. Corporate landscape

The largest corporations by revenue were still old-economy giants: Walmart, BP, Exxon Mobil, Shell, General Motors, Ford, DaimlerChrysler, Toyota, General Electric and Total were among the leading names. Fiscal periods differ across these companies, so the list illustrates scale rather than an exact league table. Energy companies benefited from rising prices, automakers faced both volume strength and margin pressure, and Walmart represented the global scale of retail logistics and supplier bargaining power.

Major corporate transactions reflected consolidation. JPMorgan Chase completed the Bank One merger, effective at 12:01 a.m. on 1 July 2004, strengthening U.S. universal banking; the 1.32-for-one share exchange was not a cash payment. Cingular's acquisition of AT&T Wireless created a larger U.S. mobile operator. Sanofi-Synthelabo's acquisition battle for Aventis reshaped European pharmaceuticals. Oracle's pursuit of PeopleSoft signaled enterprise-software consolidation: Oracle obtained control of roughly 75 percent of PeopleSoft in late December 2004, and the second-step merger completed on 7 January 2005. Lenovo announced in December that it would buy IBM's personal-computer business for US$1.25 billion in cash and equity plus roughly US$500 million of assumed liabilities; IBM's 7 December filing expected closing in the second quarter of 2005, so the deal was an announcement in 2004, combining Lenovo's manufacturing and Chinese distribution with IBM's PC brand, enterprise channels and design capabilities.

Google's 2004 IPO was smaller in revenue terms than oil or retail giants, but more important for the long-run platform economy. Its final prospectus of 18 August offered 19,605,052 shares at US$85, a total of US$1,666.4 million, of which only 14,142,135 shares were sold by the company; selling-shareholder proceeds were not company cash. Its 2004 annual report shows net IPO proceeds of US$1,161.1 million after costs, revenue of US$3,189.2 million, net income of US$399.1 million and research and development expense of US$225.6 million. These are financing and operating flows, not valuation; the US$27.3 billion market value on the 10-K cover excludes affiliates' holdings and is not total market capitalization.

Bankruptcies and failures were less central than in 2001-2002, but balance-sheet repair remained important. Airlines still faced fuel-cost and competition pressure. In Russia, the effective dismantling of Yukos's private control over core oil assets was a major corporate governance and state-capitalism event. U.S. insured banks and thrifts reported fourth-quarter net income of US$31.8 billion, though fewer than half of institutions reached a 1 percent return on assets.

### 5C. Innovation & technology

The biggest innovation shift was commercial rather than purely scientific: internet search advertising became a public-market growth story. Broadband diffusion, mobile telephony, enterprise software, semiconductors and outsourcing changed firm organization and consumer behavior. India's IT services sector and Korea's broadband ecosystem showed how human capital and telecom infrastructure translated into exportable services and digital consumption.

R&D-intensive sectors included pharmaceuticals, semiconductors, autos, aerospace and telecom equipment. China expanded manufacturing process capability; Japan and Germany retained strengths in precision engineering; the United States dominated software, internet platforms, biotech finance and venture ecosystems. ### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Sectoral employment shifted toward services in advanced economies and toward construction, manufacturing and urban services in fast-growing emerging economies. Wage growth was uneven: skilled technology, finance and engineering workers benefited, while tradable low-skill manufacturing faced pressure from global competition.

### 6C. Currencies

The U.S. dollar weakened against the euro and several commodity-linked currencies. In WDI official annual averages, the euro averaged EUR 0.804 per dollar (about US$1.24 per euro; units per dollar down 9.1 percent from 2003) and traded higher late in the year, sterling GBP 0.546 (down 10.8 percent), the yen JPY 108.2 (down 6.7 percent), the Canadian dollar CAD 1.301 (down 7.1 percent) and the Australian dollar AUD 1.360 (down 11.8 percent). The renminbi remained pegged around 8.28 per dollar, reinforcing debate over China's external surplus and reserve accumulation; the NBS year-end quotation of 8.2765 is a separate endpoint from the annual average. The Mexican peso was the exception among the top-20 currencies shown, with units per dollar up 4.6 percent. The Canadian and Australian dollars gained support from commodities. The WDI series for Turkey is expressed in the lira unit introduced in 2005, not the million-plus-lira quotations of 2004. A fall in local currency units per dollar indicates a stronger local currency; it is not by itself an investor return.

Capital controls and managed regimes remained important in China, India, Russia and several emerging economies. The global system was therefore mixed: freely floating G7 currencies, managed Asian exchange rates, and commodity currencies responding to terms-of-trade gains. Euro-area members already shared the euro, but the new EU members of May 2004 did not adopt it on accession.

### 6D. Alternative assets

Gold rose and averaged around US$410 per troy ounce, supported by dollar weakness and investor diversification. Real estate was economically important in the United States, United Kingdom, Spain, Australia and China. In 2004 these were mostly interpreted as wealth and construction supports; with hindsight, several markets were accumulating financial fragility.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

Active risks in 2004 included oil-price escalation, U.S. external deficits, China overheating, geopolitical risk around Iraq, euro-area fiscal-rule strains, Japan's deflation exit risk, and institutional uncertainty in Russia. The Indian Ocean tsunami of 26 December 2004 was a humanitarian catastrophe with major local economic consequences, especially for tourism, fisheries, housing and infrastructure in affected economies such as Indonesia, Sri Lanka, Maldives and Thailand. In Indonesia, outside the nominal top 20, the tsunami devastated parts of northern Sumatra in the final week of a year in which national real growth was 5.927 percent on the MPD series and 5.031 percent in the IMF WEO; annual national output is not a damage valuation or a measure of household welfare losses. In Thailand, which raised its policy rate three times during the year, the tourism losses in affected provinces mainly concerned 2005 expectations rather than 2004 outcomes.

Oil exporters outside the top 20 were also exposed to vintage and policy uncertainty. Iran's oil earnings supported domestic demand, but monetary expansion and CPI inflation of 14.762 percent in WDI data constrained the meaning of nominal gains; the IMF's September 2004 Article IV discussion largely described 2003/04, and its banking, privatization and business-climate agenda was a reform programme, not completed reform. Saudi Arabia's January 2005 IMF notice set privatization, private investment and employment of Saudi nationals as priorities and projected growth rather than reporting a completed 2004 fiscal outturn.

Near-misses included the possibility that oil prices would choke off the global recovery, that China's tightening would cause a hard landing, or that U.S. rate increases would break housing and credit momentum. None of those risks materialized in 2004, but they did not disappear.

Hindsight-labeled vulnerabilities were substantial. The U.S. housing and mortgage-credit boom was becoming more fragile. Global imbalances were increasingly reliant on foreign official and private demand for dollar assets. European divergence was masked by low euro-area rates and sovereign-spread compression. Commodity exporters became more dependent on high prices. In Russia, state control over strategic assets increased in ways that would matter for later investment risk. Strong asset values and benign reported loan performance did not establish that every borrower or bank was safe.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (2004 vs. 2003)

The most important shift from 2003 to 2004 was the move from recovery to boom. World trade accelerated sharply, global output growth reached a multi-decade high, and corporate profits improved across many sectors. In the IMF's retrospective data, PPP-weighted world growth rose from 3.802 to 5.266 percent and goods-and-services trade volume growth from 5.738 to 10.850 percent. China's demand became more visibly central to commodity and capital-goods markets.

Policy also changed. In 2003, the United States was still in emergency-easing mode; in 2004, the Federal Reserve began tightening, raising the funds target by 1.25 percentage points in five steps. The Bank of England was already concerned enough about domestic demand to raise rates. The ECB and BOJ remained accommodative, creating an uneven but still easy global liquidity environment. The shift also concerned instruments: the Bank of Japan's reserve-quantity expansion and the People's Bank of China's commercial-rate changes are not equivalent to the Federal Reserve's funds-target increases.

Commodity prices moved from supportive to strategically important. Oil rose enough to affect inflation, terms of trade, fiscal balances and geopolitical bargaining power; Brent rose from about US$28.83 to US$38.26 per barrel and LME copper by 60.9 percent. Russia, Brazil, Canada and Australia gained, while oil importers faced pressure.

Europe's year-over-year delta was structural: the EU expanded from 15 to 25 members. That did not instantly transform GDP rankings, but it changed investment maps, labor-market expectations and long-run integration. Spain's boom accelerated, Germany's export model strengthened, and Italy's competitiveness problems became more apparent.

The corporate delta was that post-dot-com technology regained public-market credibility. Google's IPO did not yet make it a top global company by revenue, but it changed investor perception of internet platforms. Telecom consolidation and bank consolidation also showed that scale was a major strategic response to the early-2000s cycle.

The ranking delta was limited. Turkey appears in the 2004 current-dollar top 20 and Taiwan, which is not published in the WITS/WDI country series, does not. On the alternative MPD 2023 basis, Saudi Arabia would be in the top twenty instead of Taiwan, which reflects cohort movement rather than a territorial discontinuity. China's preliminary 9.5 percent growth, the MPD's 8.034 percent and the current IMF WEO's 10.115 percent show why vintage differences should not be narrated as economic shocks.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

High-confidence data: nominal GDP rankings and current-dollar GDP values for the top 20 are high confidence when using World Bank WITS/WDI, with IMF WEO as a cross-check. Global GDP, world trade growth, central-bank policy decisions in the United States, euro area, United Kingdom and Japan, and Brent oil prices are also high confidence. Ranking levels are current U.S. dollars at market exchange rates; the MPD 2023 levels in the country headers are in constant 2011 international dollars and the IMF WEO PPP figures are in current international dollars, so the three are comparisons only and are never mixed. Government ratios use their own nominal denominators and are never computed from PPP levels.

Medium-confidence data: real GDP growth, inflation, current-account balances, exchange rates and broad fiscal aggregates are generally reliable but may differ by vintage and revision. Some figures are calendar-year estimates, while India and some fiscal indicators use fiscal-year reporting: the IMF WEO maps India's April-March year and Iran's fiscal year to the first calendar year, Australian fiscal ratios are calendarized by averaging adjacent fiscal years, and Thailand's fiscal metadata use October-September. Government debt can mean gross, net, central-government or general-government debt depending on source; Saudi and Iranian WEO government data represent central government. Chinese fiscal methods include staff adjustments, and later treatments of local-government financing vehicles cannot simply be backdated to 2004.

Lower-confidence or non-comparable data: unemployment is the weakest cross-country indicator. China's official urban unemployment is not comparable with OECD unemployment. India's annual unemployment is not cleanly comparable for 2004, and the ILO modeled rates in section 4 are estimates rather than national survey results. Mexico, Brazil and Turkey have large informal sectors. Russia's transition statistics improved by 2004 but still require caution. Budget balances for emerging economies can differ materially by central-government, public-sector and general-government definitions. Policy-rate values for Japan, Mexico, Russia, Turkey, Switzerland and Sweden are approximate settings or framework descriptions rather than dated decision records; for Switzerland and Sweden, for the Netherlands and Belgium other than the ECB policy rate and the WDI euro exchange rate, and for Korean debt and fiscal balance, the indicators are approximate estimates not taken from the IMF WEO.

GDP ranking conflicts: World Bank WITS/WDI and IMF WEO nominal GDP series can produce small ranking differences near the cutoff because of revisions, exchange-rate treatment and statistical-vintage differences. The top-20 membership is based on World Bank WITS/WDI current U.S. dollar GDP. The closest ranks are Mexico vs Korea, Turkey vs Switzerland, and Sweden vs Belgium. The apparent change against 2003, Turkey in and Taiwan out, partly reflects country coverage rather than economic performance. On the MPD 2023 basis in 2011 international dollars, and equally on the IMF WEO current-PPP basis, Indonesia, Iran, Thailand and Saudi Arabia would be in the top twenty instead of the Netherlands, Switzerland, Sweden and Belgium; those levels appear in the country headers only as comparisons.

Contemporary releases and later reconstructions are distinct. The NBS communique of February 2005 labels all figures preliminary, excludes Hong Kong, Macao and Taiwan and separates current-price values from comparable-price growth. The Saudi IMF notice of January 2005 has a numerical table ending in 2003 and projects 2004 in its prose; Iran's September 2004 consultation largely covers 2003/04. The BIS 2005 Annual Report includes early-2005 observations alongside 2004 results, so Shanghai price growth in early 2005 and new mortgage restrictions are not treated as 2004 events, and the Federal Reserve's fourth-quarter 2004 household-obligations figure was a projection in February 2005. The Australian household-credit growth figures cover partial-year windows.

**Conflicting data.** Real growth estimates differ materially by source and vintage: China 9.5 percent (NBS preliminary), 8.034 percent (MPD), 10.115 percent (IMF WEO) and 10.140 percent (WDI); Russia 9.614 percent (MPD) against 7.145 percent (IMF WEO) and 7.200 percent (WDI); Germany 2.223 percent (MPD) against 1.170 percent (IMF WEO); Mexico 5.080 percent (MPD) against 3.565 percent (IMF WEO); Brazil 7.009 percent (MPD) against 5.760 percent (IMF WEO); Saudi Arabia 13.767 percent (MPD) against 8.631 percent (IMF WEO) and the IMF's January 2005 expectation of about 5 percent; Iran 8.907 percent (MPD) against 4.337 percent (IMF WEO). World real growth was about 5.1 percent in the IMF's April 2005 briefing, 5.266 percent (PPP weights) and 4.024 percent (market weights) in the IMF WEO of October 2026, and 4.495 percent in WDI. World nominal GDP is about US$44.18 trillion in the World Bank WITS/WDI World Trade Summary 2004, US$44.26 trillion in the October 2026 WDI release of the same series, and US$44,427.5 billion in the IMF WEO. Population levels in the ranking table are WDI-style estimates and differ from MPD populations, for example India 1,122.0 million against 1,087.9 million, China 1,296.1 against 1,290.6 million, Brazil 184.0 against 186.2 million and Turkey 67.9 against 69.4 million. Equity returns differ by index and definition: the S&P 500 total return of about 10.9 percent in the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston review compares with a 12.771 percent U.S. total return in JST Release 6, and the Nikkei 225 price rise of about 7.6 percent with a 23.916 percent capital gain in the JST Japan series. Brazil's December 2004 inflation report dates the Copom meeting to 14-15 December in its opening and 15-16 December in section 6.3, while agreeing on the 17.75 percent setting. Iran's WEO fiscal metadata give an April-March reporting period while the methodology describes a 21 March-20 March year. Agreement between WDI and IMF series is not independent corroboration, since both draw on national sources.

The equity comparators in section 6A are taken from the Jorda-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database, Release 6, and are used under the CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 license.

Many microeconomic claims are therefore presented qualitatively and grounded in corporate records, central-bank reports, IMF Article IV reports and sector sources rather than a single harmonized global dataset.
