# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1952

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1952 was a transition year from the Korea-war commodity boom into a more disciplined, more policy-managed postwar expansion. The world economy was still growing, but the shape of growth changed sharply. The panic buying, inventory accumulation, and raw-material price spikes of 1950-1951 had largely reversed. By 1952, wholesale prices were falling in many countries, while consumer prices were more stable. The dominant economic fact was not a global recession, but a shift from inflationary scarcity to controlled expansion under rearmament, exchange controls, and Bretton Woods balance-of-payments constraints.

Using the Maddison Project Database 2023 as the ranking base, the largest economies in 1952 were the United States, the Soviet Union, China, the United Kingdom, and Germany. The figures below are not current-dollar GDP. They are reconstructed real output estimates in 2011 international dollars, derived from Maddison GDP per capita multiplied by population. This choice is deliberate: current-dollar exchange rates in 1952 were fixed, managed, or nonconvertible for many economies, and nominal dollar GDP would understate large low-price economies such as China and India while overstating some convertible-currency economies. For Soviet-bloc and early People's Republic of China data, uncertainty is high.

The United States remained the unquestioned center of the monetary and production system. Its output was almost three times the MPD-estimated size of the Soviet economy and more than four times the size of China. But the year's most important shifts were outside the United States. West Germany and Japan were entering their high-growth reconstruction phases. China's economy recovered strongly from the civil-war period under new state control. India's First Five-Year Plan moved from planning into implementation. Argentina, once one of Latin America's strongest economies, contracted under agricultural weakness and policy strain, while Brazil and Mexico continued import-substitution industrialization.

The microeconomic picture was uneven. Defense demand, steel, coal, machinery, chemicals, public works, and transport remained supported by Cold War spending and reconstruction. Textiles, wool, rubber, and wood pulp were hit by the reversal of the 1950-1951 commodity cycle. Consumers in high-income countries benefited from less acute goods scarcity, but still faced controls, taxes, rationing residues, or import restrictions in several countries. In lower-income and newly independent economies, growth was shaped less by consumer choice than by land reform, infrastructure bottlenecks, food supply, exchange allocation, and the state-led selection of priority industries.

Sectoral divergence was visible in observed data. Italy's textile exports and Scandinavian pulp production contracted even as West German engineering exports expanded, and Argentina experienced a severe agricultural and foreign-exchange squeeze. Monetary adjustment was not uniformly tightening: Britain raised Bank Rate to 4% on 11 March, while Belgium, the Netherlands and West Germany reduced official discount rates during the year. The Federal Reserve held its discount rate at 1.75%, but removed selected consumer-credit and housing-credit restrictions. Controls on imports, capital movements and access to exchange remained central, and parallel-market quotations differed from official parities.

At the household and firm level, the timing of transactions matters. U.S. consumer-credit growth followed the relaxation of controls; Argentine export tonnage fell sharply after the poor 1951/52 agricultural season; Mexican irrigation projects and public investment supported activity despite slower production growth. IBM's computer announcement generated orders, not completed installations. Brazil's new development bank was an institutional commitment, not a particular volume of disbursements. The ECSC's 1952 institutional beginning is distinct from the opening of its common markets in 1953.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1952

**1. The Korean War commodity boom unwound.** The outbreak of the Korean War had produced emergency buying, inventory accumulation, and a sharp rise in raw-material prices. By 1952 that process had reversed. BIS commodity indexes show a major fall from the 1951 peak, especially in textiles and several industrial raw materials. The economic significance is that 1952 proved inflation could be contained without ending postwar growth. Countries most affected included the United States, United Kingdom, Belgium, Australia, Canada, Brazil, Indonesia, and Argentina. The forward link was the stabilization phase of 1953-1954, when policymakers became more confident that rearmament and reconstruction could proceed without the same commodity-price panic.

**2. Rearmament became a structural demand source.** Military expenditure connected to the Korean War and Cold War was a continuing burden for the United States, the United Kingdom, France, the Soviet Union, China, Japan through procurement, and many NATO-related economies. The effect was not only public spending. It shaped steel, machinery, shipbuilding, aircraft, electronics, chemicals, transport, and government finance. French military budget expenditure rose from 806 to a provisional 1,081 billion old francs, and U.S. military-assistance shipments counted in merchandise exports; grants, appropriations and procurement orders describe different stages of expenditure and cannot be added together. The forward link was a durable military-industrial demand structure that continued into the mid-1950s and helped anchor parts of the U.S., Soviet, British, French, and Japanese industrial base.

**3. Bretton Woods worked, but only with controls.** Most major currencies were tied to the dollar or to official par values, while the dollar itself was anchored to gold at the official monetary price. Yet convertibility remained incomplete. The dollar shortage, sterling pressure, capital controls, import restrictions, and the European Payments Union were central to daily economic management. The United Kingdom's 1951-1952 tightening, including the Bank of England's move to a 4 percent Bank Rate, showed how exchange confidence could dominate domestic credit policy. The forward link was the gradual move toward European current-account convertibility in 1958.

**4. Reconstruction growth diverged sharply.** Germany and Japan grew much faster than the United Kingdom and some older industrial economies. The reasons included rebuilding from lower bases, productivity catch-up, disciplined labor markets, export orientation, state-guided credit, and strong external demand. The ECSC entered into force in 1952, making coal and steel integration a concrete institutional reality in Western Europe. The forward link was the 1950s growth acceleration often called the Wirtschaftswunder in West Germany and the beginning of Japan's high-growth era.

**5. Development planning became mainstream.** India was implementing the First Five-Year Plan. China was consolidating state control before its First Five-Year Plan of 1953. Brazil and Mexico pursued import substitution. Indonesia was building a postcolonial economic state while facing volatile rubber and commodity markets. In these economies, the key micro mechanism was allocation: foreign exchange, credit, land, imports, public works, and food policy mattered as much as market prices. Development institutions did not guarantee completed investment: BNDE was created in June, India's final First Plan report was presented to Parliament in December although the plan period began in 1951, and Mexico reported works begun separately from works completed. The forward link was the spread of planning, development banks, import licensing, and state-owned enterprise models across Asia, Latin America, and Africa.

**6. Socialist-bloc statistics require special caution.** The Soviet Union, China, and Poland were among the largest economies by reconstructed real output, but their national accounting systems were not directly comparable with Western GDP. Output targets, material-product accounting, administered prices, and restricted disclosure make precision impossible. Their 1952 positions are economically important, but the GDP estimates should be treated as broad size indicators, not as official measured GDP in a modern sense.

**7. Corporate capitalism was still national and industrial.** The largest firms were concentrated in automobiles, oil, steel, chemicals, railways, utilities, banking, shipping, consumer goods, and electrical machinery. Truly global company rankings by revenue or market capitalization are not available in a consistent source for 1952. The most important corporate trend was not digital platform scale, but large vertically integrated firms operating within national policy regimes, import controls, procurement contracts, and regulated finance.

**8. Inventory correction transmitted upstream.** Weaker packaging demand and stock reduction affected paper converters, pulp mills and forestry suppliers. FAO's 1953 review reports world pulp production of 36.2 million metric tons against 37.0 million in 1951, and exports of 5.20 against 6.25 million. The contraction was sharper in export-dependent northern Europe than in North America. This is sector-specific information, not a world industrial-production index.

**9. External adjustment redistributed demand.** Britain's import compression and overseas sterling-area restrictions hurt consumer-goods exporters. West German and Dutch export volumes nevertheless rose about 8% and 6%, respectively, while Sweden's fell 11%. Import licensing and changing inventories, not simply exchange parity, help explain these differences.

**10. Stabilization permitted selective easing.** Reserve recovery and lower commodity pressure allowed continental discount-rate reductions. France retained quantitative rediscount limits and exemptions. Policy instruments therefore differed even when national inflation declined.

**11. Recovery coexisted with rationing of opportunity.** U.S. households acquired more credit and durable goods, while export-dependent workers faced reduced hours or mill closures elsewhere. Brazil's statutory wage adjustment became effective in January, but a decree does not establish actual earnings or enforcement. Unemployment percentages omit much rural underemployment and are not comparable across statistical systems.

**12. Statistical geography is an economic issue.** Belgium-Luxembourg trade, French-franc-area accounts, West German budgets and combined-Germany GDP do not share a perimeter. Soviet gross industrial-output growth is not GDP growth. These distinctions change conclusions rather than merely adding footnotes.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The monetary environment in 1952 was restrictive compared with the immediate post-Korean-boom period. The United States was operating after the 1951 Treasury-Federal Reserve Accord, and the Federal Reserve discount rate remained at 1.75 percent during 1952. The United Kingdom made a more dramatic move: the Bank of England discount rate was raised to 4 percent in March 1952, part of a policy response to sterling pressure and credit expansion. France had already moved the Bank of France discount rate to 4 percent in late 1951 and relied heavily on quantitative credit control.

The exchange-rate regime was Bretton Woods: fixed or managed par values, widespread exchange restrictions, incomplete convertibility, and extensive capital controls. Sterling remained at $2.80 per pound. Japan's yen was fixed at 360 per dollar. The Deutsche Mark was around 4.20 per dollar. Italy's lira was around 625 per dollar. France's official dollar rate was stable around 350 old francs per dollar. Canada was the major exception among large economies, operating with a floating rate after abandoning its previous narrow parity commitment.

Equity-market, bond-market, and yield-curve data are not consistently available across all major countries for 1952. The best-supported global claim is qualitative: monetary policy was no longer uniformly cheap, long-term government yields had generally moved upward in several countries compared with the Korean-boom period, and governments remained heavily involved in credit allocation.

**U.S. credit controls.** The Federal Reserve's discount-rate increase to 2% belongs to January 1953. Regulation W consumer-instalment controls were suspended on 7 May, statutory authority ended on 30 June, and Regulation X real-estate credit restrictions were suspended on 16 September after the prescribed housing-starts test. Equity margin levels remained 75% throughout 1952; the reduction to 50% occurred in February 1953. These dates show selective relaxation rather than simultaneous general monetary easing.

**Continental easing.** Bank Rate in Britain rose from 2.5% to 4%. By contrast, the Bank deutscher Länder reduced its rate from 6% to 5% on 29 May and to 4.5% on 21 August; the Nederlandsche Bank went from 4% to 3.5% on 22 January and 3% on 1 August; Belgium cut from 3.25% to 3% on 18 December. Long rates and equity returns did not move uniformly. Short-rate series in the country tables include bills, lending and money-market instruments and are not all policy rates.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The Korean War and the Cold War directly shaped trade. East-West trade was restricted by security concerns, embargoes, and political separation. Western Europe traded within a payments system still designed to economize on dollars. The European Payments Union remained central to intra-European settlement. In Western Europe, 1952 also marked a major institutional shift: the European Coal and Steel Community entered into force, integrating coal and steel sectors in Belgium, France, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, and West Germany.

Trade liberalization was selective. Governments wanted exports, investment goods, and raw materials, but protected reserves with licensing and import controls. The GATT system existed, but it did not yet produce the kind of broad global liberalization associated with later decades. In many developing economies, foreign exchange allocation was a key instrument of industrial policy.

**Sterling-area and ECSC timing.** The sterling-area adjustment reached intra-area trade as well as dollar imports. BIS describes an Australian restriction on specified consumer-goods import allocations to 20% of their 1950/51 base value; this is a quota rule, not an observed 80% fall in total imports. French restrictions suspended liberalization, while continental exporters faced different product mixes. The ECSC existed institutionally in 1952, but the coal, iron-ore and scrap common market opened on 10 February 1953, and steel on 1 May 1953. Western Europe's nominal exports to the eastern bloc fell from USD 683 million to USD 660 million and imports from USD 924 million to USD 901 million.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Commodity markets were the clearest real-economy shock of 1952. Textile fibers fell sharply after the Korean War buying surge. FAO reported textile recession in woollen and worsted branches, weaker mill demand, and inventory overhang. Natural rubber prices declined in early 1952 as U.S. stockpile purchases fell and import restrictions changed, with prices stabilizing around the pre-Korean level by midyear. Wood pulp prices fell sharply outside North America, and some Scandinavian pulp mills temporarily closed.

Agriculture remained regionally diverse. North America had favorable crop prospects, especially U.S. wheat and maize, while Canadian livestock was affected by foot-and-mouth disease. Argentina suffered from weather and policy problems that reduced agricultural output and exports. Food supply remained a central issue for India, China, Indonesia, and much of the developing world.

Energy was coal-centered in Europe and still increasingly oil-centered in North America and the Middle East. OPEC did not exist. Oil was strategically important, but the 1952 energy system was not yet the OPEC-price-shock system of the 1970s.

**Price quotations.** BIS's U.S. commodity quotations are end-month prices, not annual world benchmarks: wheat was 282 cents per bushel in April and 271 in December 1952; cotton 39 and 33 cents per pound; rubber 48 and 31 cents per pound. Copper remained 24 cents per pound in the listed 1952 observations. Crude oil was quoted at 425 cents per barrel in each listed 1952 month, without a single globally representative grade. The U.S. Energy Information Administration's domestic crude first-purchase price averaged USD 2.53 per barrel in both 1951 and 1952, unchanged, a nominal national annual average rather than an international grade quotation or spot price.

**World output estimates.** BIS's rounded world-output estimates show crude petroleum rising from 595 to 620 million metric tons, electricity from 1,055 to 1,135 billion kWh and aluminium from 1,810 to 2,030 thousand metric tons between 1951 and 1952, while steel remained 210 million tons. Natural rubber fell from 1,905 to 1,790 thousand tons, smelter copper from 3,170 to 3,150 thousand metric tons and coal from 1,520 to 1,500 million tons. Wheat rose from 6,480 to 7,235 million bushels, but wheat and sugar are crop-year figures, not calendar-year observations. These commodity aggregates are not a value-added decomposition of global GDP.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor markets were generally tight in the United States and much of Western Europe, but definitions of unemployment varied too much for clean cross-country comparison. The United States had high civilian employment and low unemployment by early-1950s standards. Western Europe had full-employment objectives, reconstruction demand, and migration from rural to urban sectors. Australia and Canada were absorbing postwar immigration. Japan had a large labor reserve moving into export and industrial sectors. India, China, Indonesia, Brazil, and Mexico were characterized by rural underemployment, urbanization, and low measured formal unemployment relative to the true labor-market challenge.

Demographically, the postwar baby boom was reshaping North America, Australia, and parts of Western Europe. In lower-income economies, population growth was accelerating faster than industrial job creation. This made food supply, schooling, public health, and urban infrastructure central economic issues even when GDP growth was positive.

**Labor detail.** The UN population increase of about 47.2 million created demands on housing, schooling and food, but population levels do not measure labor-force participation. The Federal Reserve reports U.S. year-end nonfarm employment 1.2 million above a year earlier and unemployment of about 1.4 million at year-end; neither is an annual unemployment rate. U.S. manufacturing hourly earnings rose approximately 6% and weekly earnings from about USD 67 to USD 72, while annual personal saving was nearly 8% of disposable income. In contrast, FAO describes reduced hours and closures in northern European pulp mills. Comparable national union-density, educational-attainment and migration data are not available.

**Economic narrative:**

The U.S. economy combined strong industrial capacity with tighter monetary conditions than in the early Korean-boom period. The end of the most intense commodity panic helped stabilize prices, while defense procurement, housing, automobiles, electrical equipment, and consumer durables supported demand. The Federal Reserve's post-Accord position gave monetary policy more independence from Treasury financing needs than during the World War II and immediate postwar period.

The MPD expansion is distinct from the BEA real-GDP series: BEA real GDP rose from 2,656.320 to 2,764.803 billion chained 2017 dollars, or 4.084%, against MPD's 3.740%, and BEA nominal GDP was USD 367.341 billion. Different benchmarks and vintages explain why one should not overwrite the other.

The Federal Reserve's contemporary account describes a steel-strike interruption followed by recovery after the strike ended in late July. Its production index reached 235 at year-end (1935-39=100), compared with about 220 at end-1951. Fourth-quarter retail sales were 8% above the corresponding 1951 quarter. Those endpoints and quarters are not annual growth rates. Housing starts were about 1.1 million, similar to 1951, and real-estate credit restrictions were lifted in September.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Automobiles, steel, petroleum, electrical equipment, chemicals, construction, and agriculture were central sectors. Corporate America was dominated by large integrated firms such as General Motors, Standard Oil companies, U.S. Steel, DuPont, AT&T, and major banks, but a consistent global ranking by 1952 market value or revenue is not available in the reviewed sources. Household balance sheets were improving, but consumer credit and mortgage finance were still shaped by regulation and postwar housing demand. Manufacturing and consumer durables recovered late in the year, while paper and board demand weakened. Instalment debt outstanding rose USD 3.9 billion between 30 April and 31 December from a USD 14.7 billion starting stock, a change in the stock rather than annual credit origination. IBM announced its 701 computer in April and received government and defense orders in May; a 1952 delivery count is not available. Aggregate corporate profits were about USD 40 billion before tax and USD 17 billion after tax. Bank capital, company M&A and IPO totals and national household wealth are not available.

**Economic narrative:**

The Soviet economy in 1952 was a heavy-industry and defense-centered planned economy. Output was directed toward steel, coal, machinery, transport, energy, and military-related production. Agriculture remained less dynamic and continued to suffer from structural inefficiencies associated with collectivization, procurement quotas, and weak rural incentives.

The former-USSR territorial reconstruction is not a contemporary Soviet GDP release. BIS reports official increases of 11% in gross industrial output, investment and national income in 1952. These were separate Soviet measures with administered prices, not three corroborating estimates of modern GDP. The investment table mixes planned and actual figures, so its 143.1 billion rouble total is not a measure of realized capital formation.

Household saving was partly mobilized through state loans. The May 1952 loan obtained subscriptions of 36 billion roubles against a 30 billion target. Repayments, compulsory subscription practices and access to consumption matter for welfare. The retail price reductions described in April 1953 belong to 1953, not 1952.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Sectoral performance was driven by plan priorities, not market profitability. Large state enterprises, ministries, and combines controlled output. Consumer goods were secondary to heavy industry. The economic data should be read as order-of-magnitude estimates rather than directly observed GDP. Because state enterprises rather than freely traded corporate groups organized industrial expansion, a global revenue or market-capitalization rank is inappropriate. Housing prices, bank solvency, enterprise profitability and household distribution are not available. Finland's completion of reparation deliveries in September 1952 marked an institutional change in Soviet foreign trade.

### 5B. Corporate landscape

Data not available for a consistent global top 10 or top 20 companies by revenue or market capitalization in 1952. The corporate landscape was dominated by national industrial champions and large integrated firms rather than by globally comparable listed-company rankings. Important corporate clusters included U.S. automobiles and oil, British and Dutch consumer goods and petroleum groups, German chemicals and machinery, Japanese bank-centered industrial groups, Italian state-linked industry, and state enterprises in socialist economies.

Major M&A, IPO, bankruptcy, and privatization data are not available in a globally consistent form for 1952. The bigger economic event was institutional rather than corporate: the ECSC created a supranational framework for coal and steel in Western Europe. In planned economies, the closest analog to corporate events was administrative reorganization of ministries, combines, and state enterprises.

**Named company cases.** IBM supplies an announcement and order milestone; Volkswagen supplies company sales (135,941 vehicles) and a foreign-subsidiary entry in Canada; Toyota supplies physical production (14,106 vehicles); BOAC and de Havilland supply a route-service inauguration; and BNDE supplies a statutory institutional change. These heterogeneous observations cannot be ordered into a revenue or capitalization table. General Motors, U.S. Steel, AT&T, major oil companies, IRI and large European manufacturers remain relevant examples without 1952 financial amounts. Aggregate U.S. corporate profits were about USD 40 billion before tax and USD 17 billion after tax.

### 5C. Innovation & technology

1952 was an early year in the commercialization of electronic computing and jet aviation. IBM announced the 701, its first commercial scientific computer. The de Havilland Comet entered commercial service with BOAC, beginning scheduled jet passenger operations. These innovations did not yet dominate GDP, but they marked the direction of later productivity change: electronics, data processing, aerospace, and high-speed transport.

R&D spending data by country are not available in a consistent 1952 global series in the reviewed sources. The most important R&D channels were defense procurement, aircraft and electronics programs, chemical and pharmaceutical laboratories, university science, and state-funded engineering.

**Infrastructure.** Mexico's report distinguishes initiated, continuing and completed irrigation works, which is more informative than describing a plan allocation as finished infrastructure. India's multi-year development plan and Brazil's bank creation established administrative capacity and priorities, not completed 1952 projects. National R&D/GDP ratios and patent counts are not available for the twenty economies.

### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

The global labor story was sectoral reallocation. In high-income economies, labor moved into manufacturing, construction, services, and regulated utilities. In Japan, Germany, and Italy, surplus labor and reconstruction demand supported fast industrial expansion. In India, China, Indonesia, Brazil, Mexico, and Argentina, rural employment remained dominant, while urban industry grew from a much smaller base.

Union power was significant in many industrial economies, especially the United States, United Kingdom, Australia, Sweden, Belgium, and parts of Western Europe. In socialist economies, labor organization was subordinated to the state and plan. Education and technical training were becoming more important, but comparable human-capital statistics for 1952 are limited.

**Wages and hours.** U.S. hourly and weekly earnings, Argentine agricultural purchasing-power stress, northern European mill short-time, and Brazil's statutory wage effective date describe different labor outcomes. A wage bill can rise because employment or hours increase, so it is not necessarily an average wage. Import quotas and credit restrictions also affected household choice independently of CPI. Planning-system employment guarantees and low recorded unemployment do not rule out hidden unemployment. Comparable sectoral employment shares, schooling, union density and migration data are not available.

### 6C. Currencies

The main currency story was not floating exchange-rate movement, but restricted convertibility. Sterling stayed fixed at $2.80 per pound, the yen at 360 per dollar, the lira near 625 per dollar, the Deutsche Mark near 4.20 per dollar, the Swedish krona at 5.17321 per dollar, and many other currencies at official parities. Canada was the major large-economy exception with a floating rate. The dollar shortage and reserve adequacy remained more important than daily spot-market volatility.

**Parallel and multiple rates.** Official parities and actual access to foreign exchange differed. No currency-return portfolio is constructed from mixed annual-average, endpoint and parallel-market quotations.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The active macro risk in 1952 was that the Korean War and Cold War rearmament would reignite inflation and external imbalances. That risk did not fully materialize in 1952 because commodity prices had already reversed and monetary policy had tightened in major economies. The year therefore reduced one risk while exposing another: governments could contain inflation, but only with controls, high taxes, credit restraint, import restrictions, and pressure on private investment.

The sterling and dollar-shortage problem remained a systemic vulnerability. The United Kingdom's 1951-1952 policy tightening helped restore confidence, but the underlying issue of reserve adequacy and convertibility persisted. A premature move to full convertibility could have produced reserve losses and import compression.

Commodity exporters faced a different risk: the reversal of Korean-boom prices. Australia, Indonesia, Brazil, Argentina, Canada, and Scandinavian pulp exporters all had sectors exposed to falling raw-material prices. Argentina's contraction showed how agricultural weakness could quickly become macro weakness.

Hindsight risks include the fragility of Bretton Woods under asymmetric U.S. dollar centrality, the long-run consumer-goods weakness of Soviet-style planning, the political economy of state-led industrialization, and the dependence of Japan and Germany on export expansion and external demand. These were visible only partly in 1952; their full significance became clearer later.

**Realized stresses.** Britain's midyear reserve trough, Argentina's export collapse and the northern European pulp downturn were realized 1952 stresses, not only hypothetical vulnerabilities. The U.S. steel strike was a supply interruption inside an expanding annual economy. There was no synchronized world banking crisis. Tightened access to credit affected British personal borrowers much more than engineering firms; agricultural weakness constrained Argentina's import capacity; and new exchange structures redistributed costs among Indonesian importers. Administrative price stability can coexist with limited availability and nonprice rationing, so low CPI does not by itself show stable household welfare.

**Hindsight boundary.** The 1953 North Sea flood, the later Comet structural failures, the eventual Bretton Woods breakdown and long-run planning-system weaknesses are not 1952 events. India's plan, BNDE's formation and the ECSC institutions created possibilities rather than predetermined successful outcomes.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1952 vs. 1951)

1951 was the peak of the Korean War commodity panic. 1952 was the disinflation and adjustment year. The most concrete shift was in commodity prices: BIS's total world-market commodity index fell from 147 in March 1951 to 106 in March 1952 and 100 by May 1952. This changed the terms of trade, inventories, and profits for textiles, rubber, pulp, metals, and agricultural exporters.

Monetary policy was tighter in 1952 than in the early Korean-boom period. The United States held the discount rate at 1.75 percent during the year after the policy shift of 1951. The United Kingdom made a sharper move, reaching a 4 percent Bank Rate in March 1952. France had also moved to a 4 percent discount rate and relied on credit controls. The broad policy delta was from emergency accommodation toward anti-inflation and balance-of-payments defense.

The GDP-ranking delta was limited in membership but meaningful in order. The top 20 did not change under the MPD method. China moved ahead of the United Kingdom because of strong rebound growth and the UK's stagnation. Brazil moved ahead of Argentina as Argentina contracted. Spain moved ahead of Mexico. Poland moved ahead of Australia. Sweden moved ahead of Belgium.

Institutionally, 1952 was more European and more planned than 1951. The ECSC entered into force. India was implementing the First Five-Year Plan. China was preparing the First Five-Year Plan. Soviet-bloc planning continued to emphasize heavy industry. The development-state model became more normal across the non-U.S. world.

**Matched changes.** In the UN 1955 series, limited-coverage world export quantum fell 1.053% and dollar export unit values fell 2.778%. Argentina's shipment tonnage fell 47.46%, Italian textile export value fell 46.23%, Dutch import volume declined about 12% and West German export volume rose about 8%; world pulp exports fell 16.8% from 6.25 to 5.20 million tons. U.S. annual-average CPI inflation slowed from 7.9% to 1.9%, a 6.0 percentage-point deceleration, while British annual inflation increased to 10.65%. Britain's year-end Bank Rate rose 1.5 points, while Belgium's fell 0.25 point, the Netherlands' 1 point and West Germany's 1.5 points. The Brazilian minimum-wage tables became effective on 1 January, BNDE was created on 20 June and the Indian final plan report was tabled on 8 December; the ECSC common markets and the Federal Reserve's discount-rate rise belong to 1953.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

Highest confidence applies to broad monetary and commodity claims from IMF, BIS, FAO, Federal Reserve, and official historical sources. The U.S. CPI, Federal Reserve discount rate, Bank of England rate change, BIS commodity-index movement, and FAO commodity descriptions are relatively well supported. Clearly dated institutional events and labelled original table cells are also high confidence, although this does not make them economically complete. BIS and central-bank reports sometimes reproduce UN or national statistical sources, so repeated publication within one lineage is not independent corroboration. Argentine central-bank export values and Banco de México 1952 accounts are preliminary.

Medium confidence applies to MPD-derived GDP rankings for market economies with stronger historical national accounts, such as the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, France, Italy, Netherlands, Sweden, Belgium, Australia, and Japan. These estimates are still reconstructed real-output measures, not contemporaneous official GDP figures in 2011 international dollars.

Lower confidence applies to the Soviet Union, China, Poland, Indonesia, India, Brazil, Argentina, Mexico, and Spain. The reasons differ: non-market accounting in socialist economies, incomplete statistical systems in developing economies, changing territorial definitions, multiple exchange rates, price controls, and weak labor-market measurement. Soviet-bloc and early PRC GDP estimates should be treated as broad estimates with possible error of about plus or minus 20 to 30 percent. MPD, the Macrohistory Database and the UN population estimates are retrospective reconstructions without published uncertainty intervals for these annual country estimates.

Major gaps: consistent 1952 unemployment rates, government debt-to-GDP ratios, current-account-to-GDP ratios, budget balances, equity index annual returns, long-term bond yields, sectoral employment shares, corporate revenue rankings, market capitalization rankings, real-estate price indexes, and household balance-sheet data are not available for all top 20 economies in the reviewed sources. Where unavailable, the report states "Data not available" rather than guessing. Current-account and fiscal ratios are withheld where territory, accounting concept, currency conversion or fiscal/calendar timing is unresolved, which matters particularly for Belgium, France, Germany, Sweden and Italy. No nominal balance is divided by PPP output. Poland, whole Pakistan, dependencies, company rankings, national R&D and patents, education, union density, migration, bank asset quality, household wealth, official exchange-index levels and comparable 10-year yields remain incomplete.

GDP-ranking conflict: MPD real-output rankings and nominal current-dollar rankings differ because 1952 exchange rates were fixed, controlled, or nonconvertible. CountryEconomy current-dollar figures provide a useful nominal cross-check for selected countries, but this assessment does not use nominal GDP for the top-20 ranking. The ranking table is therefore a historical real-output ranking, not a market-exchange-rate ranking. World GDP and growth are Data not available as certified totals: the indicative figures in section 3A are sums over MPD territorial records; excluding identified whole-record overlaps does not create a certified disjoint partition of the world, all historical sovereign states, colonial coverage or consistent historical borders, so they are not certified world aggregates. The UN world population is a separately published aggregate, not a sum of the MPD profiles.

Output measures diverge across sources. MPD gives U.S. growth of 3.74% against BEA real GDP growth of 4.084%, and Mexico's MPD 3.82% contrasts with Banco de México's real production growth of 1.2%. Contemporary BIS current-account figures for the United Kingdom, the Netherlands and Sweden differ from later reconstructions; each vintage is kept separate. Country CPI, debt, unemployment, short-rate and exchange-rate definitions in the Macrohistory Database differ by country, and parallel-market exchange rates are kept separate from official parities. Macrohistory Database (JST) values are used under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 and HSSO city values under CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 France; these terms apply to those values and are not replaced by the project's general license.

**East and West Germany.** The combined MPD header does not erase political division. West German trade with the eastern zone fell sharply after the unimplemented September 1951 Berlin agreement and recovered somewhat later in 1952; BIS reports 1952 West German imports from eastern Germany of DM 80 million against DM 123 million in 1951. This is a bilateral merchandise flow, not East German GDP. Eastern budgets, currency, employment and enterprise accounts are not available, and western observations are not extended eastward.

**Russia and the Soviet aggregate.** The separate Russian reconstruction in MPD would rank sixth before overlap adjustment, but it is not a 1952 sovereign state beside the USSR and is not summed into the ranking. A Russia-specific household or industrial estimate cannot be inferred by taking a fixed share of union statistics.

**Yugoslavia and its constituent records.** Yugoslavia lies outside the top twenty. Serbia, Croatia, Slovenia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, North Macedonia and Montenegro are excluded wherever the aggregate is present. BIS gives 1952 imports of 111 billion dinars, comprising 81 billion commercial and 30 billion aid-financed, against 116 billion total in 1951, while exports rose from 54 to 74 billion; the 1952 figures are partly estimated. Drought raised food-import needs. The official rate of 300 dinars per dollar introduced at the beginning of 1952 was supplemented by coefficients and retention arrangements.

**Whole Pakistan.** The 1952 state included eastern and western wings. Present-day Pakistan and Bangladesh reconstructions are not substituted for a whole-state GDP estimate. UN data label Pakistan directly and give manufacturing indices of 53 in 1951 and 68 in 1952 (1953=100), a 28.302% increase from a small industrial base; this is an industrial index, not GDP.

**Mainland China, Taiwan and Hong Kong.** The China record does not combine separately administered economies. Taiwan and colonial Hong Kong require their own trade, monetary and household series, and their ports, migration and commercial intermediation followed different mechanisms from mainland land reform and planning. Annual 1952 values for them are not available here.

**Korea and dependencies.** War destruction, military procurement, displacement and relief make a normal sovereign GDP ranking particularly misleading for Korea in 1952; South and North require separate output and relief accounts. Malaya's rubber economy, the Belgian Congo, French overseas territories and other dependencies also mattered to commodity and payments networks. The franc-area and Belgium-Luxembourg cases show why colonial and monetary-area perimeters must be recorded.
