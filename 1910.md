# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1910

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The world economy of 1910 sat near the high point of the first age of globalization. The dominant macro regime was the classical gold standard, with sterling and London finance at the center of global payments, securities issuance, and long-distance capital allocation. International migration, ocean shipping, railways, telegraph networks, and commodity exports tied together economies that were still overwhelmingly agricultural in employment terms but increasingly industrial in output, trade, and finance. In the Federico-Tena World Trade Historical Database (current borders), world export volume rose 6.36% and nominal world exports 10.90%, to USD 15,391.5 million; these are merchandise-export aggregates, not a world GDP growth estimate.

The largest economy in this ranking was the United States, followed by China, the Russian Empire territorial unit, Germany, India, and the United Kingdom. This ranking looks different from modern rankings because population-heavy agrarian economies were still enormous in aggregate GDP, even when GDP per person was low. The United States and Germany represented the frontier of large-scale industrial growth, while Britain remained the financial and maritime center. China and India remained vast agrarian economies with low per capita output. Russia combined agrarian scale with rapid but uneven industrialization. Domestic performance was uneven: in the Maddison Project Database 2023 reconstruction, real GDP grew 8.55% in Canada, 7.28% in Argentina and 6.97% in Australia, against 0.43% in the United States, -6.12% in France and -4.55% in Spain. These annual estimates remain much less secure than modern national accounts.

Several 1910 events revealed stress under the surface of the prewar global order. The Mexican Revolution began in November, exposing the political fragility beneath Mexico's export and railway boom. Japan formally annexed Korea, extending its imperial economic system, although the annexation declaration preserved existing customs and tonnage duties for ten years rather than creating an immediate customs union. Qing China was under pressure from fiscal, railway, currency, and foreign loan disputes that would intensify in 1911. Russia's land reforms and industrial expansion were still underway, but they did not resolve the empire's deeper political and social vulnerabilities. States also changed the rules of finance and transport: the U.S. Mann-Elkins Act strengthened railroad-rate regulation and brought interstate telecommunications under it, postal savings was authorized to begin in 1911, Britain finally enacted the disputed Finance Act in April, and Australia moved banknote control to the Commonwealth Treasury.

At the micro level, 1910 was defined by railways, coal, steel, textiles, shipping, plantation agriculture, mining, petroleum, and the early spread of automobiles and electrification. Modern consumer durables were emerging, but for much of the world the key economic questions were still land, harvests, transport access, credit, taxation, and commodity prices. Crude-steel output increased in the United States, Germany, Britain, France, Belgium and Japan in the comparative tables of the Belgian statistical yearbook, so France's measured GDP decline was not an across-the-board industrial collapse; the January Seine flood was a severe local shock whose national cost has not been isolated. Rising coffee, cotton and petroleum quotations alongside falling wheat, coal and copper benchmarks rule out a single global inflation story. From hindsight, 1910 looks like a peak of integration before the shocks of the 1910s broke the gold standard world and redirected economic history.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1910

**Theme 1: The late classical gold standard as a global monetary anchor.** The gold standard provided the monetary framework for much of world trade and capital flow. Major currencies were linked by gold parities, exchange rates among gold-standard economies were comparatively stable, and central banks prioritized convertibility. The system mattered because it reduced exchange risk and helped long-term capital move from Britain, France, Germany, the Netherlands, and other surplus centers to settler economies, railways, ports, public debt markets, and resource projects. Current-account reconstructions show Britain (+7.82% of GDP in the Bank of England series) and France (+3.95%) as surplus economies and Canada (-11.94%) and Japan (-1.85%) as deficit economies. The forward linkage is negative as well as positive: the same fixed-rate discipline that supported integration before World War I constrained crisis response and would collapse under wartime finance.

**Theme 2: London finance and sterling still mattered more than British industrial size.** Britain was no longer the largest producer, but it remained the central node of global financial intermediation. London merchant banks, discount houses, insurance markets, and securities listings made sterling the main international currency of the period. This affected Canada, Argentina, Australia, India, South Africa, Russia, and many colonial economies through bond markets and infrastructure finance. The forward link is the gradual shift from sterling-centered finance toward a larger U.S. role, accelerated by World War I but not yet complete in 1910.

**Theme 3: Industrial catch-up and corporate scale.** The United States and Germany were the clearest cases of large-scale industrial dynamism. Steel, chemicals, electrical machinery, oil, railroads, and mass manufacturing produced a new corporate landscape. Crude steel rose 13.77% in Germany, 15.37% in France and 66.34% in Japan, the last from a much smaller base. Hitachi's first induction motor and the formation of A.L.F.A. in Milan mark particular new capabilities. Large enterprises were increasingly central to productivity and market power, creating political pressure for regulation, antitrust action, banking reform, and labor organization. The forward link is the twentieth-century rise of managerial capitalism, mass production, and state regulation.

**Theme 4: Agrarian pressure beneath aggregate growth.** Many of the largest economies were agrarian in employment and social structure. China, India, Russia, Mexico, Indonesia, and large parts of Europe depended on land systems that were often unequal, regionally fragmented, and vulnerable to crop and price shocks. In Mexico, rural land concentration and political exclusion helped turn economic tension into revolution. In Russia and China, land, taxation, and transport disputes interacted with political weakness. Reconstructed real consumption per person declined in France, Italy and Mexico. The forward linkage is the wave of revolutions, reforms, and nationalist movements that followed in the 1910s and 1920s.

**Theme 5: Imperial and colonial economies as growth mechanisms and data problems.** A large share of global output was organized through empires or colonial rule. British India, the Dutch East Indies, Korea under Japanese annexation, and parts of Africa and Southeast Asia were not sovereign national economies, yet they were central to trade, commodities, and capital flows. This creates a data problem: modern GDP tables often force later national borders onto earlier imperial structures, and British Indian fiscal, banking and industrial statistics, like Dutch colonial banking and Java sugar data, cover different territories and observation periods. It also creates an analytical problem: aggregate output does not indicate who controlled the surplus.

**Theme 6: Technology diffusion through infrastructure.** Railways, steamships, telegraph cables, electricity, urban water systems, oil refining, internal combustion engines, and industrial chemistry were the practical technologies changing prices, firms, and labor demand. The automobile was still early but economically important, especially in the United States. Electrification and chemicals had broader industrial effects in Germany, the United States, Britain, and Japan. The forward link is the productivity acceleration of mass production, motor transport, and electric power in the decades after 1910.

**Theme 7: Migration, urbanization, and labor organization.** The first globalization moved people as well as goods and money. European migration to the Americas and Australasia increased labor supply in Argentina, Canada, the United States, and Australia; the United States admitted 1,041,570 immigrant aliens in fiscal year 1910. Urbanization expanded factory labor markets and consumer markets, but also created labor conflict over wages, safety, hours, and political representation. The forward link is the rise of labor parties, unions, social insurance, and immigration restrictions in the early twentieth century.

**Theme 8: Regulation of networks, notes and savings.** U.S. railroad and telecommunications regulation under the Mann-Elkins Act, the postal-savings law, Australian note reform and the Swiss National Bank's acquisition of sole note-issuing rights on 20 June changed institutional arrangements. Legal powers and announced objectives do not establish their immediate effects on investment or financial stability.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The monetary regime was the classical gold standard. Bretton Woods did not exist. Floating exchange rates were not the norm for the main gold-standard economies. In practice, central banks and monetary authorities protected gold convertibility, and interest-rate policy was constrained by gold flows, reserves, and market confidence. Sterling-dollar parity was around $4.86 per pound, and gold parities stabilized major exchange rates within gold points; the Bank of England annual quotation was USD 4.8637 per pound, down 0.18%.

Major interest-rate environments cannot be summarized by modern policy-rate comparisons. The United States had no Federal Reserve; its 2.98% Macrohistory Database short rate is a market rate. Britain used Bank Rate under gold convertibility: it stood at 4.50% at year-end, unchanged from 1909, with an annual average of 3.72%. Germany used Reichsbank discount policy; the Macrohistory market short rates of 3.54% in Germany and 2.39% in France are not central-bank decisions. The Bank of Japan cut its head-office rate on non-collection bills from 5.84% to 5.11% on 11 January; on 7 March the collection/non-collection distinction was abolished and a 4.75% commercial-bill rate was recorded, so the annual change is not like-for-like. Many countries had no modern central bank operating framework.

Financial conditions were mixed. London remained the leading global securities and discount market. New York was larger and more important than before 1907, but the U.S. system still lacked a lender of last resort. The Dow Jones Industrial Average ended 1910 down 17.86% from year-end 1909, reflecting weak U.S. equity conditions and policy uncertainty. Major European exchanges existed in London, Paris, Berlin, Amsterdam, Brussels, Milan, and Vienna; country-portfolio returns are given in 6A. Nominal bank lending generally expanded in the Macrohistory sample, by 7.20% in Germany, 23.81% in France and 10.56% in the United States, but nominal loan growth is neither real investment growth nor evidence of healthy balance sheets.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The trade regime of 1910 was not a rules-based multilateral system like the post-1945 order. It was shaped by tariffs, imperial preference and administration, bilateral commercial relations, shipping costs, gold-standard finance, and colonial control. The United States remained a high-tariff economy after the Payne-Aldrich Tariff of 1909. In his 6 December message, President Taft reported that the maximum and minimum provisions of the 1909 tariff law took effect on 1 April 1910 and that 134 minimum-tariff proclamations covered the commercial world; he also described Canadian reciprocity negotiations held in Ottawa in November and adjourned until January 1911, so no reciprocity agreement was implemented in 1910. Britain kept a more free-trade orientation, although domestic tariff reform debates were politically important.

Geopolitical context mattered directly for capital and trade. Japan annexed Korea: the treaty was signed on 22 August and promulgated and effective on 29 August, and the accompanying declaration preserved existing import, export and tonnage duties for ten years, including on trade with Japan. Mexico's Revolution began in November. Qing China faced rising pressure over railways, fiscal reform, and foreign finance. The Austro-Hungarian, Russian, Ottoman, Japanese, British, French, Dutch, and German imperial systems shaped economic flows and data categories. The EEC and ASEAN did not exist. ### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Coal was still the dominant industrial energy source. Oil was economically important but not yet the central global energy commodity it would become later in the twentieth century. U.S. crude prices were low by later standards, and oil production was expanding in the United States, Russia, Mexico, the Dutch East Indies, and other producing regions. Electricity and oil were complementary forces in industrial modernization, not immediate replacements for coal. Coal output grew 8.85% in the United States, 22.93% in Canada and 19.21% in Australia, but only 0.25% in Britain, while it fell 6.80% in Hungary.

Metals demand was linked to railways, electrical networks, buildings, ships, arms production, and machinery. Copper benefited from electrification and telegraph or telephone systems. Steel remained central to railways, bridges, factories, ships, and urban construction. Agriculture remained the most important livelihood sector globally, with wheat, rice, cotton, sugar, coffee, rubber, tea, wool, and meat connecting farm households and plantations to world markets. ### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Global employment data are not available in a comparable 1910 series. The broad pattern is clear: agriculture still absorbed a large share of labor in most economies, while industry and services were more important in Britain, the United States, Germany, Belgium, the Netherlands, and parts of Austria-Hungary. International migration continued to reshape the labor supply in the United States, Canada, Argentina, and Australia. The U.S. Commerce report counts 1,041,570 immigrant aliens admitted in fiscal year 1910, distinct from 1,198,037 total alien arrivals; net of 380,418 alien departures, net alien movements were 817,619, up from 543,843 in fiscal year 1909. These are fiscal-year administrative classifications, not calendar migration or counts of employed workers.

Historical unemployment estimates exist for a few economies only and are not modern labor-force surveys. U.S. unemployment rose from 5.1% to 5.9% in the Macrohistory Database; the Lebergott series rises 0.7 point from 5.2%, and the two are not combined. British unemployment was 5.76% in the Bank of England series against 4.70% in the Macrohistory Database. Germany recorded 1.9%, the Netherlands 3.62%, Australia 3.3% and France 5.8%.

Urban labor markets were increasingly organized. Unions, labor parties, socialist parties, employer associations, and arbitration systems were becoming more important. Wage data cannot be compared mechanically across countries because of informal labor, household production, colonial labor systems, and inconsistent statistical definitions.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States was the largest economy in the Maddison 1910 ranking. The country combined a huge internal market with the world's leading industrial output in steel, railroads, oil, meatpacking, machinery, and mass consumer manufactures. The economy was still close enough to the 1907 financial crisis that banking reform remained a political issue, but the Federal Reserve had not yet been created. Reconstructed output shows near-stagnation (+1.02% in the Maddison 2010 series, +0.43% in MPD 2023) even though steel output grew 8.93% and coal 8.85% in the comparative official tables, while consumer prices rose 4.59% and unemployment rose to 5.9%.

The dominant domestic policy story was Progressive Era regulation. Antitrust pressure, railroad rate disputes, food and drug regulation, and debates over tariffs shaped business expectations. The Mann-Elkins Act, enacted on 18 June, strengthened railroad-rate oversight and brought interstate telegraph and telephone services within the regulatory framework, with a general 60-day commencement. The postal-savings law was enacted on 25 June but began operating in 1911. Hindsight shows that these years were also a transition from a railroad centered industrial economy toward one increasingly shaped by automobiles, electrical equipment, petroleum, and modern corporate finance.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Railroads and steel remained core, but automobiles were increasingly important after the 1908 launch of Ford's Model T. Banking was fragmented by law, with New York money markets functioning as the national liquidity hub; nominal bank lending rose 10.56%. Equity weakness in 1910 exposed how sensitive corporate valuations were to trust busting, money market stress, and policy uncertainty. Personal saving excluding consumer durables rose from USD 2.55 billion to USD 2.76 billion (+8.24% nominal) in the retrospective Goldsmith series, which includes unincorporated enterprises, nonprofit institutions and trusts. The Macrohistory nominal house-price index rose 9.56%, while Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia newspaper asking-price indices fell 1.32% in New York City and 2.93% in Boston, with asking rents up 3.12% and 0.32%. President Taft reported contracts for two U.S.-built Argentine battleships, orders rather than 1910 deliveries.

**Economic narrative:**

China ranked second by aggregate GDP because of its very large population, not because of high per capita income. The Qing Empire's GDP here is interpolated between Maddison benchmark years. The economy remained overwhelmingly agrarian, with treaty ports, regional merchant networks, handicrafts, and early modern industry operating alongside a weak late Qing fiscal and monetary apparatus.

The GDP figure here is uncertain because it is a reconstructed estimate for an empire without modern national accounts. Treaty port textiles, shipping, and banking were significant in coastal cities. Railway construction and foreign concessions linked microeconomic investment disputes to macro political instability.

**Economic narrative:**

The Russian Empire was one of the largest economies by aggregate output, with a vast agrarian base and fast growth in selected industrial regions. Its position in the ranking depends strongly on territorial reconstruction and interpolation (the Maddison unit covers the Russian Empire and related former USSR territory), so the number should be treated as a broad estimate rather than an exact national-accounting figure.

Stolypin era land reforms aimed to strengthen individual peasant proprietorship and improve agricultural productivity. Industrial growth was concentrated in railways, metals, coal, oil, textiles, and state linked capital projects, often with foreign finance. In the comparative tables of the Belgian statistical yearbook, European Russia's steel output (excluding Finland) rose 13.30% to 3,536 thousand metric tons, while coal including Finland rose 0.82%; Barro-Ursúa real consumption per person rose 4.69%. The empire looked dynamic in aggregate, but the social base of growth remained fragile.

### 5B. Corporate landscape

A precise global top 10 to 20 companies by revenue or market capitalization for 1910 is not available in a consistent source. The likely corporate giants and systemically important firms were concentrated in railways, steel, oil, banks, insurance, shipping, chemicals, electrical equipment, and colonial trading or extraction companies. U.S. Steel, Standard Oil, major U.S. railroads, Royal Dutch and Shell interests, Krupp, Siemens, AEG, BASF, Bayer, Hoechst, Mitsubishi, Mitsui, large British banks, Paris banks, Berlin universal banks, and major shipping lines were among the key corporate actors. Two documented 1910 origins stand out: Hitachi, which traces its beginnings to 1910, and A.L.F.A. (Anonima Lombarda Fabbrica Automobili), whose foundation document is dated 24 June 1910 and which later became Alfa Romeo.

The most important corporate theme was scale. Large firms could finance fixed capital, research, distribution, and international trade in ways smaller firms could not. This created productivity advantages but also political resistance. In the United States, antitrust law was increasingly central. In Germany, cartels and bank-linked groups were more institutionally accepted. In Japan, zaibatsu networks tied banking, trading, and industry to state strategy. In colonial economies, large plantation, mining, shipping, and concession firms often dominated local markets.

Major IPO data for 1910 are not available in a comparable global list, and a census of mergers, bankruptcies and nationalizations is Data not available. The Swiss banking episode illustrates the measurement problem: the Swiss National Bank's history counts 83 bank exits over 1906-1914, which cannot be reported as a calendar-year 1910 bankruptcy total. The most important institutional changes were not single transactions but the growth of regulated corporate capitalism, colonial concession systems, and infrastructure finance.

### 5C. Innovation & technology

The most economically important technologies were already visible before 1910 but still spreading: electric power, steel processes, industrial chemistry, internal combustion engines, rail logistics, steam shipping, telegraph and telephone communication, refrigeration, and improved agricultural machinery. The Ford Model T, introduced in 1908, was still early in its production history but signaled the coming importance of affordable automobiles and mass production. Hitachi's first 5 hp induction motor of 1910 is a concrete electrical-engineering event.

Germany was especially strong in chemicals and electrical equipment. The United States was strong in oil, steel, machinery, electricity, agricultural equipment, and automobiles. Britain remained strong in shipbuilding, finance, marine insurance, and trade services. Japan was absorbing and adapting industrial technologies at high speed. R&D spending data by country are not available in a consistent 1910 global series, and patent statistics require specialist reconstruction.

Infrastructure megaprojects were economically central. Railways in Canada, Argentina, India, Russia, China, Mexico, and colonial Southeast Asia shaped land values, crop choices, settlement, migration, and state revenue. Ports, canals, telegraph cables, and urban utilities linked local production to global markets. The Dutch colonial report provides a measurable telecommunications extension: government interlocal telephone route length in the Dutch East Indies rose about 68%, from 697.04 to 1,170.99 km, while wire length rose from 1,394.08 to 2,819.32 km.

### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

The global labor market was divided between industrial wage labor, agrarian household labor, plantation labor, informal urban work, and colonial labor systems. Education levels were rising in industrial economies and Japan, but literacy and schooling gaps remained large in China, India, much of Russia, Mexico, and colonial economies. Human capital data are therefore highly uneven. British Indian factory returns distinguish output from employment: from FY 1909-10 to FY 1910-11 cotton-mill employment fell from 215,950 to 214,149, whereas jute-mill employment rose from 204,104 to 216,390; since 1906-07 the cotton statistics cover British India only, unlike earlier totals including Native States and French India.

Migration had major economic effects. European migrants expanded the labor force and consumer base in the United States, Canada, Argentina, and Australia; U.S. net alien movements rose to 817,619 in fiscal year 1910 from 543,843. Emigration also affected Italy, Spain, and parts of Eastern Europe through remittances and labor-market relief. Labor movements were more organized in industrial economies, while rural labor conflicts and land struggles were central in agrarian economies. ### 6C. Currencies

The central currency fact of 1910 was gold-standard stability among core economies. The pound-dollar parity was about $4.86 per pound; the Bank of England annual quotation was USD 4.8637, down 0.18%. Sterling was the dominant international currency because of trade invoicing, bills of exchange, London finance, and Britain's global commercial position. The U.S. dollar was important, but the United States did not yet have a central bank and had not displaced sterling. The yen's December average demand-draft rate was 49.416 USD per 100 yen, or 2.0236 yen per USD.

Not all countries fit the gold-standard core. China had silver-linked and fragmented monetary practices. Colonial currencies were tied to imperial systems; the rupee's Council bills averaged 16.041 pence in FY 1909-10. Mexico had adopted gold-related reforms, and its annual market series fell from 2.28 to 2.25 pesos per USD, but political instability from late 1910 raised economic risk. Capital controls in the modern sense were not the central global issue; convertibility, gold reserves, and market access were. Quantified reserve composition is Data not available.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The most visible crisis of 1910 was Mexico's Revolution, which began in November. It transformed political risk, transport security, land relations, banking expectations, and foreign investment. At the time, observers could see unrest and regime opposition; Madero's call for an uprising on 20 November followed his arrest before the June election. With hindsight, the Revolution became one of the defining events of twentieth-century Latin American economic history; Díaz's fall belongs to 1911.

The United States faced equity market weakness and lingering institutional vulnerabilities from the 1907 panic. Because the Federal Reserve did not yet exist, liquidity still depended on private clearinghouses, Treasury actions, and New York money markets. Hindsight labels this as one reason U.S. banking reform became urgent before 1913.

China was another major risk center. Late Qing reforms tried to modernize railways, currency, administration, and military capacity, but fiscal weakness and disputes over railway finance created political strain. Hindsight connects these tensions to the 1911 Revolution, but the precise timing and scale were not knowable from 1910 data alone.

The Paris flood of January was a realized physical shock: the Seine peaked at 8.62 metres on 28 January, damaging buildings and Metro infrastructure, but a national loss estimate has not been isolated. Swiss banking stress is a classification conflict: the Macrohistory Database codes 1910 as a systemic crisis year, while the Swiss National Bank's history describes small-bank difficulties across 1906-1914 and limited contemporary systemic concern.

The largest hidden vulnerability was the first globalization itself. The gold standard, cross-border capital, and trade integration looked robust in 1910. Gold-linked exchange constraints, large external financing needs such as Canada's 11.94%-of-GDP current-account deficit, and expanding credit were contemporaneously identifiable exposures. Hindsight shows that war finance, capital controls, inflation, debt, and political breakdown could dismantle the system quickly. This is a hindsight judgment, not something that 1910 market prices fully revealed.

Other risks included crop failures, commodity price shocks, labor unrest, imperial conflict, sovereign debt stress among peripheral borrowers, and naval rivalry among great powers. Not every risk materialized in 1910, but the decade after 1910 would make several of them central.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1910 vs. 1909)

The top twenty set did not change from 1909 to 1910 under the Maddison method used here. The ordering changed. The Russian Empire territorial unit moved above Germany and India, but this is partly an interpolation result and should not be overstated. Germany edged above India. Indonesia moved above Spain. Argentina moved above Czechoslovakia and Mexico. Australia moved above the Netherlands and Austria.

World GDP growth is estimated at about +2.5%. World export volume rose 6.36% and nominal export value 10.90%; using the value change as volume would overstate expansion. Among the top twenty, Maddison annual values show strong growth for Canada, Argentina, Australia, Indonesia, the Netherlands, Germany, and the United Kingdom. France, Italy, and Spain show negative annual movements in the Maddison 2010 series; in the Maddison Project Database 2023, Italy grows 1.07% and Mexico contracts 0.16%, differences between vintages rather than structural breaks. These annual values are useful signals, but they should not be interpreted as fully modern quarterly national accounts.

U.S. unemployment rose 0.8 percentage point in the Macrohistory Database, while British unemployment fell 1.72 points in the Bank of England series; definitions differ, so levels are not directly comparable. U.S. consumer prices swung from -1.81% to +4.59%, and Italian and Japanese prices also turned from decline to increase. The Bank of Japan's January rate cut is comparable within the old facility, but the March category change interrupts the annual comparison. India's FY 1909-10 revenue account moved from deficit to surplus, and Javasche Bank assets rose 18.16% while Dutch East Indies postal savings balances rose 8.61%.

Politically, 1910 was more eventful than 1909. Japan's annexation of Korea and the start of the Mexican Revolution were major regime events. Qing China's reform tensions were closer to breaking point. In the United States, financial and antitrust uncertainty weighed on equity markets, and the Mann-Elkins Act and postal-savings law were enacted. In Britain, fiscal and constitutional debates around taxation and the welfare state signaled a changing policy environment, and the 1909 budget finally became law in April. Australia moved note issue to the Commonwealth Treasury.

Structurally, the same broad forces continued: gold-standard integration, London-led capital exports, industrial growth in the United States and Germany, colonial commodity extraction, migration to settler economies, and urban labor organization. The year did not yet mark the end of the old order, but it showed the political strains that would make the old order vulnerable.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

**Highest confidence data in this document:** the Maddison 2010 GDP, population, and GDP per capita values where annual observations exist; the official U.S. crude oil first purchase price series; the fixed U.S. official gold price; sterling-dollar gold parity; dated statutes and official institutional chronologies; original colonial finance, savings, mill and mineral returns; the comparative industrial quantities of the Belgian statistical yearbook; and original U.S. immigration counts. Their precision should not be confused with comprehensive coverage; administrative categories, boundaries and fiscal years still matter.

**Medium confidence data:** the country narratives, sectoral patterns, and financial-market descriptions, the Dow Jones 1910 year-end level and return, and the reconstructed series: Maddison Project Database 2023 growth rates, Macrohistory Database consumer prices, credit, government ratios and asset returns, Barro-Ursúa consumption, and Federico-Tena trade and population. Maddison, Barro-Ursúa and Macrohistory series often share underlying historical accounts, so agreement among them is not independent corroboration. Claims about capital flows, migration, gold-standard operation, and trade integration are well supported at the period level, but not always measurable exactly for calendar year 1910.

**Lowest confidence data:** GDP levels for China and the Russian Empire territorial unit, because the 1910 values are interpolated between benchmark years. Colonial and reconstructed territories also require caution. Poland and Czechoslovakia were not sovereign states. India, Indonesia, Austria, and Korea-related claims are shaped by imperial or colonial data categories. For Soviet bloc and developing-economy historical GDP estimates, a broad uncertainty range of plus or minus 20 to 30 percent is appropriate.

**Ranking and vintage conflicts:** The ranking keeps the Maddison 2010 levels in 1990 international dollars. The Maddison Project Database 2023, in 2011 international dollars, has usable 1910 records for 45 territories but none compatible for China, the Russian Empire or the Ottoman Empire; ordering its available records puts the United Kingdom and India above Germany, the United Kingdom above India, and Australia, the Netherlands and Austria above Mexico, and includes Switzerland and a separate Hungary among the largest twenty, while Poland, Czechoslovakia and Hungary have no matched 1909 record. In that vintage India and Germany differ by only 0.0063% of GDP, far below historical measurement precision. Growth rates also differ between vintages: Italy is -3.63% in Maddison 2010 against +1.07% in MPD 2023, Mexico +0.83% against -0.16%, Spain -3.50% against -4.55%, Japan +1.58% against +0.62%, the United States +1.02% against +0.43%, and the Netherlands +4.57% against +4.10%. The two vintages are not spliced; each growth figure is labelled with its source.

**Conflicting series:** UK Bank of England and Macrohistory alternatives are not reconciled: inflation 2.12% against 0.90%, unemployment 5.76% against 4.70%, debt 33.30% against 34.51% of GDP, and current account 7.82% against 8.19%. U.S. unemployment is 5.9% in the Macrohistory Database, with the Lebergott series rising 0.7 point from 5.2%. Spanish real consumption per person falls 7.53% in the Barro-Ursúa workbook and 9.55% in the Macrohistory consumption series. The Macrohistory current-account column for Belgium in this period is a merchandise trade balance. Fiscal balances from the Macrohistory Database, other than the separately defined U.S. federal measure, are provisional central-government flows pending reconciliation of fiscal scope, not modern general-government balances. Market short rates are not official discount rates and are shown only in Notes for countries with central banks.

**Major missing metrics:** comparable CPI inflation, unemployment rates, government debt/GDP, budget balances and current accounts outside the Macrohistory and Bank of England samples; official discount rates for most continental central banks; exchange-level equity index levels for most markets; complete yield curves and credit spreads; household debt and saving rates; national real estate prices; corporate revenues, mergers, IPOs and failures; patents and R&D; healthcare output; and sectoral employment for all top twenty economies. A complete independently corroborated world top 20 and world GDP for 1910 are not established.

**Where sources may conflict:** Maddison 2010 uses 1990 international Geary-Khamis dollars, while Maddison Project 2023 and Our World in Data presentations often use 2011 international dollars. Rankings can shift when using different base years, borders, interpolation methods, and territorial definitions. This document prioritizes a transparent 1910 ranking from the Maddison 2010 horizontal workbook because it gives usable annual and benchmark values around 1910, then cross-checks the broad picture against the later Maddison Project family. The Maddison Project's United Kingdom includes the whole of Ireland through 1920, its Germany uses 1870 frontiers through 1918, and its India record is undivided India dated 1 October.
