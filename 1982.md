# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1982

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1982 was the year in which the late-1970s inflationary system broke, but at the cost of one of the deepest synchronized downturns in the postwar era. World GDP in current US dollars was about US$11.7 trillion, while real world output grew only about 0.37 percent. The OECD/high-income core was essentially flat or contracting, with the United States, Canada, West Germany, the Netherlands, Switzerland, Saudi Arabia, and Nigeria all recording real GDP declines. The recession was not evenly distributed: China and India continued to expand, Iran rebounded from prior war and revolution disruptions, and Japan grew modestly, but the dominant global signal was disinflation through recession. Lower inflation did not mean immediate relief for workers or borrowers: US unemployment reached 10.8% in December, against an annual-average rate of about 9.7%, and goods-producing employment carried most of the payroll contraction.

The defining policy force was restrictive monetary policy, especially in the United States. The Volcker-era anti-inflation regime helped bring US CPI inflation down from double digits in 1981 to about 6.1 percent in 1982, but it also pushed unemployment and credit stress higher. By mid-1982 the Federal Reserve began allowing rates to fall sharply as recession intensified, with the federal funds rate moving from roughly 14.5 percent at the end of June to around 9 percent by late August. The Board approved seven half-point reductions in the basic discount rate between July and December, from 12% to 8.5%. That pivot helped set the stage for a major equity market turn, but it did not immediately repair the real economy.

The biggest shock outside the industrial core was the international debt crisis. Mexico's external financing crisis in August 1982 made clear that the combination of high dollar rates, a strong US dollar, weak commodity prices, and heavy foreign-currency borrowing had become systemic. The crisis was not one undifferentiated default: foreign-exchange rationing, repayment of dollar deposits in pesos, a request to postpone principal while continuing interest, bridge finance, bank nationalization and IMF negotiations occurred on different dates. Mexico's annual-average CPI increase was 58.9% in Banco de México's report, while 98.8% was the December-to-December rate, and a trade surplus coexisted with a current-account deficit because financial-service payments absorbed the goods surplus. The crisis quickly affected Latin America and also exposed vulnerabilities in Eastern Europe, especially Poland. The direction of international capital flows shifted away from easy syndicated bank lending to emergency official support, restructuring, and austerity programs; the BIS estimate of net international bank lending fell from $165 billion to $95 billion. Stabilization was unfinished at year-end: the Mexican commercial-bank restructuring took effect only in March 1983.

Commodity and energy markets also changed direction. Oil remained historically expensive but no longer looked scarce: demand weakness, conservation, and non-OPEC supply growth pushed the market toward the early-1980s oil glut. Commodity weakness hurt exporters such as Mexico, Nigeria, Saudi Arabia, Brazil, Indonesia and Chile, while helping reduce inflation in importing countries. Gold averaged around US$376 per troy ounce in 1982, far below the panic highs of 1980 but still high by pre-1970s standards. The downturn was not universal in its causes either: China's official communiqué described a strong harvest and industrial expansion, India faced a poor monsoon and industrial bottlenecks, and Australia's recession coincided with drought.

For historical GDP ranking, this assessment uses World Bank World Development Indicators current-US-dollar GDP for market economies, cross-checked against the World Bank Atlas 1985 GNP table and against Maddison Project Database 2023 real GDP in 2011 international dollars. The Soviet Union is not available in WDI GDP current US dollars, so it is inserted using an explicitly uncertain UN/Maddison-style non-market estimate derived from UNdata per-capita GDP and population estimates. Soviet bloc figures should be treated as rough approximations, with plausible error on the order of plus or minus 20 to 30 percent. Contemporary World Bank estimates and current IMF histories sometimes differ substantially; neither publication date nor numerical precision settles such conflicts.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1982

**1. Disinflation through recession.** The most important macroeconomic theme was the sharp fall in inflation expectations and measured inflation in the advanced economies. In the United States, CPI inflation slowed to about 6.1 percent from 10.3 percent in 1981. Japan's CPI inflation was about 2.7 percent, West Germany's about 5.2 percent, France's about 12.0 percent, Italy's about 16.5 percent, and the United Kingdom's about 8.6 percent. The disinflation was historically significant because it marked a transition away from the stop-go, wage-price, and oil-shock inflation of the 1970s toward a regime in which central banks would defend price stability more explicitly.

The cost was high. Real GDP contracted in the United States, Canada, West Germany, the Netherlands, Switzerland, Saudi Arabia, and Nigeria, while world growth slowed to near zero. Manufacturing, construction, mining, and housing-sensitive sectors bore the heaviest burdens: US nonfarm payrolls fell 2.267 million between the fourth quarters of 1981 and 1982, of which 2.056 million were goods-producing jobs. The payroll and unemployment figures identify who bore the contraction; they do not identify one exclusive causal contribution from monetary policy, energy prices or wages. The forward linkage was decisive: the credibility gained by anti-inflation policy in 1982 shaped the 1980s recovery, but the social and political costs helped intensify debates over unemployment, union power, industrial policy, and financial liberalization.

**2. The Latin American debt crisis becomes systemic.** The second defining theme was the external debt shock that crystallized with Mexico in August 1982. The crisis was not a single-country liquidity problem. It reflected a broader structure: petrodollar recycling had encouraged large bank lending to developing countries; many debts were dollar denominated and floating-rate; the dollar appreciated strongly from 1980 to 1982; and export prices weakened just as interest costs rose. Short maturities made continued rollover essential, and Mexico's negotiations exposed banks' dependence on other lenders continuing to participate. Mexico's debt crisis and bank nationalization in September 1982 turned creditor anxiety into a systemic emerging-market funding stop. Bridge loans and conditional longer-term commitments addressed different liquidity horizons; they did not erase the underlying debt.

The countries most affected in 1982 included Mexico, Brazil, Argentina, Chile, and several other Latin American borrowers; the BIS places the Argentine shock before Mexico's August emergency. The shock also mattered for the United States and European banks, which had significant exposures to sovereign and quasi-sovereign borrowers. The forward linkage was the lost decade of Latin American growth, IMF-supported adjustment programs, bank regulatory changes, and a new era of sovereign debt restructuring; that description is hindsight, not an outcome fully observable during 1982.

**3. Oil-market reversal and the early-1980s commodity downturn.** Energy markets in 1982 still operated under the shadow of the 1979 oil shock, but the marginal trend had turned. High prices had encouraged conservation, substitution, and non-OPEC production, while recession reduced demand. OPEC oil exporters faced lower export volumes and growing fiscal pressure. Saudi Arabia and Nigeria, both in the top 20 by 1982 GDP, recorded severe real GDP contractions in WDI data. Weaker prices and demand helped some importers while damaging oil exporters' fiscal and external finances, and oil output could expand in one producer, as in the United States and Iran, while international oil demand weakened.

This mattered historically because it showed that oil scarcity was not a permanent one-way force. The future consequence was the 1980s oil glut, culminating in the 1986 oil price collapse. For importing economies, the softer commodity environment helped disinflation. For exporters, it exposed budget structures that had been built around exceptional 1970s revenue growth.

**4. Dollar strength, managed floats, and external adjustment.** Bretton Woods fixed exchange rates were long gone, and 1982 was a managed-float world with substantial national variation: floating major currencies, the European Monetary System in Western Europe, and many developing-country pegs or managed regimes. The dollar's appreciation from 1980 to 1982 tightened global financial conditions because a large share of trade finance and external debt was dollar denominated. It also changed trade competitiveness, helping US import pressure and increasing the local-currency burden of external debts. Borrowers owing dollars and exporters earning weaker commodity receipts faced different pressures from US consumers benefiting from cheaper imports; a rise in local-currency units per dollar is a fall in that currency's dollar value, not an identical percentage loss.

Countries most affected included Mexico and Brazil through dollar liabilities, Japan and West Germany through trade competitiveness and currency policy, and European Monetary System members through internal exchange-rate strains. The forward linkage was the mid-1980s debate that ultimately led to coordinated exchange-rate diplomacy later in the decade.

**5. Industrial restructuring and labor-market stress.** 1982 made the postwar manufacturing employment model look less secure. Steel, autos, shipbuilding, textiles, and heavy engineering faced recessionary demand and structural competition. This was visible in the United States, United Kingdom, West Germany, France, Italy, Belgium, Sweden, and Canada. Unemployment statistics are not strictly comparable across countries, but the direction was clear: labor slack rose across most advanced economies. Adjustment also worked through households and balance sheets: Mexico's real bank funding contraction and falling investment, US goods-sector layoffs and Dutch bargaining over hours and wages illustrate channels hidden by GDP totals, and a nominal loan or deposit increase does not show greater real financing capacity during high inflation.

This theme mattered because recession accelerated the shift from old industrial employment toward services, electronics, finance, and flexible production systems. The microeconomic consequences were plant closures, union disputes, wage restraint agreements, and household balance-sheet stress. The forward linkage was the 1980s pattern of deindustrialization in parts of North America and Western Europe, alongside rising Japanese and East Asian manufacturing influence.

**6. Reform without convergence: China and India.** China and India were both top-20 economies by aggregate GDP in 1982, but their per-capita GDP levels were far below those of the industrial core. China grew about 9.1 percent in WDI data, reflecting early reform momentum after the late-1970s policy shift; the official communiqué reported material-product national-income growth of 7.4% and attributed the strong harvest to both production-responsibility arrangements and favorable weather. Household responsibility in agriculture, special economic zones, and light industrial experimentation were beginning to change micro incentives. India grew about 3.5 percent, still under a more controlled licensing and public-sector model but with a large agricultural base and improving food security, although a poor monsoon and power constraints weighed on the fiscal year.

The forward linkage was enormous. In 1982 neither country dominated global trade the way China later would, and neither had the financial-market centrality of the United States, Japan, or Western Europe. But the scale of their populations meant that modest institutional changes had large long-run consequences.

**7. Institutional and technological changes outlasted recession.** The AT&T settlement of 8 January and final judgment of 24 August, Sony's commercial compact-disc player introduction on 1 October and the FDA's approval of recombinant human insulin (Humulin R) on 28 October changed potential market structures. Approval, announcement and launch do not show economy-wide productivity gains during the same year, and the Dutch Wassenaar agreement of 24 November was a forward-looking institutional response rather than an already completed employment recovery.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

World GDP in current US dollars was about US$11.719 trillion in 1982. Real world GDP growth was about 0.37 percent, a near-stagnation year by postwar standards. World population was about 4.600 billion. Regional divergence was substantial: high-income economies were around recessionary conditions, East Asia and the Pacific still expanded, South Asia grew moderately, Latin America was near flat, and Sub-Saharan Africa contracted.

These composites carry the IMF's own estimates and coverage limitations. No sum of Maddison country records is used as world GDP.

Global trade volumes weakened because the industrial-country recession reduced import demand. The BIS's contemporary estimate put world merchandise-trade volume down about 2%; its volume coverage excludes centrally planned economies, although its accompanying trade-value estimate of $1,875 billion includes them. Japan's official diplomatic economic review for the period described advanced economies as stagnant, developed-country exports and imports as falling, and OPEC exports as sharply lower. The IMF also emphasized weak export markets and deteriorating terms of trade for non-oil developing countries. The trading system was increasingly vulnerable to protectionism, with pressure concentrated in steel, autos, textiles, agriculture, and politically sensitive manufacturing sectors.

Reserve-currency dynamics were dominated by the US dollar. The dollar strengthened materially from 1980 to 1982, increasing the real debt burden for dollar borrowers and reinforcing disinflationary pressure outside the United States. Capital flows reversed direction: private commercial-bank lending to emerging borrowers contracted, while IMF programs, official bridge loans, and debt rescheduling became more important. The BIS reporting-centre estimate of net international bank lending fell from $165 billion to $95 billion, an estimated net flow after interbank adjustments rather than the change in a complete global balance sheet, while net international bond finance rose from $36.5 billion to $58.5 billion; substitution in accessible markets did not imply equal access for troubled sovereign borrowers.

Commodity prices were broadly weak. Oil stayed high relative to the 1960s and early 1970s but softened from the peak conditions of 1979 to 1981. US EIA first-purchase oil prices moved from about US$30.80 per barrel in January 1982 to about US$28.02 in December 1982, and the annual average fell from $31.77 to $28.52. Gold averaged around US$376 per troy ounce, with a wide range across the year. Copper and other industrial commodities were pressured by weak manufacturing demand: the US copper producer unit value fell from $1,860 to $1,610 per metric ton. ### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

Monetary policy was restrictive at the start of the year and easier by the second half as recession deepened. In the United States, the federal funds rate fell sharply in the summer of 1982. The Federal Reserve Board approved seven half-point discount-rate reductions, on 19 July, 30 July, 13 August, 26 August, 8 October, 19 November and 13 December, moving the basic rate from 12% to 8.5%; a 13 September request was rejected. Approval dates are not automatically each Reserve Bank's effective date, and short-market rates, the discount rate and a federal-funds target are not interchangeable. The move did not represent a return to easy 1970s policy; rather, it was a recession response after inflation had started to fall. Bond yields also declined as markets began to anticipate lower inflation and weaker real activity.

The United Kingdom, West Germany, Japan, and other major economies faced the same broad tradeoff: inflation was falling, but unemployment and output weakness were rising. Germany's Bundesbank remained influential within the European Monetary System; the BIS describes cautious German easing through liquidity operations and official lending-rate reductions, while easing elsewhere was constrained by exchange-market pressure. Japan had lower inflation and continued to manage the yen in a world of dollar strength. European exchange-rate arrangements were strained by differing inflation and fiscal positions. The Macrohistory Database annual short-rate records are 12.240% for the United States, 11.380% for the United Kingdom, 8.666% for Germany and 6.935% for Japan; they measure US surplus funds, UK ordinary short lending and German and Japanese IFS money-market rates, so they are not four comparable central-bank policy rates. Credit transmission also differed by country. In Canada, the private-credit contribution to broad-money expansion fell from 33.6 to 0.2 percentage points while M2 growth fell from 27.5% to 4.2%; the contribution is not itself a credit-growth rate, which matters when attributing money deceleration to bank lending rather than other counterparts.

Equity markets were weak early in the year but turned sharply after the summer in the United States. The 1982 market bottom became historically important because it preceded a long 1980s bull market. The recovery was not a proof that the real economy was immediately healthy. It reflected falling rates, lower inflation expectations, and the belief that the policy regime had changed. Bank failures and international exposure made counterparty quality important even as market rates declined.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The Cold War framed trade and capital flows. The Soviet bloc operated largely outside normal market-economy national accounts and had its own trade networks, while Poland's debt problems showed that Eastern Europe was still vulnerable to hard-currency shortages; the BIS describes withdrawal of credit from Eastern Europe. Western sanctions and technology controls constrained East-West trade in sensitive equipment. The Falklands conflict, Polish debt problems, East-West tensions and the Iran-Iraq war formed distinct constraints on trade and finance, and the BIS places the Argentine shock before Mexico's August emergency. These contemporary observations do not show that every regional borrower had the same solvency problem.

Regional integration continued. The European Economic Community was central to Western European trade, while the European Monetary System influenced exchange-rate policy. ASEAN economies remained smaller than the top-20 group but increasingly relevant to manufacturing and trade. Latin America's external debt shock reshaped relations with the IMF, US Treasury, commercial banks, and European creditors. The GATT ministerial meeting adopted its Ministerial Declaration (document L/5424) on 29 November.

Trade disputes were most visible in autos, steel, agriculture, and textiles. Japanese export success increased pressure in the United States and Europe. Developing countries faced the opposite problem: weak demand in advanced economies, low commodity prices, and tighter finance. ### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil was the central commodity, but the 1982 story was no longer pure shortage. Demand was weakening, OPEC market power was being tested, and non-OPEC production had become more important. Saudi Arabia's real GDP contraction reflected the changing oil revenue environment, while Nigeria's contraction showed the danger of an oil-dependent fiscal and external model. The World Bank's contemporary report says the official Saudi Arabian Light marker stayed at $34 per barrel while spot quotations were lower, and it attributes part of the recovery in developing-country oil exports to Iranian production after the prior disruption.

With high nominal rates, falling inflation, and a stronger dollar, the average price was well below the 1980 spike but still historically elevated. Copper, aluminum, and other industrial metals were hit by manufacturing recession. Agricultural staples varied by crop and region; the global macro effect was less dramatic than oil and dollar finance, but food prices and import bills remained important for poorer countries. The World Bank described ample world wheat supplies alongside a poor Soviet harvest.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor markets deteriorated in most advanced economies. The United States experienced severe unemployment pressure, Canada had one of its deepest postwar downturns, and Western Europe faced a combination of cyclical unemployment and structural industrial decline. US seasonally adjusted nonfarm payroll employment averaged 90.954 million in the fourth quarter of 1981 and a preliminary 88.687 million in the fourth quarter of 1982; the 2.267 million decline included 2.056 million goods-producing jobs and 1.678 million manufacturing jobs. These are establishment job counts, not distinct unemployed people in the household survey, and annual averages, December unemployment and quarterly employment should not be mixed. Definitions varied widely, especially between market economies, corporatist economies, and socialist planned economies, so unemployment rates in this report are not treated as fully comparable.

Demographically, the world was still young and rapidly urbanizing. China had more than one billion people, India about 720 million, and the Soviet Union roughly 270 million. Urbanization, education expansion, and migration continued to shape labor supply. In microeconomic terms, households in recession countries faced tighter credit, weaker employment, and greater uncertainty, while households in reforming China saw stronger rural production incentives and rising local enterprise opportunities. China's household surveys covered 22,775 rural households in 589 counties and 9,020 worker households in 47 cities; the rural income comparison adjusted for a change in the valuation of farm products after household contracting, so it is not an unadjusted real-wage gain. ## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The main crisis that materialized was the sovereign debt crisis, with Mexico as the trigger event. The risk had been building for years through petrodollar recycling, floating-rate bank loans, and optimistic assumptions about export revenues. Hindsight shows that the system was fragile because private bank lending treated sovereign credit risk as easier to manage than it was.

**Mexico: payment and negotiation chronology.** Before August, Banco de México describes weakening oil receipts, capital outflows and increased reliance on short-term borrowing; a late-June $2.5 billion loan was still obtained, with difficulty. On 6 August dual exchange arrangements began, with a preferred rate of 49.13 old pesos per dollar for specified uses. On 13 August foreign-currency bank deposits were to be repaid in pesos and exchange trading was temporarily closed. In the third week of August, according to Banco de México, a 90-day postponement of public-debt principal was requested while interest payments continued; the IMF's retrospective history identifies official discussions on 13 August and creditor-bank talks on 20 August. The request was neither a cessation of all debt service nor a final restructuring. Exchange trading reopened on 19 August, including a rate of 69.50 pesos per dollar for specified deposit repayments, and on 30 August a $1.85 billion bridge was arranged, half through U.S. authorities and half through the BIS. Earlier oil-purchase advances and food-export guarantees were different instruments, and a guarantee is not automatically a cash disbursement.

On 1 September private-bank nationalization and generalized exchange controls were announced; they did not eliminate capital flight or guarantee that export proceeds returned through official channels, and different official market rates followed. On 20 December controlled and free exchange markets reopened under the new administration with distinct rates and a controlled daily crawl. On 23 December the IMF Executive Board approved an Extended Fund Facility commitment of SDR 3,410.6 million, approximately $3.75 billion at the contemporaneous conversion; this was not all paid in 1982, and a separate first-credit-tranche drawing described by the BIS is a different transaction. The IMF lending table records the arrangement as starting on 1 January 1983, and the commercial-bank restructuring became effective in March 1983; these later facts mark what was still unfinished on 31 December 1982. The BIS operational summary says the last bridge tranche became available in mid-December when an IMF loan had been approved, a timing that differs from the 23 December Board date and is not reconciled.

**Transmission.** Mexico's goods surplus did not eliminate the external financing gap because service payments, particularly financial services, remained large. Real deposit and funding erosion and falling capital formation explain why an improvement in the trade balance could coexist with falling productive investment. Errors and omissions are not mechanically equal to capital flight, and public-sector net borrowing is not the gross sovereign debt stock. The BIS reports Eastern European credit withdrawal and shorter maturities in major Latin American borrowing, which supports contagion through creditor behavior but does not show that every affected country was insolvent or that default was inevitable. In Argentina, the BIS describes a $500 million bridge in January 1983 while its operations chapter dates the establishment of a facility to December 1982, a sequence of announcement, availability and drawing that is not reconciled; Brazil's December bridge preceded an IMF program approved in 1983.

A second active crisis was the industrial-country recession. The United States, Canada, West Germany, and several other economies saw output contraction or stagnation. The danger was not only lost output but a political backlash against anti-inflation policy, trade openness, and labor-market adjustment.

A third risk was energy-market instability. Oil exporters still had enormous revenue, but demand weakness and excess supply made the revenue base less secure. The 1986 price collapse was not inevitable in 1982, but the conditions that made it possible were already visible.

A fourth risk was Eastern European hard-currency stress. Poland's debt and political crisis demonstrated how socialist economies could face external financial constraints despite non-market domestic systems; the BIS links Polish repayment difficulties to credit withdrawal from other Eastern European borrowers, although a regional net lending flow does not measure Poland's debt alone. The Soviet Union itself appeared powerful, but hindsight shows that productivity weakness, energy dependence, and rigid planning were major vulnerabilities.

A fifth risk was protectionism. Weak demand and job losses made import restrictions politically attractive. Autos, steel, textiles, and agriculture were especially exposed. A major protectionist spiral did not fully materialize in 1982, but the pressure shaped trade policy throughout the 1980s. The counterfactual costs of alternative policies are not quantified in the sources cited.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1982 vs. 1981)

The largest year-over-year change was the move from high inflation with tightening policy to recessionary disinflation. In 1981 many economies were still fighting inflation with high rates; in 1982 the output cost became unavoidable. US inflation fell materially, and by mid-1982 policy rates were declining: the Federal Reserve's basic discount rate fell from 12% to 8.5% within the year. In the IMF's consistent-vintage composites, world growth slowed from 2.317% to 0.754% with PPP weights, a 1.563 percentage-point decline, and from 2.279% to 0.620% with market-rate weights. US fourth-quarter nonfarm employment fell 2.267 million jobs; the annual GDP decline and the December unemployment peak measure different aspects of the deterioration.

The second major shift was the movement from latent external-debt vulnerability to open crisis. In 1981 lenders still treated many developing-country borrowers as strained but manageable. In 1982 Mexico made the systemic problem explicit. After that, capital flows to many borrowers depended on IMF programs, official coordination, and rescheduling rather than normal market access. Reported net international bank lending fell by $70 billion while net international bond finance rose from $36.5 billion to $58.5 billion, and Mexico's annual-average CPI inflation accelerated by 31 percentage points. The principal standstill, bridge financing and December conditional commitments did not settle the question of solvency; the 1983 restructuring was a later step.

The third shift was in oil and commodities. In 1981 oil-exporter power still looked durable. In 1982 demand weakness and market-share issues weakened that view; the US first-purchase crude price fell 10.23% after rising 47.15% in 1981. Saudi Arabia and Nigeria's real GDP contractions showed how fast revenue-led expansion could reverse. World trade volume in the IMF composite swung from +3.457% to -1.553%, while the BIS's differently scoped merchandise estimate showed a fall of about 2%.

The fourth shift was political and institutional. France moved closer to the limits of domestic reflation inside European exchange constraints. Spain's Socialist victory marked a new phase of democratic economic modernization. Sweden shifted toward devaluation-led competitiveness. The Netherlands reached the Wassenaar Agreement, giving wage moderation a central role in adjustment. On the Maddison basis, Iran replaced the Republic of Korea in the top 20 and Australia moved above Argentina; those constant-2011 PPP rankings cannot be compared directly with current-dollar rankings.

The fifth shift was financial-market psychology. The 1982 US equity bottom was not simply a market event; it signaled that investors believed the worst inflation phase had passed. That belief would help define the 1980s recovery, though labor markets and debt-burdened economies did not recover as quickly.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

High-confidence data: WDI current-US-dollar GDP, WDI real GDP growth, WDI population, and WDI CPI inflation for many market economies are the strongest quantitative backbone of this assessment, together with the IMF World Economic Outlook series for advanced economies, Banco de México's dated narrative and sector tables, Federal Reserve Board approval records, the BLS payroll table, the National Bureau of Statistics definitions and the original Wassenaar text. The World Bank Atlas 1985 provides a useful cross-check using 1982 GNP and Atlas conversion methodology, although GNP is not GDP and Atlas exchange-rate smoothing produces different rankings and values.

Medium-confidence data: country narratives for the United States, Japan, Western Europe, Mexico, Saudi Arabia, Nigeria, China, and India are supported by primary or institutional sources, but many microeconomic details are qualitative rather than exact. Low-confidence data: the Soviet Union and other non-market economies are the weakest part of any 1982 ranking. Official prices, exchange rates, employment measures, and output categories were not market equivalents. The Soviet estimate used here is a derived current-dollar proxy and should not be treated as directly comparable to WDI GDP. PPP-based Maddison rankings are more appropriate for real economic scale and are shown in section 4 as a cross-check; Germany's Maddison record covers the combined territory while monetary and fiscal data cover the Federal Republic. The IMF itself warns that its Iranian data before 1990 and Polish GDP before 1995 cannot be confirmed from national sources, and the German Democratic Republic lacks comparable national-account and micro data.

**Vintage differences.** The world GDP level of US$11.719 trillion and growth of 0.37% come from an earlier WDI vintage; another WDI vintage gives approximately $11.747 trillion and 0.41%, and the IMF gives $11,368.6 billion with growth of 0.754% (PPP weights) or 0.620% (market-rate weights). Population levels are taken from WDI; the Maddison records differ materially for China (1,000.3 million against 1,008.6 million), India (708.0 million against 720.3 million), Brazil (129.0 million against 126.9 million), Mexico (71.6 million against 70.4 million), Iran (42.9 million against 44.3 million) and the United States (232.2 million against 231.7 million). Mexico's current-account ratio is -0.791% in the IMF series against -3.19% in another WDI vintage; the two are not reconciled and no PPP denominator is used to force a match. Growth vintages differ for several economies, notably Iran (WDI 23.2%, MPD 15.713%) and the United Kingdom (WDI 2.1%, MPD 1.552%).

**Material conflicts.** Mexico's 98.8% inflation figure is the December-to-December rate; the annual-average CPI increase in Banco de México's report is 58.9% (IMF 59.115%), and the table uses the annual average. Banco de México's appendix tables 1 and 17 print 1980/1981 headings although their values match the 1981/1982 discussion; the CPI index pair 213.1 to 423.8 implies 98.874% and the average pair 191.1 to 303.6 implies 58.870%, consistent with the narrative distinction, so the appendix headings are not relied on. Mexico's growth is -0.2% in the original central-bank estimate, -0.1% in WDI, -0.05% in the IMF series and -0.506% in MPD. Australia's WDI growth of 3.3% conflicts with the IMF calendar-year 0.063% and MPD -0.122%, and the Reserve Bank's report covers the financial year to June 1983. China's official growth refers to national income (7.4%), the IMF gives 9.0% GDP growth and the World Bank's contemporary preliminary estimate was 4.0%; Indonesia's growth is about 4% in the contemporary World Bank estimate, 2.246% in the IMF series and -3.680% in MPD, and the measurement difference is not reconciled. India's World Bank 2.2% inflation reference is not identified as the same CPI series as the IMF's 7.736%. The Mexican and Argentine bridge-loan dates differ between the BIS operational summary and other BIS or IMF records, as described in section 7. Soviet estimates differ materially across UNdata, CIA-style, and Maddison-style approaches.
