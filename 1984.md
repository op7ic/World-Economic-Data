# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1984

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1984 was the first full year in which the early-1980s global recession gave way to a broad, though uneven, recovery. The United States was the demand engine: real GDP grew very rapidly, business investment rebounded, employment recovered, and imports surged. Japan, Canada, parts of Western Europe, and export-oriented Asian economies benefited from the same turn in the cycle. The rebound was visible in trade: GATT reported that world trade volume rose by about 9 percent in 1984, the strongest increase since the mid-1970s. GATT's first assessment, dated 14 March 1985, put world merchandise export volume growth at 9% against 2% in its 1983 comparison, with manufactures expanding faster than agricultural and mineral trade; neither merchandise trade nor GATT's commodity-production aggregate measures world GDP. Yet the recovery was not balanced. It was led by high-income industrial economies, heavily shaped by the strong US dollar, and only partially transmitted to indebted developing countries.

The dominant macroeconomic regime was disinflation combined with high real interest rates. Inflation in the major industrial economies was far below the peaks reached after the second oil shock, but monetary conditions remained restrictive by the standards of the 1970s. The Federal Reserve allowed rates to rise during part of 1984 and then eased late in the year as money growth and financial stress softened; its basic discount rate rose from 8.5% to 9% in April and fell in two late-year steps to 8%. The Bundesbank also faced the dilemma of defending the Deutsche mark while not derailing recovery, and raised its discount rate from 4% to 4.5% in June. The Bank of Japan maintained relatively stable discount-rate settings, with its official discount rate unchanged at 5%, while Japan's growth was pulled forward by exports and investment. The US fiscal deficit and high real returns attracted capital inflows, contributed to dollar appreciation, and amplified current-account divergence. A strengthening dollar made dollar-denominated debts more burdensome to borrowers whose revenues were in other currencies.

The year was also a debt-crisis year. Mexico, Brazil, Argentina, and other Latin American borrowers were no longer in acute default panic as in 1982, but they remained under IMF-supported adjustment, high inflation, import compression, and fragile banking relationships with international lenders. Finance was simultaneously expanding and rationed: international bond issuance grew while heavily indebted countries remained dependent on negotiated refinancing. Mexico's September rescheduling agreement with a bank steering committee was not the same as signed contracts, disbursement of new money, debt cancellation or a completed resolution; Brazil's reserve accumulation did not mean all borrowing was voluntary; and Argentina's December restructuring proposal remained a proposal whose implementation extended beyond the year. Recovery in Brazil and Mexico came with very high inflation and large social costs. Argentina entered democratic government under Raul Alfonsin facing external debt, fiscal stress, and accelerating prices. For developing economies, 1984 was therefore less a clean recovery than a year of adjustment under the shadow of floating-rate debt and strong-dollar financial conditions.

At the microeconomic level, 1984 was unusually important. The AT&T divestiture took effect on January 1, reshaping the US telecommunications industry. The United Kingdom privatized British Telecom in one of the first major public-utility privatizations of the postwar era; the November offer comprised 3,012 million shares at 130p, payable in instalments. Apple launched the Macintosh on 24 January at an advertised price of $2,495, Dell was founded, and Cisco was founded late in the year. These events did not dominate measured 1984 GDP, but they marked a shift toward competitive telecom, personal computing, network equipment, and equity-market privatization that would matter greatly in later decades. Chinese household surveys and industrial statistics show rising incomes, savings and consumer-durable supply, although their coverage and material-product accounting cannot be read as modern national accounts. The Continental Illinois rescue in May and the Bhopal disaster in December exposed different failures of risk control that aggregate output growth conceals.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1984

**1. A trade-led global recovery, but with the United States as the cycle leader.** The most visible macroeconomic feature of 1984 was the transition from recession repair to recovery. Industrial-country output grew strongly, with the United States and Canada leading. World trade volume rose about 9 percent, a sharp reversal from the contraction and stagnation of the early 1980s. The recovery mattered historically because it confirmed that the Volcker-era disinflation had not permanently crushed demand, but it also revealed a new imbalance: the United States absorbed imports while many other economies depended on external demand. US private investment and imports grew rapidly in the contemporary national accounts.

Countries most affected were the United States, Japan, Canada, West Germany, South Korea, Taiwan, and several ASEAN economies. Japan's recovery was export-supported and tied to the US boom. Western Europe improved, but high unemployment persisted. The forward linkage was direct: strong US demand and dollar appreciation led to widening US trade deficits and growing US-Japan and US-Europe trade tensions, setting up the 1985 Plaza Accord debate. That subsequent adjustment is a forward linkage, not an outcome already achieved in 1984.

**2. Disinflation with high real interest rates.** Inflation continued to fall in the industrial world, but the interest-rate environment stayed historically tight. US real rates were high, the dollar was strong, and variable-rate debtors faced heavy servicing burdens. The IMF noted that developing countries with large floating-rate debts were vulnerable to upward shifts in international rates. In 1984, this was not an abstract risk. It was a core part of Latin America's adjustment problem. Disinflation was also not universal: moderate CPI increases in several advanced economies coexisted with annual-average CPI inflation of 192.12% in Brazil and 65.45% in Mexico, and falling inflation rates did not restore earlier price levels or automatically reduce cash debt service.

The countries most affected were the United States, heavily indebted Latin American countries, and countries trying to stabilize currencies against the dollar. High real rates supported creditor balance sheets and helped maintain disinflation, but they constrained investment in weaker economies. The forward linkage was the continued debt-crisis management process of the mid-1980s and the eventual shift toward debt relief frameworks later in the decade.

**3. The strong-dollar world.** The dollar's strength was central to 1984. It reflected high US real yields, fiscal deficits, capital inflows, and confidence in US recovery. The strong dollar lowered dollar prices of imported goods for the United States and helped disinflation, but it also widened the US current-account deficit and pressured trade-exposed US manufacturers. For developing countries with dollar debts, it raised the real burden of repayment.

Japan, West Germany, France, the United Kingdom, Canada, and Latin American borrowers were all affected. The forward linkage was the policy reversal of 1985, when major economies coordinated to reduce dollar overvaluation. With hindsight, 1984 looks like the late stage of the dollar upswing that made the Plaza Accord politically possible.

**4. Latin American debt crisis moved from acute panic to chronic adjustment.** By 1984 the debt crisis was no longer only a liquidity panic, but it was still economically severe. Brazil, Mexico, and Argentina were negotiating with the IMF, commercial banks, and official creditors while managing high or extreme inflation. Import compression improved trade balances in some cases, but it also constrained consumption and investment. Real wages, employment quality, and public services suffered in many countries. The international credit market was divided: bond markets served stronger borrowers while debt-stressed countries depended on coordinated banks and official institutions, and rescheduling changed payment timing without necessarily being a net resource transfer or debt forgiveness.

The affected economies included Mexico, Brazil, Argentina, Venezuela, Chile, Peru, and several smaller borrowers. The forward linkage was a decade of weak growth, repeated stabilization attempts, and eventual debt restructuring. The main analytical disagreement is whether adjustment was mainly required because of domestic fiscal and monetary mistakes, because of external shocks such as US rates and commodity weakness, or because of a flawed international lending system. The best reading is that all three channels mattered.

**5. Oil-market weakness and pressure on exporters.** Oil prices remained high relative to the 1970s, but the direction of travel was downward. The IMF's 1984 oil analysis emphasized that the cumulative decline from 1981 to 1984 was material even though prices were still far above pre-1979 levels. The US crude first-purchase price averaged $25.88 per barrel against $26.19 in 1983. Non-OPEC supply, energy conservation, weak demand growth, and internal OPEC tensions reduced pricing power. Oil exporters such as Saudi Arabia, Iran, Venezuela, and Mexico faced weaker revenue conditions than at the start of the decade.

The forward linkage was the 1985-1986 oil price collapse, when the difficulty of defending prices through production restraint became much clearer. In 1984, the full collapse was not yet inevitable, but the vulnerabilities were already visible.

**6. Asian acceleration and policy differentiation.** Japan grew strongly after the recession, but the more historically consequential development was the acceleration in China and the continued industrial advance of East Asia. China recorded very rapid real GDP growth under early reform conditions, with rural decollectivization, township and village enterprises, special economic zones, and expanding external trade changing incentives. South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore, and ASEAN manufacturers continued to benefit from export markets and rising industrial capability.

The forward linkage was the shift of manufacturing gravity toward East Asia. China was not yet one of the top global economies by current-dollar GDP in the way it would become later, but 1984 showed how fast the reform economy could grow once agricultural, industrial, and trade incentives changed.

**7. Privatization, deregulation, and network-industry restructuring.** The AT&T breakup and British Telecom privatization were institutional turning points. They changed telecom from a public-utility or regulated-monopoly model toward a more competitive, capital-market-facing model. In parallel, personal computing and networking companies were moving from hobbyist or enterprise niches toward mass commercial relevance. Telecom restructuring and personal-computer launches mattered through pricing, competition and capital allocation; an offer prospectus, an order, a production count and recognized sales are different evidence categories.

The affected economies were mainly the United States and the United Kingdom, but the implications were global. The forward linkage was the 1990s telecom, internet, and IT investment cycle. These changes were microeconomic in origin, but they later affected productivity, competition policy, capital markets, and household technology adoption.

**8. Financial liberalization required operational adjustment.** 1984 was Australia's first full year after the December 1983 float of its currency; removal of the remaining bank-deposit term controls took effect on 1 August 1984 and changed competition among intermediaries. Indonesia's presidential decision of 13 January 1984 provided the legal framework for issuing Bank Indonesia certificates. These steps are distinct from earlier reforms, such as Indonesia's June 1983 banking measures, and from later foreign-bank entry, such as Australia's February 1985 decisions.

**9. Recovery concealed concentrated vulnerability.** Banking liquidity risk, chemical-process safety and indebted households could deteriorate despite expanding aggregate output. Continental Illinois required emergency support in May after energy-linked loan losses and a wholesale-funding run; the Bhopal toxic release in December was an industrial-safety and public-health disaster; and the Federal Reserve recorded household credit expanding faster than income. The contemporary evidence supports these mechanisms; the later history must not be projected backward as an inevitable sequence.

Commodity conditions were mixed. Oil remained expensive by 1970s standards but was weakening from early-1980s peaks. The World Bank Pink Sheet annual series places crude oil in the high-$20s per barrel range on average in 1984 depending on benchmark. Gold was well below the 1980 panic highs, with annual averages near the mid-$300s per troy ounce. Copper and other cyclical metals improved from recession lows but did not show a broad commodity boom. Agricultural staples varied by crop and region; there is no single global agricultural-price story for 1984.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The United States had the most important monetary-financial cycle. The federal funds rate was high by later standards, rose during part of the year, and eased late in 1984. The Federal Reserve's basic discount rate rose from 8.5% to 9% in April and fell in two late-year steps to 8%. The combination of disinflation, high nominal rates, and falling inflation meant high real rates. The US yield curve and Treasury market were volatile; long bond yields reached very high levels before the later bond bull market became clearer. Equity markets were choppy rather than euphoric, even as real output growth was strong.

The United Kingdom no longer used the old Minimum Lending Rate framework in the same way after 1981. UK market rates rose in 1984, and sterling remained vulnerable to dollar strength. The Bank of England describes a 22 November easing, then sterling weakness toward year-end; a numerical clearing-bank base-rate range for the year is not given in the sources cited. Its March 1985 account says November monetary aggregates were distorted by British Telecom privatization cash flows, so reading them as a clean credit acceleration would be unsafe, and its January 1985 emergency minimum lending rate announcement belongs to 1985, not 1984.

West Germany faced a weaker Deutsche mark against the dollar and a Bundesbank policy tradeoff between recovery and currency defense. The Bundesbank raised the discount rate from 4% to 4.5% in June; its Lombard-rate rise to 6% belongs to January 1985, not 1984. Japan maintained more stable money-market conditions, helped by low inflation, a high saving rate, and strong external demand; the Bank of Japan's official discount rate stayed at 5%, the level set on 22 October 1983 and kept until 30 January 1986. These are policy instruments; Macrohistory Database short-term market rates are not substitutes.

Exchange rates were in the managed-float world that followed the collapse of Bretton Woods. Many developing countries retained pegs, multiple exchange rates, or capital controls. Current-dollar GDP comparisons in 1984 therefore reflect exchange-rate systems as much as domestic production. This is especially important for China, the USSR, Iran, Brazil, Argentina, and oil exporters. ### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The trade environment recovered strongly, but it was not liberal and frictionless. GATT reporting described a large rise in world trade volume, with manufactured export volume up 12% in its first assessment; that is trade growth, not a ranking of national manufacturing productivity. The United States' import surge contributed to political pressure on Japan and Europe, and the contemporary BIS accounts connect strong US absorption to foreign demand and widening imbalances. Voluntary export restraints, sectoral disputes, and protectionist pressures remained important in autos, steel, textiles, and electronics. Protection pressures must be distinguished from enacted tariffs; a complete dated inventory of 1984 trade measures, sanctions and EEC or ASEAN decisions is Data not available in the sources cited.

Cold War politics shaped economic flows. The USSR and Eastern Europe remained outside the main market-economy statistical and financial system. Technology transfer controls, strategic embargoes, and restricted capital access limited East-West trade. BIS reporting-bank claims on Poland fell, but its commentary attributes part of that to write-offs or transfers to export-credit agencies, not demonstrated repayment. The EEC continued deepening its internal market and preparing for enlargement, while ASEAN remained more a cooperation framework than a full regional market; no later European accession or 1985 exchange-rate agreement is described here as a 1984 settlement. War-related oil and shipping risks around Iran and Iraq are not quantified in the sources cited.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil was the most important commodity story. OPEC producers faced lower demand growth, conservation effects from the earlier oil shocks, non-OPEC supply growth, and internal production-quota tensions. Saudi Arabia was still central to price management. Iran's oil economy was distorted by war, sanctions, and damaged infrastructure. Mexico and Venezuela depended on oil revenues while also carrying heavy external debts.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor markets improved in North America but remained weak in much of Western Europe. US unemployment fell materially from the 1982-1983 recession highs, to 7.51% in the IMF series. Canada also recovered, but unemployment remained high at 11.38%. The United Kingdom, France, Spain, Italy, the Netherlands, and West Germany had persistent structural unemployment; the IMF series show Spain at 19.94% and the United Kingdom at 11.75%. These are retrospective survey-based estimates with different definitions and are not comparable to planned-economy administrative employment claims. Youth unemployment was especially severe in parts of Europe.

Demographic trends continued to favor labor-force expansion in developing economies, especially China, India, Indonesia, Mexico, Brazil, and much of the Middle East. China was still early in the long demographic transition from high fertility to lower fertility. India had a young and growing labor force. Gulf labor migration remained economically important, though oil-sector weakness reduced the pace of some inflows. Wage growth in high-inflation Latin American economies was often negative in real terms even where nominal pay rose rapidly. Working-age population, migration and underemployment are not inferred here from total-population growth.

China's statistical communiqué reported employee real wages up 13.2%, after deflating monetary wages with the employee living-cost index; this covers employees, not all workers. Its surveyed rural per-capita net income and urban employee-household disposable resources use different populations and income concepts, and the source also reports poorer households still facing difficulty.

Global corporate rankings in 1984 were dominated by oil, autos, trading companies, telecom, and technology. Among US corporations, Fortune-style revenue lists placed Exxon, General Motors, Mobil, Ford, and IBM at or near the top. This composition shows the late-industrial structure of the world economy: energy, autos, and large-scale manufacturing still dominated sales, while computing was rising but not yet the largest revenue category. Major corporate events were unusually consequential. The AT&T divestiture created separate regional operating companies and changed the economics of long-distance, equipment, and local telecom markets; the 1 January separation allocated shares in seven regional companies to existing owners, changing ownership structure without creating equivalent new output. British Telecom's public share offering showed that a large public utility could be privatized through mass equity ownership. The prospectus title page specifies an offer of up to 3,012 million ordinary shares at £1.30: 50p payable initially, 40p on 24 June 1985 and 40p on 9 April 1986. The maximum nominal offer amount is £3,915.6 million, while 3,012 million shares at the initial 50p give £1,506 million before allocation, expenses and other qualifications; neither calculation establishes actual net Treasury receipts in 1984, and the sale is an ownership transaction, not company capital expenditure. The oil industry also saw major consolidation pressures in the mid-1980s, including large US oil transactions around Gulf, Chevron, Getty, and Texaco. These deals reflected lower growth expectations and the need to rationalize assets after the second oil shock.

Significant business births and product launches included the Apple Macintosh in January 1984, Dell's founding in 1984, and Cisco's founding in December 1984. None of these was large in macro terms in the year itself. Their importance is retrospective: they became part of the computing, direct-sales hardware, and networking ecosystem that later reshaped productivity and communications.

Notable bankruptcies and failures were less globally systemic than in 1982, but banking exposures to Latin American debt remained a major risk. The absence of a major global banking collapse in 1984 should not be misread as safety. It reflected official coordination, IMF programs, bank rescheduling, and the decision to treat much sovereign distress as a liquidity problem. Continental Illinois and Johnson Matthey Bankers illustrate emergency restructuring rather than ordinary acquisition strategy; announced government and bank commitments, guarantees, asset purchases and realized losses require separate accounting.

### 5C. Innovation & technology

The personal computer moved from specialist device toward broader consumer and office relevance. The Macintosh introduced a graphical user interface to a mass-market audience, though its early commercial performance was mixed. Apple's 24 January release advertised the Macintosh at a suggested retail price of $2,495, with 128KB of RAM; its announced orders and anticipated university demand are not fulfilled sales, and the dated release establishes a launch, specification and price rather than an economy-wide productivity increment. IBM-compatible PCs continued expanding in business. Semiconductors, software, peripherals, and networking equipment became a deeper industrial ecosystem.

Telecommunications innovation was regulatory as much as technical. The AT&T breakup created incentives for competition and interconnection. British Telecom privatization shifted capital-market expectations and corporate governance. Digital switching, data communications, and early mobile networks were not yet a mass-market story, but they were forming the infrastructure base for later decades. China reported a successful experimental communications satellite and 1,247 km of newly commissioned railway; no monetary return is inferred from these events, and the communiqué's awards and inventions are not relabeled as patent grants.

R&D intensity remained concentrated in the United States, Japan, West Germany, France, the United Kingdom, and the USSR. Defense, aerospace, electronics, pharmaceuticals, and autos were the main R&D-heavy sectors. ### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Labor-market divergence was striking. North America was recovering from recession. Western Europe still had high unemployment and industrial restructuring. Japan maintained low unemployment through firm-based employment systems and manufacturing strength. Latin American labor markets were shaped by inflation, informality, real-wage compression, and adjustment programs.

Union power was a central issue in the United Kingdom, where the miners' strike became a defining labor-market conflict; the BIS assessment treats it as reducing British annual output, whereas West German industrial stoppages mainly affected the timing of output. Wage moderation was central in the Netherlands after the Wassenaar Agreement. Sweden's centralized bargaining and welfare model kept open unemployment low but did not eliminate inflation and competitiveness pressures. In developing economies, human-capital accumulation continued, but underemployment and rural-urban migration were often more important than formal unemployment rates. China's expansion of vocational education, reported in its communiqué, is evidence of institutional change rather than a measured increase in skill-adjusted productivity, and its employee real-wage gain of 13.2% covers employees only.

US consumer borrowing and mortgages show how employment recovery could coexist with increased financial exposure, and Mexican effective lending costs show why falling inflation and lower quoted nominal rates need not remove cash-flow strain. ### 6C. Currencies

The dollar was the central currency story. It appreciated against the yen, Deutsche mark, franc, pound, guilder, Swiss franc, and many developing-country currencies. This moved current-dollar GDP rankings and changed competitiveness. US importers and consumers benefited, while US tradable-goods producers suffered. Japan and West Germany saw stronger export competitiveness but rising political pressure from the United States. In WDI official annual averages, the Deutsche mark moved to 2.85 per dollar (+0.29), the French franc to 8.74 (+1.12), the lira to 1,756.96 (+238.11), the guilder to 3.21 (+0.35), the peseta to 160.76 (+17.33) and sterling to 0.75 pounds per dollar (+0.09), while the yen was essentially unchanged at 237.52 (+0.01). These are differences in annual averages, not exchange-market returns.

The Bank of England gives sterling at $1.2200 on 23 November and $1.1580 at year-end, a late-year interval rather than a full-year depreciation measure. Dollar strength could persist despite late-year US easing; interest differentials alone do not identify the cause of capital flows.

Developing-country exchange rates were more complicated. Mexico, Brazil, and Argentina had high inflation, controls, and repeated depreciations. China used official exchange rates and capital controls; its official rate averaged 2.32 yuan per dollar. The USSR's official exchange rate was not a meaningful market price. Saudi Arabia's dollar-linked riyal helped stabilize domestic financial conditions but tied the monetary framework to the dollar. World Bank exchange-rate series for Brazil, Mexico, Argentina, Turkey and Poland are expressed in later redenominated units and are not shown as 1984 quotations. Australia's float belongs to December 1983. No reserve-composition percentage is inferred from these bilateral prices.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The largest active systemic risk was the Latin American debt crisis. Banks, sovereign borrowers, the IMF, and creditor governments were still managing the consequences of the 1982 shock. The visible risk in 1984 was a breakdown in rescheduling or a banking loss event. The broader risk, clearer with hindsight, was a lost decade of investment, productivity, and social stability across much of Latin America. Developing-country refinancing depended on creditor coordination, while floating-rate debt and dollar appreciation could worsen debt service.

The second risk was exchange-rate misalignment. The dollar's strength looked useful for US disinflation and capital attraction, but it worsened trade imbalances and developing-country debt burdens. With hindsight, 1984 was close to the peak of a misalignment that policymakers would address in 1985. At the time, the risk was visible but politically unresolved.

The third risk was oil-market instability. OPEC's ability to manage supply was weakening. The immediate 1984 problem was revenue pressure for exporters. The hindsight risk was the 1986 oil-price collapse, which would validate concerns about supply discipline and demand weakness.

The fourth risk was structural unemployment in Europe. Even as GDP recovered, labor markets did not. This created long-run social costs, fiscal burdens, and political pressure for labor-market reform. Spain, the United Kingdom, France, the Netherlands, Italy, and West Germany all faced versions of this problem.

The fifth risk was Cold War economic rigidity. The USSR appeared stable in aggregate size, but its productivity weakness, consumer shortages, and dependence on hard-currency energy exports were deeper vulnerabilities. In 1984, before Gorbachev, these weaknesses were visible to specialists but not yet resolved by policy reform.

**Continental Illinois:** The immediate mechanism was a wholesale-funding run against a bank with deteriorating loan quality, not merely high policy rates. The May temporary assistance and the later permanent recapitalization were distinct interventions. The Federal Reserve's historical review describes debate about contagion and moral hazard; it does not establish that every claimed systemic effect materialized. Calling the rescue a routine insured-deposit payout would miss its broader creditor protection.

**Bhopal:** The December toxic release demonstrated an industrial-safety and public-health failure with consequences beyond the firm's accounts. The Health and Safety Executive's retrospective documents unavailable protective systems and uncertainty about the initiating water entry. Eventual mortality and legal outcomes span later years; no final toll or settlement is dated to 1984.

**Household exposure:** US authorities contemporaneously flagged household borrowing and adjustable-rate mortgages. These are observed vulnerabilities, not a forecast that a particular later crisis was unavoidable.

**Horn of Africa famine:** Aggregate trade recovery did not eliminate severe food insecurity. The FAO's later regional review identifies life-threatening famine in 1984/85, including Ethiopia and Sudan, and emphasizes interacting drought, conflict and failures of access to food. This is not a single-cause weather explanation or a precise calendar-1984 death count. Losses of livestock, nutrition and livelihoods are economic costs that a worldwide commodity-output total can conceal.

**Statistical risk:** Modern databases can disagree even on signs and orders of magnitude. Treating the IMF's Polish CPI, Mexico's current-account ratios or the mixed German territory as automatically authoritative would create false precision, so the weakest observations are shown as Data not available with the competing values in Notes rather than averaged.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1984 vs. 1983)

The biggest delta from 1983 to 1984 was the broadening of recovery. In 1983 the recovery was visible but still uneven and fragile. In 1984, industrial-country output and world trade accelerated enough to mark a clear cyclical turning point. The United States moved from rebound to boom-like growth, Japan and Canada strengthened, and Western Europe improved modestly. Contemporary BIS estimates put US real GNP growth up from 3.7% to 6.8% and Japan's from 3.4% to 5.7%; these are not the current IMF GDP growth rates or the Maddison calculations. GATT merchandise export-volume growth accelerated from 2% to 9%, a 7-point increase in that vintage, and its manufactures comparison rose from 4.5% to 12%; the BIS's different 1983 trade figure remains separate.

The second delta was the shift from inflation panic to high-real-rate adjustment. Inflation was no longer the central fear in the major industrial economies, but the cost of defeating it remained visible in unemployment, fiscal deficits, and debt-service burdens. The economic problem moved from price acceleration to the distributional and international consequences of tight money and strong-dollar finance. The US basic discount rate ended the year at 8%, half a point lower than at the end of 1983 after an April rise, while the Bundesbank's discount rate ended half a point higher and the Bank of Japan's was unchanged.

The third delta was in trade and external balances. World trade expanded strongly, but the US current account worsened and surplus economies became more politically exposed. This set up the 1985 policy debate over exchange rates. The emerging imbalance was not only macroeconomic; it affected autos, steel, electronics, semiconductors, and consumer goods.

The fourth delta was in Latin America. The year was less catastrophic than the initial 1982-1983 crisis moment, but chronic adjustment became entrenched. Brazil and Mexico grew again, yet inflation and external debt remained severe. Mexico's December-to-December inflation fell from 80.8% to 59.2%, but its current-account surplus narrowed as imports revived; lower inflation was not the disappearance of inflation or a completed debt resolution. Argentina's democratic transition did not immediately produce macro stabilization. Finance shifted toward bonds and stronger borrowers, while debt negotiations moved toward multiyear arrangements, with proposal, agreement, ratification and disbursement remaining different milestones.

The fifth delta was micro-institutional. The AT&T breakup and BT privatization moved telecom from a regulated or public utility model toward competitive and capital-market frameworks. The Macintosh launch and the founding of Dell and Cisco were early signs of a coming information-technology ecosystem. These changes did not dominate 1984 aggregate statistics, but they altered future productivity channels. Australia's floating exchange regime was a carryover from December 1983, while the August 1984 removal of deposit controls was a new step and February 1985 foreign-bank entry a forward linkage. Continental Illinois and Bhopal added important realized risks absent from a simple recovery narrative. On the Maddison basis, the Republic of Korea entered the top 20 and Iran left it; the current-dollar ranking shows reshuffling rather than a clean entry-exit story.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

The highest-confidence data in this assessment are for the United States, Japan, Canada, Western Europe, Australia, Switzerland, Sweden, and the Netherlands. These economies had established national accounts, central banks, labor statistics, and OECD or comparable reporting. Even there, fiscal measures and unemployment definitions are not perfectly comparable. The strongest individual sources are the Maddison Project Database 2023 records and growth calculations, the IMF World Economic Outlook and Macrohistory Database series, GATT's first-assessment trade table and footnotes, the BIS's contemporary GNP and current-account tables, Banco de México's original annual report, China's original statistical communiqué, the Bank of Japan's dated rate table and the British Telecom prospectus payment schedule.

Medium-confidence data apply to China, India, Mexico, Brazil, Saudi Arabia, and Spain. National accounts exist, but exchange-rate regimes, inflation, controls, informality, or measurement changes create comparability problems. Current-dollar GDP for China in particular should not be interpreted as a full measure of real economic scale.

Low-to-medium-confidence data apply to the USSR and Iran. The USSR was a non-market economy with administered prices, nonconvertible currency, and plan-based output measures. A reasonable error band for Soviet-bloc GDP comparisons is at least +/- 20 percent to 30 percent depending on method. Iran's wartime economy, oil disruptions, and exchange controls make exact macro indicators weak, and the IMF warns that its pre-1990 Iranian data cannot be verified.

The weakest indicators in this assessment are cross-country unemployment, policy interest rates outside the major central banks, government debt/GDP for non-OECD countries, and budget balances in a common methodology. For many developing economies, formal unemployment is less meaningful than underemployment and informality. For high-inflation economies, nominal interest rates and fiscal balances can be misleading without careful treatment of indexation and quasi-fiscal operations. Government debt and fiscal balances mix IMF general-government, WDI central-government and Macrohistory Database public-debt perimeters, labelled in each table, and current-price currency conversion is never mixed with constant-PPP GDP to construct fiscal ratios.

**Vintage differences.** The world GDP level is given as the WDI-style range from an earlier vintage, $12.7 trillion to $13.0 trillion; the current WDI vintage gives $12.459 trillion and the IMF $12.098 trillion, and the earlier world growth range of 4 to 4.5 percent compares with current WDI growth of 4.728% and IMF composites of 4.623% and 4.815%. Header growth rates are the current-dollar-series estimates and differ from the Maddison 2023 rates in the country tables, most notably for China (15.2% against 11.20%), Japan (4.5% against 3.92%), Australia (5.3% against 6.90%), Spain (about 1.8% against 0.98%) and Italy (about 3.0% against 2.57%). Rounded population levels are shown; the Maddison records differ materially for India (739.0 million against about 715 million), Japan (120.0 million against about 117 million), Brazil (134.7 million against about 129 million), Mexico (75.1 million against about 77 million) and the USSR (275.3 million against about 278 million), and the Maddison Germany record (77.9 million) covers the combined territory rather than the Federal Republic's roughly 61 million. Earlier unemployment estimates differ from the IMF series for the Netherlands (around 12% against 8.09%), France (9% to 10% against 8.46%) and Italy (8% to 9% against 7.83%).

**Material conflicts.** The sources cited show three points that run against a simple recovery reading: Mexico's current-account surplus narrowed in 1984 rather than improving, as imports recovered 32% from a depressed base instead of remaining compressed; Italy's current account moved from surplus to deficit rather than improving; and Australia's currency float took effect in December 1983, before the 1984 deregulation steps, rather than following them. Poland's CPI (WDI 15.40% against IMF 75.65%) and pre-1995 GDP reconstruction (IMF -0.37% against Maddison 3.70%) conflict. Mexico's current-account ratios (WDI +2.27%, IMF +5.18%) differ, and Banco de México revised its dollar surplus from $3.9674 billion to $4.2385 billion. Canadian and British current-account signs differ across the BIS, WDI and IMF. India's WDI and IMF inflation rates differ in coverage and timing. Australian and Japanese growth differ across concepts and vintages. China's official growth refers to material-product national income (12%), not GDP. German GDP refers to the Federal Republic in the current-dollar ranking and to the combined territory in Maddison, while IMF unemployment and current-account series cover West Germany. The Macrohistory Database equity series disagree with the Nikkei and S&P 500 figures in the 6A table because the benchmarks differ. Contemporary GNP and later GDP are not necessarily contradictory measurements of an identical object, and IMF latest-actual markers do not independently confirm historical observations. Agreement between WDI and IMF series is not necessarily independent corroboration, since both draw on national sources.
