# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1988

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1988 was a late-cycle expansion year rather than a crisis year, but many of the next decade's fault lines were already visible. World GDP was about $19.5 trillion in current US dollars, and WDI real world output growth was roughly 4.6%. Trade was expanding, financial markets had stabilized after the October 1987 crash, and the major advanced economies were still growing. Yet the composition of growth was uneven: Japan and South Korea were booming, the United States and United Kingdom were running hot, continental Europe was improving, and Latin America remained trapped by debt and inflation.

The feared transmission of the October 1987 stock-market crash into a general recession did not materialize. Investment and merchandise trade strengthened across more economies, rather than depending exclusively on US imports. GATT's preliminary assessment put world merchandise trade-volume growth at 8.5%, against 5.5% in 1987; that is a contemporaneous merchandise estimate, not a services-inclusive trade measure or proof that every economy expanded. The BIS's 4.1% world-output growth composite included Eastern European net material product, which illustrates why apparently simple global headlines carry accounting qualifications.

The dominant global macro force was the interaction between strong demand and monetary tightening. The United States moved toward higher short-term rates through 1988, the United Kingdom raised rates sharply by year-end, and the Bundesbank tightened as German inflation risks and exchange-rate management became more important. Monetary policy increasingly confronted capacity pressure rather than the immediate liquidity demands of the crash. The Federal Reserve raised its basic discount rate once, from 6% to 6.5% on 9 August, and tightened reserve conditions through open-market operations; several proposed discount-rate increases were never approved. British authorities signalled clearing-bank base rates of 13% in November. Japan was the outlier: the Bank of Japan held the official discount rate at 2.5%, which supported rapid growth but also fed the asset-price bubble that would define Japan's 1989-1991 reversal; low consumer-price inflation and rising collateral values coexisted there. Neither the recovery nor liquidity assistance established that leveraged borrowers and intermediaries were solvent.

The microeconomic story was unusually important. 1988 brought the Basel I Capital Accord, the Canada-US Free Trade Agreement implementation process, the Uruguay Round mid-term review, and one of the largest corporate-control battles of the decade in RJR Nabisco. These were not side events. They showed how the economy was being reorganized around bank-capital rules, trade liberalization, leveraged finance, cross-border acquisitions, and globally integrated supply chains. Regulatory change was real but incomplete: the July Basel agreement set a transition toward an 8% risk-weighted capital minimum at end-1992, not immediate universal compliance, and coordinated US circuit breakers addressed trading interruptions and settlement uncertainty, not the underlying fair value of shares. The US thrift crisis remained active: the Federal Savings and Loan Insurance Corporation (FSLIC) incurred substantial liabilities resolving institutions, while much larger restructuring proposals described in 1989 publications belonged to the following year. Corporate-control transactions likewise have separate dates for bids, acceptance, financing and completion.

The year also had two sharp regional stress points. First, China grew at more than 11% in the WDI series but inflation rose to almost 19%, exposing the difficulty of moving from administered prices to market incentives; Chinese nominal incomes rose much faster than purchasing power, and an official survey found many urban households lost real income. Second, Brazil and Mexico were still in the shadow of the 1980s debt crisis, with Brazil's inflation at extreme levels and Mexico's inflation still above 100%. Mexico nevertheless achieved substantial December-to-December disinflation, and its debt exchange reduced face principal but required reserve expenditure; Brazil's refinancing commitments were not the same as cash disbursements. With hindsight, 1988 looks like the last full year before the end of the Cold War economy, the last pre-1989 year of the Japanese bubble acceleration, and one of the key institutional bridge years between the postwar regulated financial order and the globalized 1990s. The late-1980s expansion was neither a uniform success nor an inevitable prelude to later crises.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1988

**1. Late-cycle expansion after the 1987 crash.** The global economy did not fall into recession after Black Monday. Instead, output and trade kept expanding. This mattered historically because it reinforced confidence that central banks could provide liquidity after market stress without immediately derailing real activity. The recovery was broader than financial-market repair: stronger business investment and merchandise exports supported suppliers and transport services, although trading volumes remained depressed in important exchanges. The forward linkage was double-edged: policy credibility improved, but credit markets also became more willing to finance risk, and the forward question was whether physical capacity and profits could sustain the expansion as rates rose.

**2. Monetary tightening in the United States, United Kingdom, and Germany.** The Federal funds rate rose month by month during 1988, the Bank of England ended the year at a very high policy rate, and the Bundesbank tightened. The effective federal funds rate moved from 6.83% in January to 8.76% in December, while the Federal Reserve's annual report describes easing of reserve conditions early in the year and open-market tightening from spring and again in late autumn. The common driver was concern that demand was too strong relative to price stability and external balance. Policy instruments and intentions diverged, however: open-market restraint, discount rates, bank base rates and market yields were separate variables, and rejected Federal Reserve discount-rate requests and the unchanged Japanese discount rate rule out a generic claim that all authorities repeatedly raised policy rates. The forward linkage was the 1989-1990 slowdown in several advanced economies and the eventual pressure on highly leveraged borrowers.

**3. Japan's bubble moved from strong expansion to systemic risk.** Japan's real GDP growth exceeded 7% in the earlier WDI estimate, and the BOJ official discount rate stayed at 2.5%. Equity and land prices kept rising: the Nikkei year-end closing price rose from 21,564 to 30,159. Japanese firms continued high-profile overseas acquisitions. In 1988 this looked like financial strength; retrospectively it was a classic credit-and-asset-price boom that preceded the early-1990s bust.

**4. Trade liberalization advanced despite political conflict.** The Canada-US FTA was signed on 2 January, became law in the United States on 28 September 1988 and took effect on 1 January 1989. The Uruguay Round continued through a difficult mid-term review; the December Montreal meeting ended in deadlock, which the WTO's institutional history records as resolved in April 1989. European integration continued through the EEC framework. Integration involved implementation lags, and policy expectations could influence investment before the final legal and tariff changes occurred. The forward linkage was the North American and global trade architecture of the 1990s, including NAFTA and the WTO.

**5. Developing-country divergence widened.** China, India, South Korea, and parts of Southeast Asia grew fast; Brazil and Mexico remained constrained by inflation and debt; the USSR's planned economy was entering terminal reform stress. This divergence matters because it foreshadowed the 1990s split between East Asian export-led convergence, Latin American stabilization/reform, and post-Soviet transition shock.

**6. Banking regulation became global.** Basel I set an 8% capital-to-risk-weighted-assets minimum for internationally active banks, to be implemented by end-1992. The significance was historical: bank regulation moved from mostly national supervision toward a shared international capital framework. Risk weights affected anticipated capital needs and portfolios before final compliance, and contemporary officials debated whether greater loss absorption might be accompanied by securitization or risk migration; the agreement did not by itself establish a reduction in every type of financial risk. The forward linkage was Basel II, Basel III, and the modern debate over risk weights, capital quality, and regulatory arbitrage.

**7. Corporate finance became more aggressive.** RJR Nabisco and other leveraged deals showed how deep debt markets, tax incentives, and takeover pressure were changing management behavior. LBOs increased contractual debt service, and stable food and tobacco cash flows attracted buyers without eliminating refinancing risk. This was most visible in the United States but influenced global corporate governance. The forward linkage was the 1990s private-equity industry and the debate over shareholder value versus financial fragility.

**8. Debt relief had transaction costs and institutional boundaries.** Mexico's discounted debt exchange, Brazil's concerted lending and the Toronto official-creditor relief were distinct operations. Gross converted principal, net face reduction, collateral purchases and new cash cannot be added together as equivalent relief.

**9. Inflation was a household-allocation problem.** Price freezes, wage adjustment, shortages and indexation redistributed purchasing power. China, Mexico and Brazil show why annual-average inflation, December rates and real wages answer different questions.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

World output in 1988 was about **$19.5 trillion in current US dollars**, and WDI real GDP growth was about **4.6%**. WITS reports world trade at **37.67% of GDP**, with the world trade balance at about **-0.33% of GDP**. This was a healthy expansion by post-1970s standards, especially considering that it came one year after the 1987 global equity crash. Current WDI rows give $19,597.683 billion current-dollar GDP, 4.529% real growth and a world population of 5.116899 billion; WDI population includes source estimates rather than a simultaneous world census, and the world total is not formed by summing the country profiles below.

GATT's press release GATT/1453, dated 21 February 1989, embargoed until 28 February and explicitly based on preliminary information available in mid-February, estimated 1988 world merchandise trade at $2,840 billion, up 14% in current dollars, with volume growth of 8.5%. Manufactures trade volume increased 10%, mining products 7% and agricultural products 4%; these are trade categories, not global sector value added, and dollar depreciation and prices contributed to nominal growth.

The BIS world-output table used 1986 GDP weights and exchange rates, included economies with 1986 GDP above $0.1 billion, and used net material product at non-commercial exchange rates for Eastern Europe. Its 1988 growth of 4.1% compares with 3.4% in 1987. The table's $14,392 billion level is 1986 GDP used for weighting, not 1988 world GDP. It is a meaningful contemporary composite but not harmonized modern global GDP.

Reserve-currency dynamics remained dollar-centered, but the dollar was no longer in the one-way depreciation phase that followed the Plaza Accord. The Louvre Accord environment of managed coordination still influenced monetary choices. Japan and Germany faced the domestic consequences of earlier currency appreciation: policy had to balance exchange-rate stability, demand, and asset prices.

Global inflation was mixed. The advanced economies mostly had moderate CPI inflation: the United States about 4.1%, Japan 0.7%, Germany 1.3%, France 2.7%, and Italy 5.1%. By contrast, China reached 18.8%, Mexico 114.2%, and Brazil roughly 629.1% in the World Bank CPI-inflation series. The world was therefore not in a single inflation regime; it was split between disinflated advanced economies, overheating reform economies, and high-inflation debtor economies.

IMF WEO materials for 1988 assumed an oil price around **$14.50 per barrel**, consistent with the post-1986 low-oil-price environment; that was an assumption, not a realized annual price. The EIA U.S. crude first-purchase price averaged $12.58 per barrel against $15.40 in 1987, and the USGS copper unit value rose from $1,820 to $2,660 per metric ton (see 3D). Oil was relatively cheap, non-oil commodities had recovered from the 1984-1986 weakness, and metals were moving into a late-1980s price boom.

International capital flowed toward the United States and other deep financial markets, toward Japan's expanding financial system, and into selected emerging Asian economies. Latin American capital markets remained constrained by debt overhang, rescheduling, and inflation risk.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The United States tightened through the year. The Federal funds effective rate moved from 6.83% in January to 8.76% in December. The Federal Reserve's 1988 annual report records a single discount-rate increase, from 6% to 6.5% on 9 August. Earlier easing in reserve conditions did not produce a discount-rate cut; requests for increases in July, September and October were disapproved, and late-year requests were considered without action. Tightening through open-market operations began in spring and resumed in late autumn. Monthly effective federal funds rates are market outcomes, not interchangeable with the administered discount rate.

The United Kingdom also tightened, with Bank Rate ending 1988 at 12.875% in the Bank of England's Official Bank Rate history; the Bank's February 1989 economic commentary describes clearing-bank base rates of 13% signalled on 25 November, a differently labelled series. Germany's Bundesbank raised key rates during the year: it tightened tender conditions and raised its discount and Lombard rates in the summer, although a dated end-year instrument table is Data not available in the sources cited. Japan held its official discount rate at 2.5%, in force from 23 February 1987 until 31 May 1989, which supported domestic demand but worsened asset-price excess; the BIS nevertheless describes some firming of Japanese short rates in 1988.

Exchange-rate regimes were mixed. The major advanced currencies floated but were influenced by policy coordination. European currencies were constrained by the EMS. China still operated under heavy exchange-rate and capital controls. Brazil and Mexico had exchange systems shaped by inflation, debt negotiations, and stabilization policy.

Equity markets recovered from the 1987 shock. The S&P 500 total return was about 16.6%, while the Dow Jones Industrial Average rose about 11.9%. The FTSE 100 gained about 4.7%. Japan's Nikkei surge was much stronger and crossed 30,000 late in 1988. This recovery helped restore market confidence but also encouraged the belief that liquidity support could contain financial shocks. The post-crash response improved trading capacity and clearing liquidity but did not restore every intermediary's revenue: the BIS records UK equity turnover down more than 30% and US turnover down about 13%, while German and Japanese turnover rose by less than 10%. Declining volatility, positive asset returns and declining brokerage income occurred together. London market-makers faced high fixed costs and competition after Big Bang; the BIS discusses several explanations rather than showing that deregulation alone caused their losses.

Bond-market conditions reflected inflation concerns in the Anglo economies and credibility in Germany and Switzerland. Matched yield-curve data for 1988 are Data not available in the sources cited.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The most important bilateral trade development was the Canada-US Free Trade Agreement. It was signed on 2 January 1988, passed through the US legislative process, and was signed into US law in September: Public Law 100-449 approved US implementation on 28 September. It took effect on January 1, 1989, so 1988 did not see completed tariff elimination. This agreement was a direct precursor to NAFTA.

The Uruguay Round continued, with the December 1988 Montreal mid-term review focusing on agriculture, tropical products, services, tariffs, and institutional reform. The negotiations were difficult but historically significant because they ultimately led to the World Trade Organization in 1995. The Montreal review ended in deadlock, and the WTO's institutional history places the resolution in April 1989. European internal-market plans likewise preceded completion of the legislative program. These distinctions mattered for firms planning investment, even when cash flows and border procedures had not yet changed.

Cold War economics still structured large parts of trade and production. The USSR remained a major energy, military-industrial, and heavy-industrial economy, but reforms were weakening the planned system. Eastern European economies were still counted in the socialist bloc in 1988, though the political-economic rupture came the following year. No realized peace dividend is quantified in the sources cited, and later Soviet dissolution was not a known outcome in 1988. Eastern bloc aggregate trade balances cannot be assigned to the USSR alone. ### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil remained cheap compared with early-1980s levels. That supported oil-importing economies such as Japan, Germany, India, and many European economies, while constraining oil exporters. OPEC discipline was a continuing issue after the 1986 price collapse. The Bank of England's early-1989 commentary reports a late-November OPEC agreement setting an 18.5 million barrel per day ceiling for the first half of 1989; that was a future quota, not actual 1988 production.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

The global labor picture was not comparable across countries. Comparable unemployment data are not available for 1988 for all top 20 economies: IMF WEO rates exist for most market economies, but the sources cited give none for China, the USSR, India or Brazil, and for Switzerland, Sweden, Belgium and Austria no Maddison or IMF WEO comparison is given; socialist economies did not measure unemployment in market terms. A market unemployment rate cannot be imposed on Soviet labor allocation or Chinese registration systems. A simple cross-country unemployment table would therefore mislead. Declining unemployment in several industrial economies coexisted with high European joblessness, and migration flows, skill-specific wages and a comparable global employment total are Data not available in the sources cited.

The main labor themes were structural. Advanced economies continued shifting toward services and finance. South Korea faced stronger wage bargaining after democratization. China absorbed labor into township and village enterprises and urban construction. India remained dominated by agriculture and informal work. Brazil and Mexico saw inflation distort real wages and household planning.

China's statistical communiqué is unusually informative about mechanisms: urban living-expense income per person rose 22.2% nominally but only 1.2% in real terms, and rural net income rose 6.3% after its own price adjustment. A 13-city sample found 34.9% of households suffered a real-income decline attributable to inflation; that sample does not cover all Chinese households. Year-end saving deposits grew 23.8%, but a nominal deposit stock is neither a real saving gain nor a national saving rate.

Demographically, China and India were the largest labor reservoirs. The USSR and advanced Europe were aging more gradually. Urbanization remained central in China, Brazil, Mexico, South Korea, and India.

### 5C. Innovation & technology

The strongest technology channels were incremental rather than singular. Personal computers were spreading through business and households. Semiconductor demand supported Japan, the United States, and Korea. Telecom equipment and digital switching supported firms in the United States, Sweden, Japan, and Europe. ### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Sectoral employment kept shifting from agriculture and old industry toward services, finance, logistics, and technology-intensive manufacturing. South Korea's labor movement became more assertive after democratization. The United Kingdom and United States had more flexible labor markets but also more inequality pressure. Brazil and Mexico saw inflation undermine real wages and household planning. China and India still had enormous informal or semi-formal labor pools.

The most defensible household mechanism is the distinction between nominal earnings and purchasing power. Chinese urban and rural income deflators differ, so their real-growth rates are not a perfectly controlled comparison. Mexican wage restraint helped disinflation but also constrained demand. Soviet and Polish administered prices concealed non-price rationing. These mechanisms qualify any simple inference from GDP growth to welfare. China reported increased employment and contract labor, but those counts are not an internationally comparable unemployment rate.

Education and workforce upgrading mattered most in East Asia. South Korea's human-capital base supported electronics and heavy industry. Japan's firm-based training system helped manufacturing quality. India had pockets of technical talent but remained constrained by licensing, infrastructure, and low average incomes. Qualitatively, US and UK fixed-income markets faced rising short rates, Germany's markets reflected Bundesbank credibility and tightening, and Japan's low-rate setting supported domestic leverage. The late-year policy turn increased short-rate pressure, while expected inflation and currency concerns affected longer yields. The BIS describes convergence across ERM long rates alongside rising German yields and uncertainty over withholding tax.

No major advanced-economy sovereign default occurred in 1988. The sovereign-risk story was concentrated in developing-country debt overhang, especially Latin America. Mexico's exchanged bonds paid LIBOR plus 1.625 percentage points, against 0.8125 on the old restructured debt, but on a smaller principal; the twenty-year bullet maturity and zero-coupon collateral changed cash flows without removing all risk. Brazil's 6% exit bonds and the official Toronto relief were different structures, and the later Brady initiative was not a completed 1988 settlement.

### 6C. Currencies

The dollar remained the reserve-currency anchor but was managed in a post-Plaza/post-Louvre environment. The yen and deutsche mark remained central to policy coordination. European currencies operated inside the EMS, although Spain was outside the ERM in 1988. China, India, Brazil, Mexico, and the USSR did not have open capital-account market exchange-rate regimes comparable to the major advanced economies.

The dollar recovered during part of the year, with coordinated intervention and differing domestic conditions. The BIS's more-than-20% Deutsche mark depreciation from January to August is an intrayear move, not a calendar annual return. In WDI official annual-average terms, the yen averaged 128.15 per dollar (16.49 fewer than in 1987), the Deutsche mark 1.76, the French franc 5.96, the lira 1,301.63, sterling 0.56 pounds per dollar, the Canadian dollar 1.23, the peseta 116.49, the guilder 1.98, the Australian dollar 1.28, the won 731.46 (91.10 fewer), the rupee 13.92, the yuan 3.72 and the rupiah 1,685.70; these are annual averages in the source's units, not currency investment returns. ### 6D. Alternative assets

Gold was lower than in 1987 in several historical price summaries; the USGS gold unit value fell from $15.4 million to $14.1 million per metric ton, a statistical benchmark rather than a London fixing. ## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The active crises of 1988 were mostly slow-burning rather than sudden. Latin America's debt and inflation crisis continued. Brazil's inflation was extreme. Mexico was stabilizing but still had triple-digit inflation. The USSR's economic reform process exposed the structural weaknesses of central planning but did not yet produce the post-1991 collapse. The US thrift crisis was already active, not only a hindsight vulnerability: deposit insurance, weak capital and risky portfolios interacted, and recovery in equity prices did not solve insolvent mortgage institutions. FSLIC's incurred liabilities, later appropriations and ultimate taxpayer losses are separate quantities, and the incoming administration's proposed $90 billion program was not an enacted 1988 rescue.

The major near-miss was the 1987 crash's aftermath. By 1988 the real economy had avoided a crash-induced recession, but the lesson many market participants drew was that central banks would provide liquidity in stress. With hindsight, that helped normalize more aggressive leverage. Circuit breakers could coordinate trading pauses but could not guarantee solvency or prevent repricing.

Systemic vulnerabilities building in 1988 included Japan's asset bubble, US leveraged buyouts, US savings-and-loan losses, UK household and property leverage, Swedish credit liberalization, China's inflationary reform stress, and Soviet shortages. These did not all break in 1988, but each became important in the next five years. LBO leverage and Japanese asset prices were contemporaneously debated risks: the BIS offered competing valuation explanations and warned that capital gains could outrun earnings capacity. Later crashes are hindsight, not outcomes established by 1988 evidence.

Debt and inflation remained regionally concentrated. Mexico's reserve-funded debt exchange and Brazil's negotiated agreement changed creditor positions without resolving every financing constraint. Chinese buying panics and weak real-income gains exposed distributional stress, and the US drought was a material supply shock despite global expansion, so the sources cited do not support a uniform crisis-free characterization of the year.

Policy mistakes visible in retrospect include Japan's prolonged low-rate stance, insufficient early recognition of US S&L losses, and the difficulty China had sequencing price reform with credit restraint. At the time, however, many policymakers saw growth as evidence of resilience rather than excess.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1988 vs. 1987)

The biggest change from 1987 was that financial panic did not become recession. 1987 ended with a stock-market crash; 1988 ended with a stronger belief in central-bank crisis management. Equity markets recovered, and real GDP growth remained robust. GATT's matched vintage shows merchandise trade volume accelerating from 5.5% to 8.5%, and the BIS's world-output growth rose from 3.4% to 4.1%. Current WDI world growth rose from 3.751% to 4.529%, or 0.778 percentage points. Different composites need not show identical acceleration, and nominal-dollar growth is not real growth.

Monetary policy shifted from emergency reassurance to tightening. The Federal funds rate rose through 1988, the Bank of England raised rates aggressively, and the Bundesbank tightened. The US basic discount rate moved from 6% at the end of 1987 to 6.5%, a single increase. Japan did not tighten, holding its discount rate at 2.5% and creating a widening contrast between Japan's asset boom and other advanced economies' anti-inflation stance. British restraint intensified while housing turnover slowed.

Trade policy moved forward. The Canada-US FTA implementation process made North American integration concrete. The Uruguay Round survived its difficult mid-term phase, although the Montreal meeting in December ended in deadlock that was resolved only in April 1989. These developments made 1988 more structurally important than its crisis-free surface suggests. Basel's agreement, new exchange benchmarks such as the DAX and CAC 40, and trade legislation were institutional changes whose compliance, live histories and implementation effects were not all realized in 1988.

China's overheating became much more visible in 1988 than in 1987. Inflation moved to a politically significant level. Brazil's inflation problem worsened dramatically. India's growth rebounded strongly, in contrast with China's 2.2% grain decline. Mexico's December-to-December inflation fell 107.5 percentage points even though its annual-average rate remained very high. South Korea remained in high-growth mode and used the Seoul Olympics to signal industrial modernization.

The USSR moved deeper into reform but not yet into open collapse. Compared with 1987, the economic contradictions of perestroika were clearer: limited market mechanisms were introduced without a complete price, budget, ownership, or financial system. The current-dollar top 20 shows no confident entry or exit, and the Maddison selection has none either; within the Maddison records, Japan moved ahead of China.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

**Highest-confidence data:** World GDP, WDI real GDP growth, WITS trade share, market-economy nominal GDP for most advanced economies, CPI inflation where the World Bank/DataHub series reports values, Federal funds monthly values, BOJ discount-rate status, Bank of England Bank Rate, and Basel I institutional facts. Dated original documents are also strong: the Federal Reserve's annual-report record of discount-rate decisions, the GATT trade estimate, the Chinese statistical communiqué, Banco de México's debt-exchange figures and the SEC circuit-breaker design. Confidence attaches to those source definitions and dates; faithful transcription of a database cell establishes what the source says, not historical truth.

**Medium-confidence data:** Brazil's 1988 nominal GDP is taken from the WITS country series because a WDI mirror series row is blank for 1988; it is consistent with the World Bank/WITS source family. Equity-market annual returns for the S&P 500, Dow and FTSE are cited from financial-data aggregators rather than official exchange archives.

**Lowest-confidence data:** Former USSR current-US-dollar GDP is the weakest figure in the ranking. It is constructed from UNdata current-dollar GDP per capita and Maddison population, then cross-checked against Maddison PPP-style reconstructed GDP. It should not be compared mechanically with market-economy current-dollar GDP. A 20-30% uncertainty band is appropriate. The Maddison former-USSR growth of 2.13% is conditional source-record arithmetic, not certified historical-polity growth.

**Vintage differences.** The world aggregates in the text come from an earlier WDI vintage: about $19.5 trillion and about 4.6% growth, against $19,597.683 billion and 4.529% in the current WDI rows and $19,300.492 billion and 4.530% (PPP weights) or 4.492% (market weights) in the IMF WEO; the contemporary BIS composite gives 4.1%. The ranking-table and header growth rates are WDI rates; Japan's 7.1% compares with 6.79% in the current WDI vintage and 6.662% in the IMF WEO. The Maddison growth rates in the country tables are a different series; examples of the gap include China (5.58% against WDI 11.3%), Spain (6.63% against 5.1%), Australia (4.49% against 5.6%), Japan (6.20% against 7.1%), India (10.42% against 9.6%) and Korea (11.90% against 10.6%, with the IMF at 12.133%). Rounded population levels match the Maddison records to one decimal place. The former USSR's current-dollar proxy of $779.2 billion and its Maddison level of 3,199.635 billion 2011 international dollars measure different things.

**Material conflicts.** Argentina's contemporary BIS growth (+2.2%) conflicts in sign with the IMF WEO (-1.957%) and Maddison (-1.90%). Brazil's BIS inflation (582%) and growth (-0.3%) differ from WDI inflation (629.11%) and IMF WEO growth (+0.264%). British CPI is 4.16% in WDI and 4.61% in the IMF series, and the British current account is -3.88% of GDP in WDI, -3.29% in the IMF series and a $26.1 billion deficit in the BIS account. The British year-end policy figure is 12.875% in the Bank of England's Official Bank Rate history and 13% for the clearing-bank base rates signalled on 25 November in the Bank's commentary; the two are differently labelled series. German GDP refers to the combined territory, while the IMF's unemployment, prices and current account refer to the Federal Republic; the BIS's $48.6 billion West German surplus is not divided by combined-territory output. Japan's growth differs across Maddison, WDI and the IMF. China's growth is 11.2% real GNP in the original communiqué, 11.3% in WDI and 5.58% in Maddison, and its retail, living-cost and CPI inflation measures (18.5%, 20.7% and 18.8%) describe different price indices; India's BIS inflation entry (9.8%) is a second-quarter observation. The Federal Reserve's annual report describes easing of reserve conditions early in the year before open-market tightening from spring and again in late autumn, which contradicts a description of the federal funds rate rising month by month; it records one discount-rate increase, with requested increases disapproved. Current data contradict the description of Japanese growth as exceeding 7%, which rests on the earlier WDI estimate. The WTO's institutional history records that the December Montreal review ended in deadlock, resolved only in April 1989, which qualifies the statement that the Uruguay Round survived its mid-term phase. The BIS records the US thrift crisis as active in 1988, with roughly $40 billion of FSLIC liabilities incurred, rather than only a building vulnerability. The US drought and China's 2.2% grain decline mean that the absence of a single global food-price shock cannot be established without a matched staple-price panel. Canadian current-account data in the Macrohistory Database have an unresolved CAD/USD conversion description, so the WDI ratio is used. Bank Indonesia's English-language history prints 1998 under its 1988 heading, while the Indonesian-language page dates Pakto 88 to 27 October 1988. Agreement between WDI and IMF series is not necessarily independent corroboration, since both draw on national sources, and no source is treated as correct simply because it is more recent.

**Unavailable or not harmonized:** Comparable unemployment for all top 20 economies in 1988; policy rates for every top 20 country; debt/GDP, budget balance, current-account balance, and exchange-rate point data for every country, and for Switzerland, Sweden, Belgium and Austria no Maddison or IMF WEO comparison is given; official global company revenue/market-cap rankings for 1988; an annual official commodity price table from the World Bank Pink Sheet. Further gaps are full equity-market coverage and annual endpoints, matched ten-year yields and spreads, migration, sector employment, wage distributions, R&D expenditure, city housing beyond the two samples shown, sanctions inventories, some policy-rate sequences, and separate national accounts for the German Democratic Republic, Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia. Where a figure is not given in the sources cited, the document states "Data not available" rather than guessing; that is a statement about the sources, not a claim that the data never existed.

**Conflicting source issues:** Nominal GDP and PPP-style historical GDP produce different rankings. China and the former USSR rank much higher on Maddison 2011 international-dollar GDP than on market exchange-rate GDP. For non-market economies, current-dollar conversion is partly an accounting convention rather than a market valuation. For developing economies with inflation, exchange-rate changes can make nominal current-dollar GDP rankings unstable. Third-party index data carry their own rights restrictions.
