# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1986

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1986 was defined by a rare combination of lower oil prices, falling inflation, large exchange-rate movements, and policy reform. The collapse in crude oil prices after Saudi Arabia shifted toward defending market share changed global terms of trade in a matter of months. Oil importers received an income transfer, inflation in industrial economies dropped, and central banks gained room to ease. Oil exporters, especially Mexico, Indonesia, Iran, Nigeria, Venezuela, and the Soviet bloc, faced immediate fiscal and external pressure. The transfer did not deliver an immediate, uniform growth acceleration: within an importing country, households and transport users could benefit while oilfield services, reserve-backed lenders and producing regions deteriorated. The U.S. domestic first-purchase crude price averaged $12.51 per barrel against $24.09 in 1985, and December-to-December employment in U.S. oil-and-gas extraction fell from 570,000 to 422,000.

The year also showed how exchange rates could reshape measured economic power. After the 1985 Plaza Accord, the dollar continued to fall, the yen and major European currencies appreciated, and nominal GDP in current US dollars rose sharply for Japan and Western Europe even when real growth was modest. This is why two rankings are needed: a Maddison real-output ranking in 2011 international dollars, which includes the former USSR, and a World Bank current-dollar cross-check for market economies. On Maddison's real-output basis the United States remained first, followed by the former USSR, Japan, China, and Germany. On current-dollar World Bank data, the United States, Japan, Germany, France, and Italy led the ranking among countries with comparable market-exchange-rate data. Trade evidence shows continuity in aggregate volume alongside a major compositional change: GATT's March 1987 first assessment estimated merchandise export volume growth of 3.5%, the same as its 1985 figure, with petroleum trade volume up 9% and manufactured trade up only 3%.

The most important structural policy changes were forward-looking rather than immediately visible in GDP. The Uruguay Round of GATT negotiations began in September 1986 and eventually produced the WTO framework. The Single European Act, signed on 17 and 28 February 1986 and in force from 1 July 1987, moved the European Community toward the 1992 internal market. The United States enacted the Tax Reform Act of 1986. The United Kingdom implemented the London financial market "Big Bang" and privatized British Gas. France began a privatization cycle under the Chirac government. Vietnam launched Doi Moi. These changes helped define the late-1980s shift toward liberalization, financial integration, and market-oriented reform.

The year was not simply a benign disinflation story. Latin American debt stress remained severe, Mexico contracted, Brazil grew but with very high inflation, and oil-exporting economies faced a sudden loss of export revenue. The financial response was asymmetric: policy easing in the United States and Japan coexisted with tight credit in Mexico and balance-sheet distress in the U.S. oil-producing region, and the BIS reports Mexico's commercial-bank new-money agreement as signed in March 1987, not as a completed 1986 disbursement. Japan's monetary easing and asset-market acceleration were positive at the time but, in hindsight, helped create conditions for the asset bubble that peaked in 1989. The Soviet system faced low oil revenues, Chernobyl-related costs, and deeper evidence of productivity weakness. 1986 therefore sits at an inflection point: the world economy was expanding, but the distribution of gains and risks was changing quickly. Statistical disagreement is also material: Banco de México's original current account for 1986 was in deficit while the IMF's current series shows a surplus ratio, Chinese official growth refers to GNP rather than GDP, and Germany's pre-unification series cover different territories.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1986

**1. Oil-price collapse as a global terms-of-trade shock.** The most important price event of 1986 was the oil crash. World Bank analysis later described the 1985-86 episode as a 61 percent fall in oil prices between January and July 1986, following Saudi Arabia's December 1985 policy change. GATT reporting also described a fall from about $30 per barrel in November 1985 to a temporary spot-market low around $10 in mid-1986. The shock lowered inflation and import bills for oil importers, but it weakened oil exporters' fiscal revenues, external balances, and debt-service capacity. Lower petroleum prices also reduced the value of reserves pledged against loans, and oil-service employment and investment responded much faster than the long-lived stock of producing facilities; importer gains did not negate producers' losses. The countries most affected included Mexico, Indonesia, Iran, the USSR, Venezuela, Saudi Arabia, Nigeria, and other producers. The forward linkage was durable: lower oil prices persisted for years and changed the bargaining power inside OPEC.

**2. Disinflation and monetary easing in advanced economies.** Lower energy costs reduced inflation in industrial economies and allowed monetary authorities to ease without reigniting the inflation psychology of the 1970s. The Federal Reserve lowered reserve pressure and discount rates, with federal funds trading around the 6.25 to 6.375 percent area after the July 1986 discount-rate cut; the BIS describes U.S. discount-rate cuts from 7.5% at the start of 1986 to 5.5% after the August step. The Bank of Japan cut the official discount rate repeatedly between January 1986 and February 1987, from 5% to 3% during 1986 and to 2.5% on 23 February 1987. Lower rates supported asset prices and housing, but the easing cycle had uneven consequences. It supported US demand and Japan's domestic stimulus, while raising concerns about financial excess in the years that followed. Lower policy rates reduced some refinancing costs, but principal outstanding and weak collateral still constrained borrowers; currency composition, floating-rate reset dates and access to new lending mattered as much as headline rates.

**3. Exchange-rate realignment after the Plaza Accord.** The dollar's nominal effective exchange rate fell by about 17 percent in 1986 compared with its 1985 average, according to GATT reporting. The yen and major European currencies appreciated sharply. This mechanically inflated the current-dollar GDP of Japan and Western Europe and squeezed exporters in Japan and Germany, although exporters could absorb currency appreciation in margins rather than immediately lose all sales. It also changed trade-value statistics: a large part of the rise in dollar-denominated world trade value was a valuation effect rather than a pure volume effect. The forward linkage was the Louvre Accord of 1987 and Japan's subsequent policy dilemma between exchange-rate stability and domestic asset inflation.

**4. Market liberalization, trade diplomacy, and institutional reform.** 1986 began policy processes whose full impact came later. The Uruguay Round was launched in Punta del Este and sought to go beyond manufactured-goods tariffs into agriculture, services, investment measures, and intellectual property; its launch opened negotiations rather than completing a regime. The Single European Act amended the European Community treaties and accelerated the internal-market program, although signature in February 1986 preceded entry into force in July 1987. The United Kingdom's Big Bang changed the City of London's structure by removing fixed minimum commissions and single-capacity restrictions, bringing new capital, technology and competition; higher turnover did not guarantee higher intermediary profits or lower systemic risk. Privatizations in Britain and France changed state ownership models. Vietnam's Doi Moi announced a new reform path. These moves connected microeconomic institutional change with future macro performance.

**5. Debt, inflation, and adjustment in Latin America.** Brazil and Argentina grew in 1986, but inflation remained a central macro constraint. Brazil's currency conversion, price freeze and wage-adjustment rules changed real incomes and inventories; a monetary-unit change did not itself eliminate inflation. Mexico contracted sharply under debt pressure and lower oil revenue. The region's policy debate still revolved around external debt, exchange-rate credibility, fiscal adjustment, and heterodox anti-inflation plans. The forward linkage was the continued search for debt restructuring, culminating later in Brady-style approaches.

**6. East Asian industrial acceleration.** Japan remained the dominant Asian industrial economy, but South Korea's 1986 growth above 10 percent and current-account swing into surplus showed the rise of export-led late industrialization. China maintained very high real growth while reforming through township and village enterprises, special zones, and decentralized incentives; its official output, retail and enterprise evidence also shows why aggregate expansion does not certify every firm's health. This theme foreshadowed the larger Asia-centered production-network shift of the 1990s.

**7. Non-market economy stress.** The former USSR still ranked as the world's second-largest real-output economy in Maddison estimates, but the data are highly uncertain and not directly comparable to market-price GDP. Low oil revenues, administrative inefficiency, Chernobyl-related costs, and the early Gorbachev reform agenda exposed a widening gap between measured scale and economic flexibility. The issue mattered for Eastern Europe as well, including Poland, where output recovered modestly but inflation and external constraints persisted.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

World GDP in current US dollars was about $15.35 trillion in World Bank WDI data, up from about $13.02 trillion in 1985. This large nominal increase reflected real growth, inflation, and especially dollar depreciation. World real GDP growth was about 3.25 percent, down from about 3.77 percent in 1985. World population was about 4.94 billion.

The value increase was heavily affected by exchange-rate translation. Reserve-currency dynamics remained dollar-centered, yet the dollar's depreciation against the yen and major European currencies dominated trade-value and GDP-value comparisons. Global inflation generally declined in advanced economies, especially because oil prices collapsed. Inflation remained much higher in several developing and debtor economies, notably Brazil, Mexico, Turkey, and Poland.

Capital flows continued to run from surplus economies toward the United States, which recorded a large current-account deficit. Japan and Germany ran substantial surpluses. The BIS table of current accounts gives the United States -$140.6 billion, Japan +$85.8 billion and West Germany +$35.7 billion, while its oil-exporting developing group, which includes Mexico and Indonesia, moved from +$3 billion in 1985 to -$36 billion in 1986; the Eastern European group is separate. The worldwide table has a -$64 billion discrepancy, which does not measure a world borrowing need, and these balances measure flows, not outstanding foreign assets. Latin American debtors remained constrained by debt-service obligations and limited voluntary private lending. Oil exporters experienced a negative income shock and had less capacity to finance imports or recycle surpluses.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

In the United States, monetary conditions eased as inflation fell and growth moderated. The Federal Reserve's August 1986 policy record described federal funds trading around 6.25 to 6.375 percent after the July discount-rate cut. The BIS describes discount-rate cuts from 7.5% at the start of 1986 to 5.5% after the August step; that is the posted discount rate, not the federal-funds annual average. In Japan, the Bank of Japan moved through a major easing cycle as yen appreciation hurt exports and wholesale prices weakened: its effective-date table gives 4.5% from 30 January, 4% from 10 March, 3.5% from 21 April and 3% from 1 November, against 5% before the first cut, and the 2.5% rate belongs to 23 February 1987. In West Germany, long-term yields moved in the 6 percent range in OECD/FRED data, while the Deutsche mark's strength restrained inflation and complicated export performance; the Bundesbank discount rate was 3.5% from March 1986, and its reduction to 3% came in January 1987. The United Kingdom moved toward a lower-rate environment but still had materially higher nominal rates than Germany and Japan; a single annual official UK policy rate is Data not available in the sources cited.

Exchange rates were managed floats rather than Bretton Woods-style fixed parities. The dominant FX story was the post-Plaza depreciation of the dollar. The yen's appreciation transformed Japan's current-dollar GDP and export incentives. The Deutsche mark and other European currencies also appreciated, making European GDP look much larger in dollars and reinforcing disinflation through cheaper imports.

Credit conditions were easier in the core advanced economies than in the early 1980s, but credit was not uniformly available. Highly indebted developing countries remained constrained. Banco de México describes reduced productive-sector credit as part of adjustment to missing foreign finance, and U.S. petroleum lenders faced falling collateral and impaired borrowers despite easier aggregate monetary conditions. BIS discussion of monetary control distinguishes minimum reserve ratios, intervention rates and posted discount rates; they cannot be collapsed into one comparable policy-rate column. Equity markets were strong: the S&P 500 rose about 14.6 percent on a price basis and about 18.7 percent on a total-return basis; the Dow Jones Industrial Average rose about 22.6 percent; Japan's Nikkei 225 rose more than 40 percent; and the FTSE 100 rose about 18.9 percent. ### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The Uruguay Round began in September 1986; it was not the creation of the WTO, which came later. Its agenda was historically important because it moved the trade regime toward agriculture, services, investment measures, and intellectual property, not just tariffs on manufactured goods. The European Community moved toward deeper integration through the Single European Act, signed on 17 and 28 February 1986 and in legal force from July 1987. Spain and Portugal joined the European Community in 1986, a different institutional event. Cold War structures still shaped trade flows, technology restrictions, defense spending, and the planned-economy trading area. The Iran-Iraq War continued to affect oil markets and regional risk. Trade tensions remained high around the US deficit and Japanese surpluses, especially in autos, electronics, and semiconductors; the exact legal instruments of the US-Japan semiconductor dispute are not given in the sources cited.

No numerical sanction effect is estimated in the sources cited. The source-defined Eastern trading area and oil-exporter groups are not current geographic or political classifications. The Soviet Union's hard-currency export exposure is analytically different from domestic administered-price output accounting.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil was the dominant commodity. The shift in Saudi output strategy and the OPEC market-share struggle lowered prices dramatically. Importers such as Japan, Germany, India, and much of Western Europe benefited through lower energy import bills and lower inflation. Producers such as Mexico, Indonesia, Iran, and the USSR suffered revenue losses. Banco de México reports the Mexican export crude average at $11.8 per barrel, 46.6% of its 1985 average, a decline of 53.4%. Mining trade volumes rose because lower prices encouraged consumption and stockbuilding, but dollar unit values fell; GATT's mining trade volume, which includes fuels, rose 7%. Agriculture remained shaped by subsidy competition and weak international demand growth, with GATT agricultural trade volume up 1%. Metals were weak in 1986 but began to move toward the 1987-89 boom.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor markets were not comparable across countries. OECD unemployment remained high in several European economies: Spain was above 20 percent in IMF WEO data, the United Kingdom above 11 percent, Belgium about 10 percent, France near 9 percent, and West Germany near 8 percent. Japan and Switzerland had very low measured unemployment, while China and several developing economies used definitions that do not compare directly with OECD labor-force surveys. Demographically, the world remained young, urbanization continued in Asia and Latin America, and migration flows into North America, Western Europe, and the Gulf remained economically significant. Wage growth generally lagged productivity in some tradable sectors exposed to import competition, while high-inflation economies faced unstable real wages.

The National Petroleum Council's February 1987 report gives a December-to-December fall in U.S. oil-and-gas extraction jobs from 570,000 to 422,000, down 148,000 or 25.96%, and in oilfield-service employment from 315,000 to 206,000, down 34.60%. These are establishment-sector measures, not national unemployment, and they are not comparable with the 1982 annual-average employment base the council also reports; the council is an industry advisory body whose report is hosted by the Department of Energy. Banco de México records Mexican manufacturing hours down 5.4%, manufacturing employment down 4% and real minimum wages down 7.9%; a 0.4% increase in IMSS insured persons reflects different institutional coverage and is not a contradictory national employment estimate. **Economic narrative:**

The US economy expanded at a moderate pace, with lower inflation and easier monetary policy supporting demand. The dollar's fall helped future export competitiveness, but the current-account deficit remained large. The Tax Reform Act of 1986 lowered marginal rates and broadened the base, changing incentives for households, corporations, real estate, and tax shelters; the size of its effect on property prices or tax-shelter losses is Data not available in the sources cited.

Contemporary BIS national accounts put 1986 real GNP/GDP growth at 2.5%, against 3.44% in MPD and 3.43% in WDI; the BIS table combines national GNP/GDP conventions and a much earlier vintage. Its consumption increase of 4.1%, residential investment increase of 9.4% and non-residential decline of 1% give a differentiated demand picture rather than a replacement GDP estimate.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:**

At the micro level, services, finance, technology, and consumer sectors were strong. The oil patch weakened sharply because lower crude prices hurt drilling and energy credit; lower revenues passed into drilling, service jobs and reserve-backed credit, and oilfield-service employment fell from 315,000 to 206,000 between December endpoints. Banking exposure to energy and Latin American debt remained a vulnerability. Consumer demand benefited from lower inflation and interest rates, while manufacturing remained pressured by import competition and the delayed effects of dollar depreciation. Computers and software diffused through corporate offices; Microsoft's investor FAQ dates its IPO to 13 March 1986 at an offer price of $21 per share, which does not by itself measure software-sector revenue growth. Autos faced import competition; energy and oilfield services contracted; finance benefited from lower rates but carried LDC and energy-credit risks; housing was supported by lower borrowing costs, though tax reform changed real-estate incentives.

**Economic narrative:**

The Soviet economy remained enormous in real-output estimates but structurally rigid. The oil-price collapse reduced hard-currency earnings, while Chernobyl imposed direct costs, energy-system disruption, and reputational damage. Early Gorbachev reforms sought acceleration and administrative improvement, but the system still relied on plan targets, heavy industry, and defense-linked production.

MPD's displayed growth of 4.13% is source arithmetic on a territorial reconstruction, not an official GDP observation or a measure of freely valued consumer welfare. Lower export prices can reduce hard-currency purchasing power even when reported domestic volume rises. A national monetary cost of Chernobyl is not given in the sources cited, and later collapse was not inevitable on the 1986 evidence.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:**

The microeconomy showed chronic shortages and weak consumer choice despite large industrial capacity. Energy, metallurgy, machinery, and military-industrial production dominated. ## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The main active crisis was the oil-price collapse and its fiscal impact on exporters. Mexico was the clearest macro casualty among the top economies, while Indonesia, Iran, and the USSR faced major terms-of-trade losses. Brazil and Argentina had high inflation and fragile stabilization plans. The oil shock was a realized revenue and collateral shock, with concentrated losses and uneven consumer gains: indebted exporters faced less foreign exchange, lenders faced impaired reserve values, and workers faced industry-specific layoffs. Lower interest rates could soften debt service without restoring export receipts. The Chernobyl disaster was a low-probability, high-impact shock with long-lived health, environmental, and economic costs, particularly for Ukraine, Belarus, Russia, and the Soviet fiscal system; claims that it was entirely unforeseeable are not supported by the sources cited; a national economic cost is not quantified in the sources cited, and the sources do not support attributing a particular fraction of Soviet decline to it.

Systemic vulnerabilities were also building. In hindsight, Japan's 1986 easing cycle and asset-market boom were early warning signs of the late-1980s bubble. Financial liberalization in London improved efficiency but also increased the complexity and speed of securities markets, and regulatory restructuring altered counterparty and operational exposure. The BIS's contemporary concern was that markets could become vulnerable when rising asset prices and debt outpaced underlying earnings. The FDIC's retrospective analysis of banking problems in the Southwest supports a mechanism linking energy and property concentration, but later failure totals cannot be counted as failures in 1986. The US current-account deficit and Japan-Germany surpluses showed persistent global imbalances. Debt overhang in Latin America remained unresolved; Brazil's stabilization strains and Mexico's financing negotiations show that an apparently favorable world price movement did not remove domestic policy trade-offs.

Tail risks that did not fully materialize in 1986 included a generalized advanced-economy recession after the oil shock and exchange-rate realignment, a disorderly dollar collapse, and a complete breakdown of OPEC coordination. These risks were contained, but only temporarily. Policy coordination continued into 1987 through efforts to stabilize exchange rates. No probability of a crash, sovereign default or banking insolvency is given in the sources cited, and the absence of a realized global recession is not proof that all vulnerabilities were resolved.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1986 vs. 1985)

World real GDP growth slowed from about 3.77 percent in 1985 to about 3.25 percent in 1986, while current-dollar world GDP rose sharply because of dollar depreciation. The biggest structural break was oil: 1985 still looked like a weak oil-market environment, but 1986 became a full price crash. U.S. first-purchase crude prices fell 48.07% on the same annual benchmark, Mexican export crude prices fell 53.4% on Banco de México's stated ratio, and U.S. extraction employment fell 25.96% between December endpoints; these separate calculations are not interchangeable. Inflation in industrial economies fell, and central banks had more room to ease: the U.S. and Japanese discount rates ended the year two percentage points lower.

The exchange-rate regime shifted from Plaza Accord announcement to real economic adjustment. In 1985, currency realignment was a policy objective. In 1986, it became visible in trade volumes, corporate profits, and GDP rankings. Japan's current-dollar GDP rose dramatically, but exporters faced a real squeeze. Germany's exports also slowed relative to prior years. GATT's merchandise-volume growth stayed at 3.5%, while petroleum trade volume rose sharply and manufactured export growth slowed from 5.5% to 3%; its current-dollar trade value moved from $1,922 billion to $2,110 billion, a calculated 9.78% rounded to 10% in the report, which is not world GDP growth. The fall in oil exporters' current accounts and the rise in Japanese and West German surpluses illustrate the redistribution of external balances.

Trade policy moved from concern over imbalances to formal negotiation. The Uruguay Round launch made 1986 a starting point for the next global trade regime. European integration moved from programmatic ambition to treaty change through the Single European Act, whose signature preceded entry into force. Domestic policy also shifted, with US tax reform, UK financial deregulation, UK privatization, French privatization, and Vietnam's Doi Moi. NTT moved from the prior year's corporatization into a share-sale process continuing into 1987. None of these institutional changes amounted to completed world-market integration.

In the top-20 Maddison ranking, there were no entries or exits versus 1985. Canada overtook Mexico, Korea overtook Australia, and Argentina overtook Poland. These changes reflected the oil shock, East Asian export acceleration, and rebound dynamics in parts of Latin America. Maddison growth changes are calculated from adjacent source rows; disagreement with IMF or national releases is a measurement question until territory, accounting concept and vintage are reconciled.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

**Highest confidence:** World Bank WDI current-dollar GDP, WDI real GDP growth, WDI population, IMF WEO inflation and current-account indicators for OECD and major market economies, Federal Reserve policy records, GATT trade-volume estimates, and World Bank oil-price analysis. Original Maddison Project Database records, IMF WEO and Macrohistory Database cells, GATT and Banco de México tables, BIS policy and balance-of-payments passages with their footnotes, the Bank of Japan's dated rate table and the Norwegian city indices are also strong on value and definition, which is not the same as perfect historical truth. Shared national-account lineages mean IMF, BIS and national releases are often related rather than independent evidence.

**Medium confidence:** IMF WEO fiscal and debt indicators for 1986, especially where national accounting standards changed later. Current-account data are more reliable than fiscal-balance data for several countries, but still subject to later revision. IMF fiscal and labor data require their exact metadata even when numerically populated.

**Low confidence:** GDP ranking for the former USSR and other planned economies. Maddison estimates are the best consistent long-run source used here, but they are PPP-style historical reconstructions, not market-dollar GDP. Germany is also a special case because WDI/IMF pre-1990 data generally refer to West Germany, while Maddison's Germany series is a historical reconstruction. Unemployment rates are not comparable across OECD, developing, and planned economies. Exchange rates for countries with redenominations, capital controls, or legacy euro conversion are not reliable from WEO GDP-ratio methods.

**Vintage differences.** The IMF WEO indicators in the country tables are the October 2026 vintage; an earlier WEO vintage gives different values in several cases, most notably Japan's gross debt (72.34% against 64.23%), the UK's gross debt (44.93% against 41.36%), Canada's gross debt (71.13% against 69.32%), Japan's current account (4.12% against 3.90%), Korea's current account (2.39% against 2.17%) and the UK's current account (-0.97% against -0.74%), with smaller differences for other CPI, unemployment, fiscal and current-account cells. The earlier vintage also gives values that the October 2026 data in the sources cited do not, including Brazil's unemployment (3.60%), the Netherlands' CPI (0.00%) and China's urban unemployment (2.00%); for Mexico's current account it gives -0.89% against the October 2026 +0.348%, a sign conflict. WDI world GDP ($15.35 trillion) and growth (3.25%) differ from the IMF composites ($14,895 billion; 3.371% and 3.178%). Header growth rates from WDI differ from the Maddison 2023 rates, most notably for Australia (4.59% against 2.07%), China (8.80% against 6.50%), Spain (3.25% against 4.18%), Mexico (-3.75% against -3.06%) and Korea (10.62% against 11.22%).

**Material conflicts.** GATT/1409's reconstructed trade matrix gives world merchandise trade of $2,110 billion for 1986. Mexico's original current account was a $1.270 billion deficit while the October 2026 WEO ratio is +0.348% of GDP, an unresolved sign conflict across vintages. Chinese contemporary GNP growth (7.8%), MPD GDP growth (6.50%), revised IMF growth (8.9%) and WDI growth (8.80%) are different measures, and the employee living-cost index (7%) differs from IMF CPI (6.5%). Pre-unification Germany's series cover different territories: Maddison is combined Germany, while IMF CPI, unemployment and current account cover West Germany and IMF growth is constructed from West German data. Poland's early IMF GDP cannot currently be confirmed by national sources, and the Soviet reconstruction has valuation and boundary uncertainty. Saudi Arabia's growth differs in sign between WDI (+1.01%) and MPD (-6.09%). Oil benchmarks change in 1986 across the BIS, South African and Energy Institute series, so no same-benchmark annual percentage crosses those joins. The Macrohistory Database equity series differ from the S&P 500, Nikkei and FTSE figures because the benchmarks differ. The U.S. discount rate (5.5% at year-end) and federal funds (around 6.25-6.375%) are different instruments. Agreement between WDI and IMF series is not necessarily independent corroboration, since both draw on national sources.

**Important missing data:** comparable 1986 current-dollar GDP for the USSR; harmonized unemployment for China, India, Indonesia, Poland, and the USSR; exact 1986 policy rates for many non-OECD economies; official annual-average exchange rates for most economies; harmonized corporate revenue and market-cap rankings; global real-estate price series; comparable household debt and savings by country; consistent sectoral productivity data; exact annual prices for several commodities and agricultural staples; country-specific M&A, failures, consumer debt, skill wages, R&D, patents and migration; and eight-indicator profiles for East Germany, Czechoslovakia and other historical polities. Data not available means not given in the sources cited, not that the statistic or event never existed.

**Weakest claims:** corporate landscape rankings, non-US and non-Japan equity returns outside major benchmark sources, real-estate conditions outside Japan/US/UK, and micro-level household behavior in planned or high-inflation economies. These are included only as qualified historical interpretation, not as precise statistical claims. Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database values are used under the CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 license.
