# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1934

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1934 was a recovery year inside the Great Depression, but it was not a return to the pre-1929 world economy. The defining fact was divergence. Countries that had left gold earlier, or that had devalued and eased policy, generally recovered faster than countries still tied to gold-bloc orthodoxy. The United States, the United Kingdom, Sweden, Australia, Canada, Germany, Japan, Brazil and Argentina all show meaningful recovery or expansion in reconstructed GDP series. France, the Netherlands, Belgium and Czechoslovakia remained weaker, reflecting the continuing drag of deflation, exchange-rate commitments and trade fragmentation.

The world economy was no longer governed by the late-nineteenth-century liberal model of gold convertibility, freer capital movement and expanding multilateral trade. Instead it was divided into currency blocs, exchange-control systems, imperial preference areas and bilateral clearing arrangements. In the United States, the Gold Reserve Act of 1934 formalized the new monetary regime by moving monetary gold to the Treasury and setting gold at $35 per troy ounce. In Germany, recovery was intertwined with exchange control, public works, rearmament and increasingly coercive labor-market policy. In Japan, the benefits of earlier yen depreciation mixed with imperial expansion and military demand. In the Soviet Union, growth reflected forced industrialization rather than market recovery.

At the micro level, 1934 was a year of selective rebound. Construction, public works, autos, utilities, textiles, mining, armaments-linked heavy industry and some export sectors improved. Agriculture remained weak and uneven: drought in North America, low commodity prices and rural debt burdens limited household purchasing power. Banking systems were more stable than in 1931-1933, but credit did not fully normalize. Equity markets were no longer in free fall, yet they were far below 1929 highs and remained vulnerable to policy uncertainty.

The top economies by reconstructed aggregate GDP were not simply the richest countries. China, India and Indonesia ranked high because of population scale, while their GDP per capita remained low. Conversely, Sweden, Switzerland, Denmark and Australia had high income per person but smaller aggregate output. The Soviet Union and colonial economies require special caution because their GDP estimates depend heavily on modern reconstruction and are not directly comparable with market-economy national accounts.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1934

**1. Recovery became visible, but it was policy-regime dependent.** The most important historical theme was the widening gap between early devaluation or expansionary countries and countries still committed to gold. The United States, Britain, Sweden and Australia had more monetary flexibility than France, Belgium and the Netherlands. The forward linkage was that the gold bloc's delayed adjustment prolonged deflationary pressure until later devaluations and policy shifts.

**2. The gold standard was no longer a coherent global system.** 1934 did not have Bretton Woods, floating fiat currencies or a restored classical gold standard. It had a fractured monetary order. The United States retained an external gold price but restricted domestic gold convertibility; Britain managed sterling outside gold; Japan had already depreciated; Germany used exchange controls; France and several neighbors stayed on gold. This fragmentation shaped trade, capital flows and central-bank behavior.

**3. World trade recovered only partially and under restrictions.** Depression-era tariffs, quotas, import licensing, clearing agreements and imperial preferences remained central. The 1934 U.S. Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act pointed toward a gradual American shift away from unilateral tariff escalation, but the world trading system was still deeply damaged. Countries most affected included export-dependent Argentina, Canada, Australia, the Netherlands, Belgium, Czechoslovakia, Japan and the Dutch East Indies.

**4. State intervention moved from emergency response to structural policy.** New Deal agencies in the United States, IRI in Italy, Nazi public works and controls in Germany, Soviet planning, Japanese military-linked industrial policy and Vargas-era intervention in Brazil all show a larger economic role for the state. This was not one policy model. It ranged from democratic relief spending to authoritarian mobilization and command planning. The common thread was that governments no longer relied on passive market adjustment.

**5. Agriculture remained a drag on global recovery.** Farm prices had improved from the worst depression lows in some markets, but rural debt, drought, commodity surpluses and weak purchasing power persisted. The United States faced severe drought conditions; Canada and Australia remained exposed to wheat and wool prices; Argentina and Brazil depended on export demand; India, China and Indonesia were still overwhelmingly agrarian. Rural weakness limited consumer demand and fed social stress.

**6. Non-market and authoritarian growth created measurement problems.** Germany, the Soviet Union, Italy and Japan all expanded state direction of output. In Germany and Japan, rearmament-linked demand and exchange controls distorted market signals. In the Soviet Union, administered prices and forced allocation make GDP estimates especially uncertain. The forward linkage is that headline growth in these cases often understated future macro risk and overstated household welfare.

**7. Finance stabilized but did not liberalize.** Bank failures and panics were less dominant than in 1931-1933, but capital markets were fragmented. The United States moved toward federal securities regulation and deposit insurance. Germany and many smaller countries used exchange controls. International lending remained cautious. The pre-1929 pattern of large private capital flows did not return.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

**World GDP and growth.** For the 55 MPD-covered countries with data in both years, aggregate GDP was about **5,285,217 million 2011 international-$**, compared with **5,043,480 million** in 1933, implying aggregate growth of about **4.79%**. This is not an official world total. It is a reconstructed country-sum covering the available MPD countries for 1934 and should be used as a directional benchmark. The covered population total is about **1,857.0 million**.

**Global trade volume.** The Federico-Tena world export volume index rose 6.09%, from 94.46 to 100.22 (1913=100), while the League of Nations estimated the rise in the world trade quantum at about 3%; the two series differ in construction and vintage. Contemporary League of Nations publications and later scholarship agree that world trade had begun to stabilize from the 1929-1932 collapse but remained far below late-1920s levels and heavily restricted by tariffs, quotas, exchange controls and bilateral clearing.

**Reserve currency dynamics.** Sterling remained central inside the sterling area and British Empire. The U.S. dollar became more important after devaluation and gold inflows, but the 1934 system was not a modern dollar standard. Gold still mattered for official reserves and credibility, especially for the United States and the gold bloc. The French franc and other gold-bloc currencies were under deflationary pressure because maintaining parity required restrictive adjustment.

**Global inflation trends.** Inflation was not a single global phenomenon. Devaluation countries experienced reflation or stabilization after earlier deflation. Gold-bloc countries remained more deflationary. Germany and Italy used administrative controls that complicate price interpretation. Commodity prices improved unevenly from depression lows but remained weak in many agricultural markets.

**Key commodity prices.** Gold was fixed by the United States at $35 per troy ounce after January 1934. Historical U.S. crude-oil series put crude around roughly $1 per barrel in 1934, depending on series and grade. Wheat prices were highly regional; USDA 1934 wheat reports show country-specific prices and continuing weather-driven stress. Copper and other metals were recovering from depression lows but remained sensitive to industrial demand. **International capital flows.** Capital moved toward perceived safe reserve centers and away from high-risk borrowers, but flows were constrained by exchange controls, capital-market distrust and sovereign debt problems. The pre-1929 pattern of broad private international lending did not return. Germany conserved foreign exchange through controls, while sterling-area countries and gold-bloc countries operated within separate financial circuits.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

**United States.** Monetary conditions were expansionary relative to the depression trough. The Gold Reserve Act changed the gold valuation and strengthened the Treasury's role. Federal Reserve reports describe lower discount rates and abundant reserves, while member-bank borrowing at Reserve Banks remained very low; the New York Reserve Bank discount rate fell from 2% to 1.5%. Federal deposit insurance began on 1 January, with coverage per depositor raised from $2,500 to $5,000 on 1 July. The dollar was not a freely convertible domestic gold currency, but the official gold price became a central anchor.

**United Kingdom.** The United Kingdom continued a cheap-money regime after leaving gold in 1931. Sterling was managed, and the sterling area functioned as a major currency bloc. Low rates supported housing and domestic recovery. External trade remained affected by imperial preference and global trade barriers.

**Germany.** Germany's monetary and credit environment was directed rather than liberal. Exchange controls, bilateral clearing, off-budget finance and state procurement shaped credit allocation. Official interest rates alone do not describe financial conditions. The crucial constraint was foreign exchange, not simply domestic bank liquidity.

**Japan.** Japan had already left gold and benefited from earlier yen depreciation. Fiscal and military-linked demand supported output. Credit and industrial finance were linked to zaibatsu banks and state priorities. Exchange-rate flexibility aided exports but also fed external tensions.

**Stock markets.** U.S. equity prices stabilized compared with the crash years. The Dow-Jones Industrial Stock Price Index began 1934 around 102.85 in the FRED monthly series and ended the year above the late-1933 level, although still far below the 1929 peak. ### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The global trade system in 1934 was fragmented. The United States passed the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act on 12 June, an important policy pivot toward negotiated tariff reductions: it gave temporary authority to change tariffs by up to 50% in bilateral agreements, and the agreement with Cuba was signed on 24 August. The immediate world nonetheless remained dominated by protection. Britain operated within imperial preference arrangements. Germany expanded bilateral clearing and foreign-exchange control. Japan's trade expansion was tied to yen depreciation and imperial policy. Argentina's trade strategy was shaped by access to British meat markets. Colonial economies such as India and the Dutch East Indies remained exposed to metropolitan policy and global commodity demand. Czechoslovakia cut the gold content of its crown by 16⅔% on 17 February. After the United States Silver Purchase Act of 19 June, China imposed a silver export duty in October.

Geopolitical context mattered economically even before the Second World War. Japan's position in Manchuria, Germany's rearmament drive, Italy's authoritarian industrial policy, Soviet planning and the political polarization of Spain all influenced investment, trade expectations and sectoral demand. The Cold War did not yet exist; the relevant geopolitical economy was imperial competition, authoritarian mobilization and the collapse of interwar collective security.

Regional integration in the modern sense was limited. There was no EEC, ASEAN or Bretton Woods system. Integration occurred through empires, currency blocs, customs preferences and bilateral agreements rather than multilateral institutions.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil prices remained low by later standards, and petroleum demand was tied to autos, industry, shipping and military planning. The United States remained the dominant oil producer, while the Dutch East Indies, Venezuela, the Soviet Union and Middle Eastern concessions were increasingly important in strategic thinking. OPEC did not exist.

Metals followed industrial production. Copper, steel, coal and iron ore benefited in countries with industrial recovery, especially the United States, Germany, Japan, Sweden and parts of the British Empire. Gold's price shift was the major monetary commodity event. Provisional Soviet returns show coal output rising from 76.0 to 93.7 million metric tons and steel from 6.8 to 9.6 million tons. Silver mattered particularly for China, where silver-linked monetary arrangements made external silver-market changes a domestic macro issue.

Agriculture was uneven. Wheat, cotton, coffee, sugar, rubber and wool all faced the legacy of surplus, weak demand or weather shocks. U.S. drought made 1934 a major agricultural stress year. Brazil remained tied to coffee, Argentina to meat and grains, Canada and Australia to wheat and pastoral exports, and India, China and Indonesia to agrarian household incomes. An international rubber agreement concluded on 7 May restricted exports from 1 June. Annual sterling quotations from the Federico-Tena commodity price database for selected grades were as follows.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Global labor markets remained depressed even where output recovered. Unemployment data are not comparable across countries because definitions varied: registered unemployed, insured workers, relief rolls, official exclusions and urban-only measures can produce very different rates. The United States, Germany, Britain, Canada, Australia and much of Europe still had high labor-market distress, although Germany's official unemployment decline was partly a result of policy coercion and reclassification.

Demographically, large agrarian populations in China, India and Indonesia kept those economies high in aggregate GDP rankings despite low per-capita output. Urbanization continued in the United States, Europe, Japan, Latin America and colonial port economies, but the depression slowed migration and investment. International migration was far more restricted than before 1914 and politically constrained by unemployment. World population in the Federico-Tena reconstruction on 1991 borders rose 1.14%, by 24.1 million, to 2,146.9 million.

Wages and productivity diverged by regime. In democratic market economies, wage bargaining, relief policy and deflation shaped real wages. In authoritarian economies, labor organization was restricted or abolished. In the Soviet Union, labor allocation and incentives were administrative. Across countries, household consumption lagged behind the strongest industrial production rebounds.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States was the largest economy in the MPD 2023 reconstruction and moved from emergency stabilization in 1933 to a more complex recovery in 1934. The Gold Reserve Act centralized monetary gold at the Treasury and formalized the $35 per troy ounce gold price, which increased the dollar value of gold reserves and supported a reflationary policy regime. Real output recovered strongly in the MPD series, but the level of activity remained below the late-1920s peak and unemployment remained extremely high by any modern standard. The dominant macro story was therefore recovery without normalization.

Fiscal and institutional policy continued to define the year. New Deal spending, public works, emergency relief, deposit insurance, securities regulation, farm programs and industrial codes changed expectations and the structure of federal economic intervention. The recovery was not smooth: drought and crop failure hit farm regions, while industrial production gains slowed after the early rebound from the 1933 trough. Monetary conditions were easy, bank reserves were abundant, and the Federal Reserve's own annual reporting emphasized lower discount rates and small member-bank borrowing at the Reserve Banks.

Official series show the strength of the rebound. BEA real GDP rose 10.81% to $972.263 billion in chained 2017 dollars and nominal GDP reached $66.800 billion, while Federal Reserve industrial production rose 8.39% on annual means. Consumer prices rose 3.1% on average and 1.5% from December to December. After the Gold Reserve Act of 30 January, the monetary gold stock rose $4.202 billion during the year, mainly from the $2.812 billion revaluation and $1.217 billion of net imports and releases from earmark. The temporary federal deposit insurance fund began on 1 January with 12,617 participating banks and covered 14,205 by year-end; on 1 October, 98% of depositors were fully insured but only 44% of deposit liabilities.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:**

Manufacturing, utilities, autos, chemicals, food processing and construction supplied the main cyclical rebound. Agriculture was split between price support and severe weather damage, especially in the Plains. Finance stabilized after the banking crisis, but household balance sheets were cautious and consumer credit was still impaired. Corporate activity centered on survival, restructuring and regulation rather than new equity issuance; the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 created the SEC and changed the microstructure of public markets. Real estate improved unevenly, with residential construction supported by lower rates and federal housing institutions but still far below the 1920s boom. The Securities Exchange Act of 6 June created the Securities and Exchange Commission, the Communications Act of 19 June created the Federal Communications Commission, whose commissioners took office on 11 July, and Executive Order 6757 of 29 June set up the Committee on Economic Security. In the New York City market area, newspaper asking prices for housing fell 3.80% and asking rents 0.52%.

**Economic narrative:**

China was the second-largest economy by aggregate GDP in the MPD 2023 ranking because of its very large population, not because of high income per person. ### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Labor markets were still depression labor markets. Output recovery did not eliminate mass unemployment. In the United States and Canada, unemployment remained severe despite growth. In Britain, joblessness was regionally concentrated in coal, shipbuilding and older industrial districts, although the Bank of England reconstruction shows unemployment falling from 13.93% to 11.73%. In the Soviet Union, labor allocation was administrative and often coercive, so unemployment is not a meaningful market statistic.

Sectoral employment shifted toward public works, construction, state-supported manufacturing, heavy industry and services where recovery was strongest. Agriculture still absorbed vast labor forces in China, India, Indonesia, Poland, Brazil and much of Latin America. Education and skill formation were uneven: advanced industrial economies expanded engineering and technical capacity, while rural literacy and schooling constraints limited productivity growth in many agrarian economies. International migration was restricted by depression politics and immigration controls. ### 6C. Currencies

1934 currencies belonged to distinct regimes. The U.S. dollar was revalued against gold through the $35 per troy ounce price. Sterling floated or was managed outside gold and anchored a currency bloc. The French franc, Belgian franc and Dutch guilder remained gold-bloc currencies, increasing deflationary pressure. The yen had already depreciated after Japan left gold. The Reichsmark had an official value, but exchange controls and clearing agreements made the official rate less informative than access to foreign exchange. Silver remained important for China, creating a distinct vulnerability to silver-price and policy changes.

Capital controls and exchange restrictions were common. This means that simple exchange-rate tables can mislead: official parities, tourist rates, clearing rates and black-market rates could differ, and the ability to obtain foreign currency could matter more than the posted rate.

Annual averages show the scale of the dollar devaluation. Sterling rose 19.46% against the dollar, to $5.04. In the Macrohistory exchange rates, whose quotation timing is source-specific, units of local currency per dollar fell 23.41% for the French franc, 23.24% for the guilder, 23.13% for the Belgian franc, 21.63% for the lira, 13.59% for the yen and 7.14% for the Reichsmark, and fell only 0.75% for the Canadian dollar and 0.54% for the peseta, while the krona weakened slightly, with kronor per dollar up 3.58%. The United States monetary gold stock rose $4.202 billion during 1934, of which $2.812 billion came from the revaluation of gold.

### 6D. Alternative assets

Gold was the key alternative and monetary asset. In the United States, the $35 per troy ounce price was central to policy. Private gold ownership and convertibility rules had changed, so gold was not a freely held domestic hedge in the same way as in earlier periods.

Real estate recovered unevenly. U.S. and British housing benefited from lower rates and policy support, but values and construction remained below boom conditions in many regions. Germany's construction was driven by public works and state employment programs. Agricultural land values remained under pressure in regions hit by drought, debt and low commodity prices. ## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

**Active crises.** The Great Depression was still the central crisis. Unemployment, weak private investment, rural distress and fragmented trade remained active problems. U.S. drought conditions created a major agricultural shock. Spain faced severe political conflict. Gold-bloc countries faced deflationary strain. Germany's recovery carried hidden fiscal, external and political risks. Japan's expansion created geopolitical and trade risks in East Asia.

**Near-misses and vulnerabilities.** The global monetary order remained vulnerable because countries were pursuing incompatible exchange-rate and trade strategies. Gold-bloc deflation could have deepened. Germany's foreign-exchange shortage could have triggered sharper external crisis without controls. Commodity-dependent economies remained exposed to renewed price declines. Banking systems were more stable than in 1931-1933 but still cautious.

**Hindsight-labeled risks.** With hindsight, German rearmament, Japanese militarization and the weakening of collective security were central to the path toward war. Those outcomes should not be presented as mechanically determined by 1934 GDP data, but economic mobilization, exchange controls, autarky policies and armaments demand were already visible. The Soviet command economy's industrial expansion also carried severe welfare and allocation costs that headline growth does not show.

**Policy mistakes visible in retrospect.** Prolonged gold-bloc adherence appears costly because deflation delayed recovery. Protectionism and bilateral clearing helped individual countries conserve foreign exchange but weakened the world trading system. Some public works and relief programs supported demand, but uncertainty and inconsistent policy implementation sometimes reduced private investment. In authoritarian economies, growth policies increased future systemic and geopolitical risk.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1934 vs. 1933)

The main delta from 1933 to 1934 was the move from acute stabilization to uneven recovery. In 1933, the banking, gold and debt crises still dominated global attention. In 1934, recovery was more visible in output data, especially in the United States, Germany, Canada, Brazil, Argentina, Sweden, Australia and the United Kingdom. The MPD-covered aggregate for the 55 economies observed in both years rose by about 4.79%.

The monetary-regime split became clearer. The United States formalized the new gold price and Treasury gold control. Britain continued cheap money and sterling-area management. Gold-bloc Europe remained weak. Germany moved deeper into exchange-control and mobilization economics. Japan continued to benefit from earlier monetary depreciation and state-linked demand.

Trade policy also shifted at the margin. The U.S. Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act opened a path away from pure tariff escalation, but the practical 1934 world was still dominated by protection, quotas and bilateral agreements. Argentina's relationship with Britain, Germany's clearing system, imperial preference and colonial trade rules all show the same broader pattern: trade became political and negotiated rather than broadly multilateral.

The GDP ranking changed little at the top. The United States remained first. China and the Soviet Union ranked second and third in MPD 2023, reflecting large populations and Soviet industrialization. The United Kingdom, Germany and India formed the next cluster, with the United Kingdom moving from 5th to 4th and India from 4th to 6th. At the lower edge, Sweden entered the MPD 2023 top 20 while Switzerland exited. Older Maddison 2010 estimates instead place Mexico at rank 20, demonstrating the sensitivity of lower-rank historical PPP comparisons.

New risks emerged or intensified. Germany's recovery increasingly depended on rearmament and controlled finance. Japan's economy was tied more closely to military and imperial expansion. Spain's domestic conflict worsened. Gold-bloc countries accumulated pressure for eventual devaluation. The United States had strong recovery but still faced unemployment, drought and policy uncertainty.

Matched measures show the divergence. MPD GDP growth swung from -3.40% to +8.37% in the United States, from -7.14% to +10.63% in Canada and from -3.39% to +3.46% in Spain, but from +7.14% to -0.97% in France and from +8.91% to +0.52% in Japan. League of Nations industrial indices rose 25.68% in Germany, 23.08% in Sweden, 22.54% in Canada, 13.53% in Japan, 11.83% in the United Kingdom and 4.35% in the United States, but fell 7.95% in France. United States consumer prices rose 3.1% after falling 5.1%. Official discount rates fell by 0.5 pp in New York, 1 pp in Belgium and 1 pp at the Java Bank and rose 1 pp in Italy, while long-term bond yields fell in every market in the League of Nations table. The sterling price of crude rubber nearly doubled in the year the international restriction scheme began, while wheat fell 11.68% and copper 7.27%.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

**Highest-confidence countries.** 1934 predates modern standardized national accounts for most countries. The United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, Sweden, Netherlands and Belgium have relatively stronger statistical traditions for prices, banking, trade and public finance. Even for these countries, modern GDP series for 1934 are still reconstructed, not contemporary national accounts in the post-1945 sense.

**Medium-confidence countries.** Germany, France, Italy, Japan, Argentina, Brazil, Poland, Czechoslovakia and Spain have useful historical data, but political conditions, changing borders, controls, inflation or deflation, and inconsistent labor definitions reduce comparability. Germany and Italy require special caution because official statistics operated inside authoritarian regimes with policy incentives.

**Lowest-confidence top-20 countries.** China, India, the Soviet Union and Indonesia require the greatest caution. China and India were large agrarian economies with limited modern national accounting. Indonesia was a colonial economy whose data reflect Dutch East Indies administrative and commodity records. The Soviet Union was a non-market command economy with administered prices and politically shaped reporting. For the Soviet Union, GDP comparisons carry substantial, unquantified uncertainty.

**Metrics most often unavailable.** Comparable CPI inflation, unemployment, policy interest rates, government debt-to-GDP, current-account balance, budget deficit-to-GDP and exchange rates are not available in a single verified 1934 cross-country source for all top 20 economies. Some country-specific series exist, but mixing them without harmonization would create false precision, so many country indicator tables show Data not available.

**Where sources conflict.** Maddison 2010 and MPD 2023 differ in calibration and methodology. The upper ranks are stable, but the lower edge of the top 20 differs: MPD 2023 places Sweden at rank 20 and Mexico at rank 22, while older Maddison 2010 places Mexico at rank 20 and Sweden at rank 21. This conflict is explicitly flagged. Commodity prices also differ by market location, grade and unit, so global single-price statements are avoided except for official gold.

**Weakest-evidence claims.** Corporate rankings, global market-cap rankings, sector-by-sector employment shares and real-estate prices are the weakest areas because systematic 1934 global data are Data not available. For many countries, microeconomic descriptions rely on qualitative economic history rather than verified annual statistics. The evidence is strongest for the GDP ranking, monetary-regime context, U.S. gold policy, and the broad gold-standard versus devaluation pattern.

**Indicator sources and definitions.** Policy rates are official discount rates in force at the end of the year as published in the Federal Reserve Bulletin of January 1935, not annual averages; India, Canada, Argentina and Brazil had no operating central bank, and no official policy rate is shown for Australia or the USSR. CPI, unemployment, debt, current-account, budget and exchange-rate entries for most advanced economies come from the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database (Release 6), and equity returns and national house prices from its rate-of-return extension; these data are used under the CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 licence. Exchange rates from the Macrohistory Database are shown as local currency per US dollar, French rates converted to francs of the time and Australian rates to Australian pounds; quotation timing is source-specific. United Kingdom indicators use the Bank of England millennium dataset, United States CPI the Bureau of Labor Statistics series and United States federal debt the Statistical Abstract of 1949. Wholesale-price indices for China, India, the Netherlands Indies, Argentina, Poland and Czechoslovakia are not consumer-price indices and are not used as CPI. Budget balances are Data not available for most economies because central-government fiscal years and coverage are not reconciled with calendar-year nominal GDP; the arithmetic ratios are given as context where the definitions allow. Bond yields are League of Nations annual averages with unstandardized maturities. The Zurich rent index is taken from Historical Statistics of Switzerland Online under the CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 France licence. Soviet output figures for 1934 are provisional returns. League of Nations industrial-production indices are national series on their own bases (Italy 1928=100, an alternative base for the United Kingdom, the Swedish industrial federation index for Sweden, net imports of raw materials for the Netherlands and officially reported output for the USSR), so their changes, not their levels, are comparable across countries.

**Source conflicts.** Maddison 2010 and MPD 2023 share lineage, so the older series is a sensitivity check rather than independent validation. United States real GDP rose 8.37% in MPD 2023 but 10.81% in the BEA chained-dollar series, and United Kingdom real GDP rose 6.61% in MPD 2023 but 5.97% in the Bank of England series. The Federico-Tena world export volume index rises 6.09%, against about 3% in the League of Nations trade quantum. The United States current account is $223 million in the Macrohistory Database against a preliminary $375 million from the Bank for International Settlements. United Kingdom unemployment is 11.73% in the Bank of England dataset against 11.90% in the Macrohistory Database, and public debt 179.49% against 186.64% of GDP. The Dow Jones industrial average rose about 4.14% between year-ends, whereas the national equity reconstruction for the United States, on a different index and timing basis, shows a price fall of 7.12%. The League of Nations index of United States industrial production rises 4.35%, against 8.39% in the Federal Reserve annual-mean series. The Macrohistory short rate for Japan (5.44%) does not reconcile with the official 3.65% discount rate, and the rate-of-return documentation describes British equity coverage as all-share in its summary but as blue-chip in its detailed table. The Macrohistory unemployment entry for Italy is a linked index rather than a rate, the Spanish series has an uncertain denominator, French unemployment covers only industrial wage and salary earners, and the Belgian external balance is a merchandise trade balance. The 1933 United States federal debt implied by the Macrohistory ratio differs slightly from the $22,539 million printed in the Statistical Abstract. The German current-account denominator is linked to gross national product growth for this period, not an observed national-accounts level.

**Territorial definitions.** GDP growth for the former USSR is withheld because the national-income source behind the MPD record has not been matched to the territory of its population series; the raw record change is +9.81%. India corresponds to undivided India, and Indonesia to the MPD Indonesia series, which covers the Dutch East Indies and includes Timor. Levels and growth for 1933 and 1934 come from the same MPD 2023 vintage.
