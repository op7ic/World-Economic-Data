# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1957

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1957 was a late-boom year that turned into an early-downturn year. The world economy began the year still shaped by strong postwar investment demand, reconstruction in Western Europe, rapid Japanese industrial growth, import-substitution drives in Latin America and development planning across newly independent and decolonizing economies. By the end of the year, the United States had entered the recession dated from August 1957 to April 1958, credit conditions were easing after earlier restraint, and policymakers were reassessing how much inflation control could coexist with growth.

The year's largest structural change was European integration. The Treaties of Rome were signed on 25 March 1957, creating the institutional foundation for the European Economic Community and Euratom; the EEC treaty entered into force on 1 January 1958. This did not create an integrated market or eliminate tariffs in 1957, but it changed expectations for trade, investment and scale economies in West Germany, France, Italy, Belgium and the Netherlands. The EEC story was therefore not a one-year output shock; it was a forward-looking regime shift that shaped the next two decades of Western European growth.

The Cold War economic dimension sharpened. The Soviet launch of Sputnik on 4 October 1957 demonstrated a dated technical capability, not a measured contribution to Soviet GDP, and the later US policy responses belong to their own dates. At the same time, the Suez oil disruption of 1956 was still being processed in 1957: the canal reopened to full traffic on 10 April 1957, while inventory, freight and sourcing effects continued. Western Europe learned that oil logistics, canal routes, tanker capacity and Middle Eastern geopolitics were macroeconomic variables, not just foreign-policy issues.

The top 20 economies by 1957 GDP in this report are based primarily on the Maddison Project Database 2023, using real GDP in 2011 international dollars. This produces a PPP ranking of source territorial records, not a modern nominal-dollar ranking and not a certified historical-sovereign ranking. The ranking has important uncertainty: Soviet-bloc and Chinese GDP are reconstructions with substantial, unquantified uncertainty, and a Maddison 2010 cross-check produces a different ordering for China, Germany and the United Kingdom and a different lower tail. The treatment of the Germany aggregate and of whole Pakistan, which included East and West Pakistan, remains material to the historical ordering.

No certified world GDP level or growth rate exists for 1957. A matched sum of 145 MPD 2023 country records rose 3.578% between 1956 and 1957, a useful indicator of the observed cohort rather than a world total. Contemporary BIS tables show the world export-quantum index rising from 124 to 130 (1953=100), approximately +4.84%, and world crude-petroleum production rising from 856 to 899 million metric tons. Contemporary national sources also diverge from the long-run reconstructions: Banxico put Mexico's 1957 real-product growth at about 3.4%, against 7.8% in MPD, and China's National Bureau of Statistics gives 5.1%, against 3.083% in MPD.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1957

**1. The world economy moved from investment boom to cyclical caution.** At the start of 1957, the global economy was still influenced by an intense wave of public and private investment. By the end of the year, US recession and credit easing had changed the tone. US industrial production in the fourth quarter of 1957 was 4.8% below the fourth quarter of 1956. The United States, Canada and parts of the sterling area were most exposed to the slowdown. The forward linkage was the 1958 downturn, which tested postwar stabilization tools and helped normalize more active monetary easing in recessions.

**2. European integration became a durable growth framework.** The Treaties of Rome were signed to establish the EEC and Euratom among Belgium, France, West Germany, Italy, Luxembourg and the Netherlands. The immediate 1957 data did not show the full payoff, and treaty signature was not implementation of tariff elimination in 1957, but firms and policymakers began to plan around a larger market. Germany, France, Italy, Belgium and the Netherlands were most affected. The forward linkage was the common market transition of the 1960s, with scale economies, tariff reduction and intra-European trade deepening.

**3. Japan entered the front rank of industrial catch-up.** Japan overtook India in the MPD 2023 ranking in 1957, reflecting fast industrial growth rather than population scale alone. The Jimmu boom was peaking, and balance-of-payments pressure forced policy restraint: the Bank of Japan raised its official discount rate to 7.665% on 20 March and 8.395% on 8 May. Japan was most affected, but the theme mattered globally because it foreshadowed export competition in steel, ships, machinery, electronics and automobiles.

**4. Development planning and import substitution shaped the non-OECD world.** India, Brazil, Mexico, China and Indonesia all used state direction, public investment, foreign-exchange allocation and industrial policy in different ways. The significance is that 1957 was not simply a market-cycle year; it was also a year in which many governments pursued structural transformation. The forward linkage was mixed: some industrial capacity expanded, but external constraints, inflation and administrative bottlenecks became recurrent problems.

**5. Energy logistics became macroeconomically visible.** The Suez crisis showed how canal closures and pipeline disruptions could change shipping costs, oil availability and industrial confidence. In 1957, the acute disruption eased, but the lesson remained. Western Europe, the United Kingdom, the Netherlands, France, Italy and oil-linked Middle Eastern economies were most affected. The forward linkage was greater attention to tanker capacity, alternative routes, oil diplomacy and strategic energy planning.

**6. Cold War science and dual-use technology entered the growth debate.** Sputnik and FORTRAN made 1957 a landmark year for science-led growth. Sputnik demonstrated technical capability rather than a measured 1957 national-output contribution, while FORTRAN offered a mechanism for lowering scientific and engineering programming costs, without a quantified 1957 aggregate productivity effect. The United States, USSR, Japan and Western Europe were most affected. The forward linkage was expanded education, computing, aerospace-related engineering and public R&D.

**7. Monetary regimes remained Bretton Woods-constrained but not fully liberal.** The dollar-gold system and fixed exchange parities framed policy, but capital controls, exchange restrictions and nonconvertibility still mattered. Sterling pressure, French external imbalance and development-economy exchange controls showed that Bretton Woods was a managed system, not a frictionless fixed-rate world. France introduced a 20% surcharge and premium scheme on 12 August, initially selective and extended on 28 October to all official-market transactions, while Brazil, Argentina and Indonesia operated multiple or free-market rates. The forward linkage was the 1958 move toward current-account convertibility in Western Europe and the longer-run Triffin dilemma.

The observed country cohort still expanded in 1957, but the year was less synchronized than a single global growth figure suggests. Europe and Japan were still in catch-up mode. Latin America pursued investment-led industrialization. The United States slowed into recession. China and the USSR remained outside market-price comparability, which makes their aggregate GDP estimates useful for scale but weak for precise macro interpretation. The matched MPD cohort does not certify that all remaining territories are disjoint, and changing coverage must not be read as measured growth.

**BIS trade tables.** The Bank for International Settlements' Twenty-Eighth Annual Report, published on 9 June 1958, covers 1 April 1957 to 31 March 1958 institutionally but identifies the calendar years of its economic tables. It distinguishes nominal trade turnover from export volume. The 1957 overseas-sterling and Latin American quantum cells cover January to September only, and a separate $102.6 billion export-flow table annualizes the first half of 1957; neither is converted into a complete calendar-year all-country export figure.

**World population.** The UN World Population Prospects 2024 figures are retrospective estimates, not an observed contemporaneous census total. Dividing the incomplete MPD cohort by world population would not give a valid world GDP per capita.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

Major economies operated under Bretton Woods, but the system still included capital controls, exchange restrictions and managed credit. The United States began the year with restrictive monetary conditions; the Federal Reserve discount rate was 3.5 percent from 23 August and 3.0 percent from 15 November, as easing began. The United Kingdom cut Bank Rate to 5% on 7 February, then raised it to 7% on 19 September amid sterling pressure. France faced inflation and external-payments stress and raised its official discount rate to 4% on 12 April and 5% on 13 August. Japan used policy-guided finance and tight credit to address balance-of-payments pressures. West Germany's stronger external position allowed discount-rate cuts during 1957, to 4.5% on 11 January and 4.0% on 19 September, in contrast with French tightening.

Equity markets were mixed. JST equity-return estimates show negative US returns, strong returns in France and Japan, and weakness in Belgium and the Netherlands. Bond markets were also mixed, with long yields still high in several European economies. The policy lesson is that 1957 was not yet a generalized global financial crisis, but it was a year of tighter money, external reserve pressure and late-cycle equity caution.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The Treaties of Rome were the most important trade-regime development. The EEC treaty was signed on 25 March 1957 and entered into force on 1 January 1958, according to EUR-Lex; the BIS report likewise treats the agreement as becoming effective in early 1958. They set up the common-market framework for the Six and linked trade liberalization to institutional integration. The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade remained the broader multilateral setting, but 1957's most durable change was regional.

The BIS also records GATT accession by newly independent Ghana and Malaya during 1957, and the July Australia-Japan agreement removing licensing and tariff discrimination against Japanese goods. These were specific 1957 trade changes rather than early versions of later convertibility or regional organizations.

Geopolitics mattered directly. Suez demonstrated the vulnerability of European oil supply routes. The Cold War shaped public investment, technology competition and trade barriers between blocs. Indonesia's December 1957 takeover of Dutch enterprises showed how decolonization could rapidly change ownership, shipping, trade and foreign-investment conditions. Soviet-bloc economies, China and much of the developing world relied on administered trade and foreign-exchange controls rather than open capital markets.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil was the strategic commodity. The Suez Canal and Iraq Petroleum Company pipeline disruptions had sharply reduced the movement of Middle East oil to the West during the crisis. The UN dates the reopening of Suez to full traffic to 10 April 1957: the closure was a 1956 shock with continuing 1957 inventory, freight and sourcing effects, not a new 1957 war. By 1957 the acute disruption was easing, but shipping routes, tanker availability and oil diplomacy remained macroeconomic risks. Coal remained central in Western Europe, especially for the United Kingdom, Belgium, Germany and Poland, although oil's share in energy systems was rising.

World crude-petroleum production was 899 million metric tons in 1957, against 856 million in 1956, including shale oil and natural gasoline; this is a physical-production estimate (+5.02%), not a crude price or oil-sector real GDP. US crude prices rose by 25-35 cents per barrel in January and were maintained, while lower freight costs softened the landed European impact.

**US oil-price comparator.** The US Energy Information Administration's domestic crude first-purchase annual average was $3.09 per barrel in 1957, versus $2.79 in 1956, a +10.753% change. These nominal national annual averages are not an international grade quotation, December close, freely traded world spot price or investor holding-period return, and overlapping Census and EIA lineage is not independent corroboration of other US price series. They do not fill the gap in world commodity coverage.

Agricultural conditions were mixed. FAO reporting for the period emphasized stock accumulation issues, agricultural export earnings and uneven price trends. The United States had large public stock-management problems, while India and China remained vulnerable to harvest outcomes. Australia and Argentina remained important agricultural exporters. Sugar rose from 3.2 to 6.7 US cents per pound between November 1956 and April 1957 before reversing, and the Canadian wheat export quotation fell nearly 5% from January 1957 to January 1958; source markets and dates differ, so comparable 1957 global price data for all staples are Data not available.

Metals were softer. The BIS reports a copper surplus, a tin quotation of £730 per long ton in October and zinc at US 10 cents per pound in July. These are dated market quotations, not annual global price averages.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor markets in high-income countries were generally tight before the late-year downturn. US unemployment averaged 4.3 percent in the JST series, while the United Kingdom, West Germany, Japan, the Netherlands, Australia and Sweden reported much lower unemployment by their own definitions. These numbers are not strictly comparable because measurement methods differed and some economies had administrative rather than market labor systems.

Demographically, the postwar baby boom, urbanization and migration were central. World population was about 2,852,618,337 on 1 July 1957 in the UN WPP 2024 estimates, up 2.04651% on a year earlier. West Germany, France, Italy, Japan, Mexico, Brazil, India and China all faced different versions of rapid urban-industrial adjustment. Australia and Canada used immigration to expand labor supply and domestic demand. In developing economies, labor-market statistics often understate underemployment because large shares of the population worked in agriculture or informal activity.

**Economic narrative:**

1957 marked the end of the long post-Korean expansion and the start of the 1957-1958 recession. The economy still posted positive annual real GDP growth in the Maddison estimate because weakness arrived late in the year, but durable goods, inventories and industrial production were rolling over by the second half. Inflation was still visible, and monetary policy had been restrictive through much of the year before easing began as recession risk became obvious.

The policy story was a transition from anti-inflation restraint to recession response. The Federal Reserve had raised discount rates to 3.5 percent at all Reserve Banks during 1957, effective 23 August in the BIS table, then cut to 3.0 percent from 15 November. Fiscal policy was not yet countercyclical in the modern sense, but automatic stabilizers and defense-related federal demand cushioned income. The economy remained the dominant world producer and financial center under Bretton Woods, but the year exposed the cyclical sensitivity of automobiles, steel, housing finance and inventory accumulation.

National accounts give calendar-year nominal GDP of $474.039 billion in current dollars and BEA real GDP of 3,215.065 billion chained 2017 dollars, +2.11%; these retrospective national accounts are distinct from the historical PPP reconstruction used for ranking. The Federal Reserve industrial-production annual mean was 21.735 (2017=100), +1.42%, an arithmetic mean of twelve seasonally adjusted monthly observations covering manufacturing, mining and utilities. The late-year reversal is clearer in the BIS tables: industrial production in the fourth quarter of 1957 was 4.8% below the fourth quarter of 1956. The BIS 3.1% during-year CPI rise and the BLS 2.9% December comparison differ by vintage and series and are reported side by side.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:**

Manufacturing was the swing sector, especially automobiles, steel and capital goods. Consumer durable demand weakened as credit became tighter and inventories rose. Computing and aerospace gained visibility through FORTRAN's commercial release in 1957 and the first production Boeing 707 flight on 20 December 1957, distinct from the prototype's earlier flight and from commercial service in 1958; these are dated innovations, not quantified 1957 productivity effects. Credit conditions tightened before the late-year easing; this is not a bank-solvency census. Hourly wage rates rose 2.4% in nominal terms and fell 0.7% in real terms in the BIS table. Philadelphia Fed historical asking-price indices rose 2.694% in New York City and fell 7.762% in Los Angeles (section 6D); commercial real-estate and housing data are not harmonized enough for a confident national assessment.

**Economic narrative:**

The Soviet economy was the second-largest economy in the MPD 2023 ranking, but every aggregate should be treated as a rough PPP reconstruction rather than an official market valuation. 1957 was defined by the coexistence of heavy-industry expansion, administrative reorganization and technological prestige. The launch of Sputnik on 4 October 1957 was not simply a scientific event; it demonstrated technical capability within a non-market system, without identifying a causal GDP contribution.

Khrushchev's reforms shifted industrial administration toward regional economic councils, an attempt to reduce central-bureaucratic bottlenecks while preserving plan allocation. Heavy industry, metallurgy, energy, machinery and space-related engineering remained favored over household consumption. Agricultural performance and consumer living standards remained weaker than headline industrial achievements suggested. Hindsight note: later stagnation debates make 1957 look like a high-capability but increasingly coordination-heavy phase of Soviet growth.

NASA's archive dates Sputnik 1 to 4 October 1957 and NASA's own operating start to 1 October 1958. This dates the engineering milestone, not its contribution to Soviet 1957 GDP, productivity or household consumption, and no causal growth premium is estimated. Contemporary Soviet national-income and gross-industrial-output measures would need reconciliation to MPD's reconstructed GDP before numerical comparison.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:**

The strongest sectors were heavy industry, energy, rail transport, machinery, applied science and state construction. Household consumer goods remained supply-constrained. Corporate events in the market sense are not applicable; enterprises were state units inside ministries or regional councils. Banking, securities markets and private real estate were not comparable to market-economy institutions. Data confidence is low to moderate, with substantial, unquantified uncertainty for GDP and much greater uncertainty for micro indicators.

**Economic narrative:**

1957 was the final year of China's First Five-Year Plan and sits just before the Great Leap Forward. The MPD 2023 estimate places China third by total GDP because of its enormous population, not because of high GDP per capita. ### 5C. Innovation & technology

Sputnik was the year's most visible technology event. The USSR launched Sputnik 1 on 4 October 1957; NASA's institutional creation followed in 1958, with operations beginning on 1 October 1958, rather than being an already operational US agency in 1957. Economically, Sputnik's importance was not the satellite's direct commercial value, but the signal it sent about state-funded education, engineering, rocketry, materials and electronics. It changed expectations about the economic returns to science and technology.

FORTRAN was the major computing milestone. By making high-level programming practical for the IBM 704, it lowered the cost of technical computation and widened the potential user base for computers in science, engineering, finance and government. Boeing dates the first production 707 flight to 20 December 1957; scheduled commercial service came later. This milestone pointed toward a future in which long-distance business travel, air freight and tourism would become cheaper and faster.

These are dated innovations, not a 1957 R&D/GDP, patent-count or productivity series. Infrastructure megaprojects were prominent outside the North Atlantic as well. Brazil's Brasilia project, India's Second Plan industrial investments, Soviet heavy-industry projects and European reconstruction infrastructure all illustrate that 1957 growth was physically capital-intensive.

### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Sectoral employment was shifting from agriculture toward manufacturing and urban services in Japan, Italy, Brazil, Mexico, India and China, though at very different speeds. In the United States and the United Kingdom, the central labor issue was less rural transition and more cyclical adjustment, union bargaining, productivity and sectoral reallocation. In Sweden, Germany and the Netherlands, coordinated wage systems and low unemployment shaped policy.

US bond returns were positive as late-year recession expectations improved the outlook for interest-rate cuts. Japanese long rates were high in the JST series, consistent with policy-guided credit and rapid-growth financial conditions, but the Japanese input is a historical compilation rather than a liquid benchmark. US, UK and other maturities are not harmonized merely by the "long rate" heading, and a complete yield curve and comparable credit spreads are Data not available.

### 6C. Currencies

Bretton Woods fixed exchange rates framed the major market economies. The US dollar remained linked to gold at the official monetary price. Sterling was still a reserve currency but was under pressure in 1957. Continental European currencies were moving toward stronger current-account convertibility, but full freedom of capital movement was not yet in place. France and Spain faced external constraints and exchange controls; France's 20% surcharge and premium scheme from 12 August was extended on 28 October to all official-market transactions. Japan operated under a fixed exchange-rate framework at 360 yen per USD and used credit policy to manage external pressure. Canada's dollar floated.

For the USSR, China, Poland and many developing economies, exchange rates were not market-clearing macro prices. They were administrative, multiple or control-bound, so dated official, free and multiple-rate observations are reported separately. In Brazil the free-market rate moved from approximately 65 to 90.80 cruzeiros per USD during 1957; Argentina's free-market peso was quoted at 40.70 in mid-April, 42.65 in mid-September and 36.55 in mid-December against an official 18-peso parity; Indonesia's June exchange-certificate system replaced a second-quarter range of 11.36-57.38 rupiah per USD. A single annual economy-wide rate is not implied for these economies. Parallel and black-market quotations from JST (France, Japan, Spain and Sweden) appear in the country notes, not as official values.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

**US recession risk materialized.** The NBER-dated recession began in August 1957. At the time, the key visible risks were tighter credit, inventory adjustment, industrial slowdown and weakening durable-goods demand. With hindsight, the downturn became a short but sharp postwar recession; fourth-quarter industrial production was already 4.8% below a year earlier.

**Sterling and external reserve pressure persisted.** The United Kingdom faced the problem of defending an international currency while running a domestic economy under stop-go constraints, and Bank Rate rose to 7% on 19 September. This was not a collapse, but it was a warning sign for the sterling area's longer decline.

**French external imbalance and political strain intensified.** High growth coexisted with inflation, budget pressure and balance-of-payments stress, leading to the August surcharge scheme and its October extension. Hindsight clearly links this to the stabilization and political changes of 1958, although the precise path was not knowable at the start of 1957.

**Suez exposed energy-route fragility.** The immediate crisis came from 1956, but 1957 was when firms and governments adjusted to the lesson. Oil logistics, tanker supply and Middle Eastern routes became strategic economic vulnerabilities.

**Indonesia's December takeover of Dutch enterprises was a corporate and trade shock.** It affected shipping, plantations, trading houses and foreign-investment confidence. This was a major microeconomic event with macro implications for Indonesia and Dutch-linked firms.

**Latin American exchange and inflation stress.** Brazil's free-market cruzeiro depreciated from about 65 to 90.80 per USD, the Buenos Aires cost of living rose about 25.00%, and Argentina relied on an IMF drawing and Export-Import Bank credit. These were country-specific external-financing strains rather than a regional crisis.

**Non-market data opacity was itself a risk.** The USSR, China and Poland reported administrative statistics that were not directly comparable with market national accounts. Hindsight shows that hidden agricultural and allocation problems could be much more serious than headline industrial data suggested.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1957 vs. 1956)

The top 20 membership in MPD 2023 did not change from 1956 to 1957. The main rank changes were Japan overtaking India, Indonesia overtaking Spain, and Sweden overtaking Belgium. World GDP growth is not established from available world observations; the matched 145-record MPD cohort grew 3.578%, but this masked a US downturn, strong Japanese and Western European growth, and development-economy volatility. The world export-quantum index rose about 4.84%, and world population about 2.04651%.

The policy delta was large. 1956 had been dominated by late-boom inflation pressure and the Suez crisis. 1957 brought treaty signatures for integration beginning in 1958, the start of the US recession, Sputnik and renewed awareness of external-payments fragility. Monetary policy in the United States shifted from restraint toward easing late in the year, while the United Kingdom and France remained constrained by external credibility. Discount rates rose during 1957 in the United Kingdom, France, Japan, India, Argentina, Spain, the Netherlands, Sweden and Belgium, while West Germany cut its rate and the United States ended the year where it began.

The structural delta was also large. The EEC was not yet in force in 1957; no causal 1957 output effect is measured, but the treaty established a prospective common-market framework. Japan's rise became harder to ignore. India's plan strategy moved deeper into heavy industry but with short-run strain. Brazil accelerated development investment. China approached the policy turn that would become the Great Leap Forward in 1958. Indonesia moved from postcolonial coexistence with Dutch enterprises toward abrupt economic decolonization.

Risks that resolved: the worst immediate Suez oil disruption eased, with the canal reopened to full traffic on 10 April. Risks that emerged or intensified: US recession, sterling pressure, French stabilization pressure, Indonesia's ownership shock, and the hidden fragility of socialist agricultural systems.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

**Highest-confidence data:** US, UK, Canada, Australia, Sweden, Belgium, Netherlands, Italy, France, Japan and West Germany have the best comparative macro-financial coverage through JST and national/institutional sources, and the BIS year-end discount-rate table supplies dated official policy rates for most of them. Even here, unemployment definitions and fiscal series differ from modern standards, and several JST exchange-rate inputs (France, Japan, Spain and Sweden) are parallel or black-market quotations that are shown only in the notes.

**Moderate-confidence data:** Brazil, Mexico, Argentina, India, Indonesia and Spain have usable long-run GDP reconstructions but weaker comparable 1957 data for inflation, unemployment, fiscal balances, current accounts and corporate conditions. Development planning, import controls and informal labor reduce comparability. City and regional cost-of-living indices (India's 27 towns, São Paulo, Buenos Aires, Djakarta food prices, Mexico's worker basket), Poland's administered cost-of-living index and the Soviet state-retail index are shown in the notes as proxies, not as national CPI values.

**Lowest-confidence data:** Former USSR, China and Poland should be treated as reconstructed non-market estimates. GDP has substantial, unquantified uncertainty; the 20-30 percent error band often used as a rule of thumb is not a source-validated confidence interval. Micro indicators such as profitability, unemployment, credit quality, real estate and consumer confidence are not comparable with market economies.

**GDP-ranking conflict:** MPD 2023 is used as the controlling source for the main ranking. Maddison 2010 gives a different rank order, especially around China, Germany and the United Kingdom, and around the lower end of the top 20, where it places Venezuela and Switzerland. Maddison 2010 and MPD 2023 share a lineage, while OWID and Gapminder redistribute Maddison inputs, so none of these is independent corroboration of the ranking. The PPP bases and revised reconstruction methods differ, and exact sovereign ranking remains unresolved; lower-tail ranks should be read as approximate.

**Material unresolved contrasts:** Mexico's contemporary 3.4% real-product growth versus MPD 7.8%; China's NBS 5.1% versus MPD 3.083%; US CPI vintage and timing differences (BLS annual average 3.3%, December 2.9%, BIS during-year 3.1%, JST 3.4%); UK Bank of England versus JST debt, unemployment and fiscal series; Spain's basic rediscount versus official discount rate; Belgian national versus Belgium-Luxembourg external accounts; Canada's current-account currency-unit ambiguity; central-government versus public-sector fiscal balances; and Zurich's unreconciled rent rebasing. A shared dataset origin is not a second independent source.

**World aggregates:** No certified world GDP level or growth rate is used. The OWID long-run figure of about $12.87 trillion and growth of about 3.8 percent have an unidentified vintage; the matched 145-record MPD sum of 11,790.077 billion 2011 international dollars, +3.578%, is an incomplete-coverage analytical aggregate. Known whole-record exclusions do not certify that all remaining territories are disjoint.

**Historical polities.** Monetary and financial data in the German profile concern the Federal Republic, where the Bundesbank Act became effective on 1 August 1957, replacing the Bank deutscher Länder; no separate West German MPD PPP level is constructed by allocating the combined record. The combined Germany record does not separately measure the German Democratic Republic; BIS reports changes to non-commercial eastern-bloc exchange rates between April and November 1957, but these cannot price a current-dollar GDP or a market exchange return, and separate 1957 GDR GDP, CPI, labor, debt, current account, policy rate and fiscal balance are Data not available. Whole Pakistan included East and West Pakistan; the MPD modern-territory Pakistan and Bangladesh records are not relabelled a sovereign ranking or summed without boundary and timing checks, and a whole-state 1957 profile is Data not available. This gap could affect the historical top 20 even though it does not change the selected record cohort. Russian and other successor-territory reconstructions are not added to the former-USSR aggregate. China, Taiwan and Hong Kong scope, Indonesia and Timor scope, colonial Africa and newly independent Ghana and Malaya require separate political and statistical footprints, and no aggregate fills those gaps.

**Weakest data areas:** global commodity prices beyond gold and the US crude average, exact world trade volume beyond the BIS export-quantum index, corporate league tables, cross-country unemployment comparisons, non-market economy current accounts, and real-estate conditions outside the named US and Norwegian cities. These are either Data not available or described qualitatively.

**Important data not available for 1957:** a certified world GDP and growth series, an independently harmonized historical-sovereign ranking, complete country-by-country CPI and unemployment for the top 20, a same-basis global annual commodity-price table for oil, copper and agricultural staples, a global top-company ranking by revenue or market capitalization, comprehensive exchange equity levels, most city housing observations, and modern-style household balance-sheet data.
