# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1914

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1914 was the year in which the late nineteenth-century global economy broke. In the first half of the year, the world still operated largely within the classical gold-standard order: sterling was the dominant international currency, London was the main clearing center, cross-border capital moved with few peacetime restrictions, and long-distance trade connected industrial Europe, North America, Latin America, and Asian colonial economies. By year-end, the same system was under emergency controls. Major exchanges had closed, gold convertibility was suspended or restricted in many belligerent countries, government war finance began to displace private capital markets, and trade routes were redirected by wartime risk and state priority.

The dominant shock was the outbreak of World War I after the July crisis. The shock was financial before it was fully industrial. Banks and investors tried to repatriate funds, gold moved toward perceived safe centers, sterling came under stress, and securities markets closed in London, New York, Paris, Berlin, and elsewhere. The newly created Federal Reserve System was not yet operating when the crisis began. The United States relied first on Treasury action, emergency currency arrangements, gold management, private clearing-house cooperation, and the closing of the New York Stock Exchange on July 31. The twelve Federal Reserve Banks opened only on November 16, 1914.

The real economy split quickly into three zones. First, belligerent Europe entered war mobilization, with civilian consumption squeezed and government demand rising. Germany, France, Austria-Hungary, Russia, Belgium, and parts of the Balkans faced direct military disruption, mobilization of labor, and transport bottlenecks. Second, the neutral and overseas economies experienced trade and financial shocks but also new demand for food, raw materials, shipping, credit, and later munitions. Third, colonial and semi-colonial economies were exposed through imperial procurement, shipping constraints, commodity price volatility, and the redirection of external trade.

Statistically, 1914 is difficult. Modern national accounts did not yet exist for many countries. GDP figures below are therefore historical reconstructions, mainly in 1990 international dollars from Maddison legacy series, cross-checked against the Maddison Project 2023 documentation and Broadberry-Harrison wartime estimates. For China, Russia, Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman sphere, and many developing economies, the uncertainty is high. Exact 1914 observations are missing in some historical series, so adjacent-year or index-adjusted estimates are explicitly labeled rather than hidden.

Annual measures show the scale of the break. The Federico-Tena world export-volume index fell 13.07% from its 1913 base, and current-price exports of the reporting polities fell 18.22%. The Maddison Project Database 2023 shows real GDP falling 8.26% in the United States, 14.81% in Germany and 7.12% in France. US crude steel output fell 25.75% and US commercial failures rose 13.99%, while coal output fell 34.95% in France and 26.83% in occupied Belgium. Real consumption per person fell in all twelve ranked economies covered by the Barro-Ursua series in the Macrohistory Database.

The most important structural change was the shift in economic power and financial leadership. London remained central in 1914, but the crisis exposed the fragility of sterling-centered finance when Britain became a belligerent. The United States was still formally neutral, but its combination of output scale, gold inflows, and creditor potential made it the natural financial backstop for the Entente over the next few years. In hindsight, 1914 was not just the first year of a war. It was the opening of the long unwinding of the first age of globalization.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1914

**Theme 1: Collapse of the classical gold-standard operating environment.** Before the war, the gold standard linked major currencies through fixed gold parities and helped stabilize exchange rates. In July and August 1914, the system faced a sudden scramble for liquidity and gold. Britain, Germany, France, Russia, and others imposed restrictions, suspended payments, declared moratoria, or shifted note issue to wartime purposes. The Reichsbank stopped redeeming notes in gold on the afternoon of July 31, a stop made retroactively lawful by the law of August 4; France made Banque de France notes forced tender on August 5; Britain's Currency and Bank Notes Act of August 6 issued Treasury notes while keeping gold payable on demand at the Bank of England. The significance is that the gold standard did not simply stop because policymakers changed theory. It stopped because war finance and private liquidity demands became incompatible with peacetime gold convertibility.

Countries most affected were Britain, Germany, France, Russia, Austria-Hungary, Belgium, and the United States. Britain had to protect London bill markets and sterling while financing war. Germany and France rapidly moved toward war controls. Russia entered the war with a large agrarian economy and limited fiscal capacity. The United States stayed on gold but had to manage panic-driven gold exports. The forward link is direct: wartime inflation, blocked convertibility, and postwar attempts to restore prewar parities contributed to interwar monetary instability.

**Theme 2: Financial-market closure as crisis management.** The financial crisis arrived through securities liquidation, exchange-rate pressure, bill-market distress, and fear of gold outflows. The New York Stock Exchange closed on July 31; listed bonds reopened on November 28, selected stocks on December 12 and all stocks on December 15, with minimum prices kept until April 1, 1915. London and continental exchanges also closed or operated under restrictions. This was not a normal bear market. It was a market-function crisis in which authorities stopped trading to prevent forced liquidation and preserve payment systems.

The countries most affected were the United States and Britain because of the scale of cross-border securities and sterling-dollar settlement. The forward link was the rise of New York as a wartime and postwar capital market. With European borrowers increasingly looking outside London, American financial institutions and Treasury policy gained importance. The crisis also showed the value of central-bank liquidity tools just as the Federal Reserve was being born.

**Theme 3: Total war begins to replace liberal globalization.** The prewar economy was not fully free-trade, but it relied heavily on private finance, shipping insurance, commodity exchanges, and imperial trade networks. From August 1914, governments began to prioritize military supply, shipping access, food, coal, metals, rail capacity, and foreign exchange. War demand changed relative prices and business risk. Firms producing steel, chemicals, transport equipment, uniforms, foodstuffs, and financial services connected to government procurement became strategically important.

Germany, France, Britain, Russia, Austria-Hungary, Italy, Japan, and the United States were most affected. The forward link was the emergence of wartime planning institutions, price controls, rationing, and public debt expansion. Some of this apparatus disappeared after 1918, but the precedent for state direction of industrial economies remained.

**Theme 4: Resource mobilization depended on structure, not just size.** Russia, China, India, and the Ottoman lands were large in population and aggregate economic weight, but much of their output was agricultural and difficult to mobilize for industrial warfare. Richer economies had greater fiscal capacity, rail density, industrial capital, banking systems, and skilled labor. Broadberry and Harrison stress that development level influenced military and industrial mobilization, not just population or total GDP.

The most affected countries were Russia, Austria-Hungary, Germany, Britain, France, India, and the United States. The forward link was decisive: industrial capacity, credit access, and food logistics helped determine wartime endurance. The same pattern later shaped interwar debt settlements, reparations disputes, and debates about economic planning.

**Theme 5: Commodity and shipping channels became strategic.** Oil, coal, grain, copper, steel, nitrates, rubber, and shipping insurance moved from commercial variables to strategic constraints. Oil was still far smaller than coal in total energy use, but naval fuel, motor transport, and industrial demand made it increasingly important. U.S. crude first purchase prices were about $0.81 per barrel in 1914, down from $0.95 in 1913, and wartime demand pushed prices higher later in the conflict. Gold remained fixed in the U.S. at $20.67 per troy ounce under the gold standard. On August 10 Britain authorized the Treasury to spend up to 2.2 million pounds on share or loan capital of the Anglo-Persian Oil Company.

The countries most affected included the United States, Britain, Germany, Mexico, the Dutch East Indies, Japan, Russia, and major agricultural exporters such as Argentina, Canada, Australia, and India. The forward link was the wartime rise of state procurement and the later strategic importance of oil and raw materials in diplomacy.

**Theme 6: The United States moved from peripheral reserve participant toward financial leader.** The U.S. economy was already the largest in aggregate GDP by Maddison-style estimates, but London was still the leading international financial center. The 1914 crisis changed the trajectory. The United States had to defend gold convertibility and stop market panic, but its neutrality, productive capacity, and gold position gradually made it the supplier and creditor for European demand.

The countries most affected were the United States, Britain, France, Canada, Argentina, and other economies tied to Atlantic finance. The forward link was the wartime expansion of dollar finance, the later emergence of New York as a rival to London, and the long-run reserve-currency transition that became clearer after World War I and the interwar crises.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

**United States:** The United States entered 1914 without an operating central bank. The Federal Reserve Act had been passed in 1913, but the Reserve Banks opened on November 16, 1914. During the panic, Treasury Secretary William G. McAdoo used gold-management measures, emergency currency authority, and exchange closure to protect convertibility and prevent disorderly foreign liquidation of U.S. securities. U.S. policy was therefore crisis management rather than modern central-bank open-market policy. Once open, the Federal Reserve Bank of New York discounted eligible paper of 30 days or less at 5.5%, reduced to 5% on December 18 and 4.5% on December 23.

**United Kingdom:** Britain faced the center of the global liquidity shock. The Bank of England's Bank Rate moved from 3 percent to 10 percent during the crisis, then was reduced as emergency measures stabilized markets; it stood at 5% at the end of 1914, as at the end of 1913, and averaged 4.04% over the year. The Currency and Bank Notes Act of August 6, 1914 made Treasury one-pound and ten-shilling notes legal tender and effectively withdrew gold coin from ordinary circulation, although section 1(3) kept the notes payable in gold on demand at the Bank of England during office hours. London remained powerful but no longer looked like a frictionless global clearing center.

**Continental Europe:** Germany, France, Russia, and Austria-Hungary shifted rapidly to wartime finance. Gold convertibility and normal discount-market conditions were interrupted. Central banks became fiscal-war institutions as much as monetary stabilizers. The Reichsbank stopped gold redemption on July 31, and the law of August 4 made the exemption retroactive to that date. France's official archival chronology dates forced tender to August 5, with the Banque de France note ceiling raised from 6.8 to 12 billion francs. The Swiss National Bank's 1914 report gives annual-average official discount rates of 4.90% in Germany (5.88% in 1913), 4.22% in France (4.00%), 4.44% in the Netherlands (4.52%), 5.52% in Italy (5.71%) and 5.03% for the Austro-Hungarian Bank (5.95%).

**Japan:** Japan, still a rising industrial and imperial power, joined the war on the Allied side and occupied German positions in Asia, but its financial system was less central to the global liquidity panic than London and New York. The Bank of Japan raised its commercial-bill discount rate from 6.57% to 7.30% on July 6, before the European war began. Japan declared war on Germany on August 23.

**Exchange-rate regime context:** Exchange rates were formally anchored by the gold standard at the start of the year, but crisis-market exchange rates and capital controls made simple parity quotes misleading. Credit conditions tightened sharply in July and August, then loosened selectively where governments and central banks supported bills, banks, or war finance. Stock markets closed in multiple countries, preventing a normal annual equity-return measure. Bond markets became increasingly dominated by war finance.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The defining geopolitical event was World War I. Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia in late July, and escalation brought Germany, Russia, France, Britain, Belgium, Japan, and the Ottoman Empire into the conflict by late 1914. Trade was affected by blockades, naval risk, embargoes, insurance disruption, and the militarization of shipping. World export volume fell 13.07% in the Federico-Tena index.

There were no major liberalizing trade agreements in 1914 comparable to later multilateral agreements. The key change was the opposite: governments began to regulate exports, shipping, finance, and procurement. Regional integration projects such as the later European Community did not exist. Empires, not free-trade blocs, were the key economic regions. The Panama Canal opened to commercial traffic on August 15 for vessels drawing no more than 30 feet, under a US circular of July 31.

Bilateral economic tensions were inseparable from military alliances. Germany's trade access was increasingly constrained by British sea power. Britain and France needed overseas supply and finance. Russia needed external credit and war materials. Japan used the conflict to expand its position in East Asia. Neutral economies such as the United States, Netherlands, Sweden, Switzerland, Spain, Argentina, and Brazil faced the commercial problem of trading amid belligerent pressure. Within the British Empire, a parliamentary resolution of September 16 kept the ordinary pay and charges of Indian expeditionary troops chargeable to Indian revenues, while the cost of replacement forces was to be repaid by Britain.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Global employment data are not available in a comparable annual format. Labor markets changed through military mobilization, transport disruption, and the early reallocation of male labor from civilian industry and agriculture into armies. In Europe, the effect was immediate. In neutral economies, the first effect was uncertainty, then demand shifts toward exports and war-related production. Historical national estimates in the Macrohistory Database rose from 4.3% to 7.9% in the United States, from 2.9% to 7.2% in Germany and from 2.1% to 3.3% in the United Kingdom, but fell from 5.0% to 3.3% in Australia; definitions differ. Lebergott's separate US estimate rises from 4.4% to 8.0% of the civilian labor force.

Migration patterns were interrupted. The prewar Atlantic migration system was heavily affected by shipping disruption and wartime controls. US immigrant admissions in the fiscal year ended June 30, 1914 were 1,218,480 against 1,197,892 a year earlier (+1.72%), a period that ended before the European war began. Urbanization and industrial labor growth continued in the United States, Japan, and parts of Europe, but war redirected labor markets toward state demand. **Economic narrative:**

The U.S. was the largest economy by aggregate output, but not yet the unquestioned center of world finance. The main 1914 story was financial stabilization under neutrality. European liquidation of U.S. securities threatened gold outflows and exchange disorder. The Treasury helped manage liquidity, and the NYSE closure prevented forced liquidation from becoming a disorderly gold drain.

The Federal Reserve's delayed operational timing mattered. The system opened in November, after the worst July-August panic had already been handled through Treasury action, emergency currency, and private cooperation. This made 1914 a bridge between the National Banking era and modern central banking.

The contraction was industrial as well as financial. Crude steel output fell 25.75% to 23.61 million long tons and pig iron 24.65% to 23.33 million long tons, while Dun commercial failures rose from 16,037 to 18,280 (+13.99%) with liabilities up from $272.7 million to $357.9 million (+31.26%); these are business failures, not bank failures. Shiller's monthly stock price series fell from 8.04 in December 1913 to 7.35 in December 1914 (-8.58%), with the exchange closed from July 31 to December.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Agriculture, railroads, steel, oil, banking, and export trade were central. War initially hit finance and trade logistics, but later demand for food, metals, ships, credit, and manufactured goods strengthened U.S. firms. Large corporations such as U.S. Steel, Standard Oil successors, major railroads, and New York banks were strategically positioned, but verified 1914 global company rankings are Data not available. Ford announced the five-dollar day on January 5, combining conditional profit-sharing with an eight-hour day. The Federal Trade Commission Act of September 26 prohibited unfair methods of competition, with the commission operating from March 16, 1915, and the Clayton Act of October 15 addressed stock acquisitions, interlocking directorates and labor organizations. Patent grants and reissues rose 16.94% to 41,850. Macrohistory bank loans rose 4.63% and nominal wages 0.74%, while real consumption per person fell 1.24%. Quality-adjusted newspaper asking prices rose 6.14% in New York City and 2.83% in Boston, while asking rents rose 0.48% in New York and fell 1.20% in Boston.

**Economic narrative:**

China was large in aggregate GDP because of population, but income per head was very low by industrial-economy standards. The economy remained overwhelmingly agrarian, regionally fragmented, and fiscally weak after the 1911 Revolution. The Republic of China existed, but state capacity was limited and national statistical systems were not strong enough for modern macro indicators.

China's 1914 direct exposure to the European financial panic was smaller than that of London or New York, but the war affected trade channels, treaty-port commerce, silver flows, and foreign influence. Japan's entry into the war and actions in Shandong and the Pacific foreshadowed later economic and geopolitical pressure.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The active crisis was World War I and the associated financial crisis. The near-miss was a broader collapse of payment systems and gold convertibility before governments established emergency mechanisms. The NYSE closure, British bank holiday measures, Bank of England market support, Treasury note issue, and U.S. emergency currency all show how close the system came to disorderly liquidation. Stress also reached beyond the main exchanges: Dutch East Indies postal savings lost 13.26% of their July balance in August withdrawals, Argentine deposits fell 19.7% between the ends of 1912 and 1914, and US commercial failures rose 13.99%.

Systemic vulnerabilities visible in hindsight included overreliance on London-centered finance, dependence on gold convertibility during geopolitical shock, high European military commitments, fragile agrarian mobilization in Russia and Austria-Hungary, and the absence of a fully operational U.S. central bank in July 1914.

Policy mistakes visible in retrospect include underestimating the duration and economic scale of the war. Many governments acted as if short-war finance and temporary market controls would be enough. Hindsight shows that 1914 opened a multi-year fiscal, monetary, and industrial transformation.

Tail risks that materialized included exchange closures, gold restrictions, fiscal explosions, invasion of industrial regions, and breakdown of ordinary trade finance. Tail risks that did not fully materialize in 1914 included a permanent collapse of the U.S. banking system and immediate famine in major Western economies.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1914 vs. 1913)

The structural break from 1913 to 1914 was extreme. In 1913, the global economy was still near the peak of the first age of globalization. In 1914, war ended the assumption that capital, shipping, gold, and securities markets would remain continuously open. The change was not gradual; it happened in weeks. World export volume fell 13.07%, and world population grew 0.63%.

The biggest trend reversal was from private international capital mobility toward state-controlled war finance. Britain, France, Germany, Russia, and Austria-Hungary moved from ordinary fiscal management toward emergency borrowing and monetary accommodation. The United States shifted from being exposed to European liquidation to becoming a potential supplier of credit and goods. The Federal Reserve Banks opened on November 16, and annual-average official discount rates fell in the United Kingdom, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands and Austria-Hungary but rose in France, while the Bank of Japan raised its rate in July.

New risks emerged in food supply, shipping, commodity access, and industrial labor. Risks that had seemed manageable in 1913, such as gold outflows or exchange pressure, became system-level crises in 1914. Economic power also shifted subtly. The United States' industrial scale became more valuable because it was outside the European battlefield, while Britain's financial dominance became more vulnerable because Britain was a belligerent.

Country-level GDP deltas show wartime disruption, though source conflicts remain. Maddison legacy rows suggest 1914 declines for the United States, Germany, France, Canada, Belgium, Argentina, Netherlands, Brazil, Spain, and Japan; small gains for the United Kingdom, India, Mexico, and Sweden; and severe reconstructed declines for Russia and Austria-Hungary. The Maddison Project Database 2023 broadly agrees on direction but shows the United Kingdom at -0.12% and Italy at -4.77%, and its rates swung from +3.33% to -8.26% in the United States and from +4.49% to -14.81% in Germany. These values should not be read as fully comparable modern national accounts.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

Reliable statistics are strongest for the United States, United Kingdom, Germany, France, Canada, Australia, Netherlands, Sweden, and Belgium, but even for these countries, 1914 wartime disruption creates conflicts across sources. The biggest conflict in this assessment is the difference between Maddison legacy annual rows and Broadberry-Harrison wartime index tables for some early-war GDP changes. Dated statutes, central-bank reports and official production returns record specific events and quantities, not national aggregates.

Data confidence is weakest for China, British India, Russian Empire, Austria-Hungary, Mexico, Brazil, Dutch East Indies, and other colonial or developing economies. The problem is not that the economies were unimportant. The problem is that modern national accounting systems did not exist, borders changed, and later historians had to reconstruct output from fragmentary fiscal, production, trade, population, and price evidence.

The following figures are estimates rather than official national accounts: nearly all GDP, GDP per capita, and growth values; Russia's 1914 GDP; Austria-Hungary's 1914 GDP; China's use of a 1913 adjacent-year estimate; and colonial territory estimates. For Soviet bloc or pre-Soviet Russian territories and developing economies, uncertainty of plus or minus 20-30 percent is plausible. Russia's level multiplies a former-USSR territorial aggregate by an index for a different territory, and the Broadberry-Harrison table used for Austria-Hungary is headed Austria, so both bridges are uncertain.

**Vintage and unit conflicts:** The levels and growth rates in section 4 are Maddison legacy estimates in 1990 international dollars, while the Notes give Maddison Project Database 2023 rates in 2011 dollars; the 1913 assessment shows 2023-release growth rates and no legacy 1913 rate is available, so the growth columns of the two years are different vintages and no YoY change is computed. The 2023 release's India record covers undivided India dated to 1 October fiscal-year midpoints, its Indonesia record includes Timor, its United Kingdom record includes all of Ireland, its Germany record uses 1870 frontiers, and its Austria record covers only the later republic. It has no 1914 records for China or the former USSR.

**Source conflicts:** For the United Kingdom, the Bank of England millennium dataset and the Macrohistory Database differ on consumer-price inflation (+2.55% against -0.30%), unemployment (3.16% against 3.3%), debt (41.38% against 27.27% of GDP) and the current account (5.29% against 5.51% of GDP); the Macrohistory values are shown and the sources are not blended. Bank Rate averaged 4.04% in the Bank of England dataset and 4.05% in the Swiss National Bank's compilation. Japan's Bank of Japan commercial-bill rate (7.30%) differs from the Macrohistory Japan discount rate (8.61%), and the Macrohistory Japanese house-price index rises 61.40%, a value not used. US unemployment is 7.9% in the Macrohistory Database and 8.0% in Lebergott's estimate, which are not spliced. The Federico-Tena world export-volume index (-13.07%) includes the authors' estimates for missing wartime returns, while the unadjusted reporting-polity sum falls 15.72%. Mexico's exchange series changes currency in August, and the Mexican consumer-price columns are blank. Germany's short-rate, long-yield and equity series, Italy's and Japan's long-yield series and Belgium's short-rate series change source or instrument around 1914. US immigration totals are fiscal years ended June 30, and India's postal savings figures are for fiscal year 1913-14.

**What does not exist in modern form:** Important data that simply does not exist in a modern comparable format for 1914 includes global unemployment, global CPI, exact global GDP growth, comparable policy rates for all top 20 economies, current-account balances for many countries, government debt/GDP for many developing and colonial economies, corporate market-cap rankings, standardized real estate prices, and comparable consumer confidence data. Budget balances relative to GDP are not shown for any economy because fiscal years and government coverage are not reconciled with calendar GDP. Values derived from the Jorda-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database and its rate-of-return extension are used under the CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 license; Federico-Tena commodity-price data are used under CC BY 4.0.
