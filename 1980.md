# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1980

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The world economy of 1980 was defined by the second oil shock, high inflation, monetary tightening, and the first visible turn from the postwar Keynesian demand-management regime toward stricter anti-inflation policy. Oil prices were far above 1978 levels, the Iran-Iraq War began in September, and oil exporters accumulated large surpluses while oil-importing countries absorbed external deficits. Industrial-country output weakened sharply, world trade slowed, and the International Monetary Fund and Bank for International Settlements both described the period as one of unusually difficult adjustment. Current World Bank data put world real GDP growth at 1.82 percent, down from 4.14 percent in 1979, while world GDP in current US dollars rose from $10.181 trillion to $11.480 trillion. The BIS estimated oil exporters' current-account surplus at $108 billion, with a narrower identified investible surplus of $87 billion.

The United States remained the largest economy, but 1980 was not a year of U.S. growth leadership. It was a year of policy transformation. The Federal Reserve's post-October-1979 operating framework, high interest rates, and temporary 1980 credit controls produced a short but important recession. Policy did not move in one direction: the Federal Reserve tightened, imposed credit restraints from 14 March, eased during the downturn and tightened again, ending the year with a 13 percent basic discount rate plus a separate surcharge. Japan's discount rate peaked at 9 percent before falling to 7.25 percent, and Britain cut Minimum Lending Rate from 17 to 14 percent. The United Kingdom was undergoing a similarly visible policy shift under the Thatcher government, with high rates, fiscal restraint, sterling strength, and a deep manufacturing downturn. West Germany and Japan slowed but remained comparatively resilient because of export strength and more disciplined inflation records.

Developing economies split sharply. Iran suffered a major contraction, while Maddison reconstructions show continued expansion in China, India, Brazil and Mexico. Oil exporters such as Saudi Arabia gained income and external surpluses. Oil-importing developing countries faced higher import bills, wider current-account deficits, and greater dependence on foreign borrowing. Brazil and Mexico still appeared dynamic in 1980, but their expansion relied heavily on debt, indexation, state-led investment, and oil-price assumptions. With hindsight, 1980 was one of the key buildup years before the 1982 Latin American debt crisis.

The microeconomic mechanisms behind the macro shock were clear. Energy-intensive manufacturing, automobiles, housing, steel, and shipbuilding struggled in many advanced economies. Oil, gas, coal, infrastructure, defense-linked production, and some electronics sectors performed better. China was still small in nominal current dollars, but 1980 was historically important because special economic zones were formally established, giving the reform era an institutional base for export manufacturing and foreign investment. Toyota's exports rose 29.0 percent while its domestic registrations fell 7.2 percent, US private residential investment fell 18.6 percent, Argentina liquidated a large bank, India nationalized six more banks and Poland's Gdansk agreement of 31 August created an independent union.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1980

**1. The second oil shock became a global balance-sheet shock.** Oil prices had already risen sharply after the Iranian revolution, and in 1980 the Iran-Iraq War added geopolitical risk. The significance is that the oil shock was not just a relative-price move. It redistributed income toward OPEC exporters, pushed oil importers into current-account deficit, increased inflation expectations, and forced monetary authorities to choose between output stabilization and disinflation. Saudi Arabia and other oil exporters benefited, while industrial importers and oil-importing developing countries were most exposed. The forward linkage was petrodollar recycling through international banks and the debt vulnerability of Latin America and other borrowers.

**2. Anti-inflation monetary regimes hardened.** In the United States, the Federal Reserve moved away from interest-rate smoothing toward reserve and money-aggregate control after October 1979, and 1980 showed the real-economy cost. The Bank of England maintained very tight conditions, and the Bundesbank also prioritized inflation control. This matters historically because it marks the transition from the inflation-tolerant 1970s to the disinflationary early 1980s. The countries most affected were the United States, United Kingdom, West Germany, Canada, and other economies tied to dollar or European interest-rate conditions.

**3. Industrial-country recession and trade slowdown exposed structural weaknesses.** Output slowed across the OECD, with especially severe pressure in the U.S. and UK. World trade lost momentum as industrial production weakened and import bills shifted toward oil. Steel, autos, shipbuilding, and construction bore much of the adjustment. This forward-linked to the deeper 1981-1982 downturn, deindustrialization pressures in the UK and U.S. Midwest, and industrial policy debates in Europe.

**4. Oil exporters' surpluses and developing-country deficits widened together.** The same oil-price movement created surpluses in Saudi Arabia and other exporters and deficits in Brazil, India, Turkey, and many smaller importers. International banks recycled those surpluses into sovereign and quasi-sovereign borrowers. In 1980 this still looked manageable. With hindsight, rising dollar interest rates made the structure fragile.

**5. China moved from reform idea to institutional experiment.** The creation of special economic zones in 1980 was not yet macroeconomically large, but it was a decisive micro-institutional step. The significance is that the zones created controlled spaces for foreign capital, export production, wage and managerial experimentation, and local regulatory learning. Shenzhen became the best-known example. The forward linkage was the 1980s and 1990s export-manufacturing boom.

**6. Non-market and exchange-controlled economies strain standard GDP comparison.** The USSR, China, India, and several developing economies cannot be ranked as confidently as OECD economies using exchange-rate GDP. The issue is historical, not merely statistical. Administered prices, controlled exchange rates, rationing, shortages, and non-market allocation break the assumptions behind current-dollar GDP comparison. This theme affects every ranking claim in this document.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

World nominal GDP in 1980 was $11.480 trillion on World Bank current-U.S.-dollar data, against $10.181 trillion in 1979, and world population was 4.437 billion. Real growth slowed materially from the late 1970s as the oil shock and monetary tightening hit industrial output: current WDI world real growth was 1.82 percent against 4.14 percent in 1979. World trade volume growth weakened to about 1.5 percent in the BIS estimate, against about 7 percent in 1979, with weakness in the second half; BIS customs data give partly provisional exports of $1,983 billion and imports of $2,035 billion, and the value of trade was heavily distorted by energy prices.

The BIS trade volume series exclude China, the USSR and specified eastern European economies, while its nominal values include them, and the BIS 1980 trade estimates were partly provisional; the IMF composites use different weights and coverage. The U.S. dollar remained the dominant reserve and invoicing currency, but the post-Bretton-Woods system was now clearly a managed-floating world rather than a fixed-rate order. European countries operated within the young European Monetary System, while many developing economies maintained pegs, crawling pegs, or capital controls.

Inflation was the dominant global macro variable. The U.S., UK, Italy, Spain, Brazil, Mexico, and many oil-importing developing countries faced double-digit consumer inflation. West Germany, Japan, Switzerland, and the Netherlands had lower but still elevated inflation. Commodity conditions were mixed: oil and gold were historically high, food prices were pressured by poor harvests and sugar-market moves, and industrial commodities weakened as recession reduced demand.

International capital flows moved from oil exporters toward international banks and then toward deficit borrowers. This was the petrodollar recycling mechanism. It financed adjustment in the short term but raised duration, currency, and interest-rate risk for borrowers. OPEC current-account surpluses were very large, at about $108 billion for oil exporters in the BIS estimate, while oil-importing developing-country deficits widened sharply.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The United States operated under the Volcker-era anti-inflation regime. Interest rates were volatile and high, credit controls briefly compressed borrowing, and the 1980 recession showed that monetary tightening could have fast effects on durables and housing. The UK maintained very high policy rates and monetary targets, reducing Minimum Lending Rate from 17 percent to 16 percent on 3 July and to 14 percent effective 25 November. West Germany's Bundesbank tightened to defend anti-inflation credibility. Japan also leaned restrictive because oil-imported inflation threatened price stability.

The exchange-rate context was managed floating. Bretton Woods was gone, but pure laissez-faire floating did not describe reality: central banks intervened, European currencies were linked through the EMS, and many developing countries used pegs or controls. Key FX movements included a strong pound, a volatile dollar, oil-linked dollar flows, and pressure on currencies in high-inflation economies.

Credit conditions were tight in advanced economies and increasingly risky for developing borrowers. Equity markets were volatile. U.S. equities recovered in nominal terms at times despite recession, but high rates limited valuation expansion. Bond markets were under pressure because inflation and policy rates kept yields high.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The Tokyo Round of GATT had been concluded in 1979 and was in its implementation phase in 1980: the first of eight annual tariff reductions began in 1980, while the government-procurement and customs-valuation agreements entered into force on 1 January 1981. Its tariff and non-tariff agreements mattered, but the immediate trade environment was dominated by recession, energy prices, and Cold War restrictions. The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in late 1979 affected East-West economic links, technology transfer, and grain-trade politics: President Carter's 4 January address withheld 17 million tonnes of additional grain above an existing eight-million-tonne commitment and restricted high-technology licensing. The Iran-Iraq War added direct energy-market risk, and the UN Security Council adopted Resolution 479 on 28 September.

Regional integration was uneven. The EEC continued to deepen internal arrangements and the EMS provided a framework for currency coordination. ASEAN existed but was not yet a major integrated production bloc. Latin America relied heavily on external bank finance rather than regional integration as a growth engine.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil was the central commodity. U.S. domestic first-purchase crude prices rose from an annual average of $12.64 to $21.59 per barrel (+70.81 percent), while US crude production rose only 0.80 percent, and international oil prices remained historically high. OPEC output fell as demand weakened and conflict disrupted supply. Saudi Arabia's role as a large producer and surplus country was critical. Gold reached a historically extreme nominal peak of $850 per ounce in London on 21 January 1980 as inflation, geopolitical risk, and currency uncertainty drove demand for monetary hedges.

Industrial metals and other cyclicals weakened when industrial production slowed, although price paths varied by metal and country. Energy conservation, fuel-efficient automobiles, coal investment, nuclear power programs, and North Sea oil development were all micro responses to the oil shock.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor-market outcomes diverged. U.S. and UK unemployment rose as recessions and structural adjustment hit manufacturing: US annual unemployment was 7.1 percent against 5.8 percent in 1979, with goods-producing employment especially exposed, and the UK ONS rate rose from 5.4 to 6.8 percent. Japan and Sweden maintained lower open unemployment, partly because of labor-market institutions. Developing-country unemployment data were often weak, with informal employment absorbing shocks. Wage growth often lagged inflation where indexation was absent, reducing real purchasing power. China reported money wages up 14.1 percent, workers' living costs up 7.5 percent and real wages up 6.1 percent for covered workers, while Mexico's minimum wage rose 17.7 percent and its manufacturing mean wage 23.4 percent.

Demographically, world population reached 4.437 billion. China and India had enormous labor forces but low current-dollar GDP per capita. Urbanization continued across Latin America and Asia. Migration to oil-exporting Gulf economies expanded because construction and infrastructure programs required foreign labor. In advanced economies, the baby-boom cohort was entering prime working age, increasing labor-force pressure just as growth slowed.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States was the anchor of the world economy and the center of the anti-inflation policy pivot. The dominant story was not output growth but the collision of high inflation, oil-price pass-through, and a Federal Reserve that accepted a much tighter credit environment than in the earlier 1970s. Credit controls introduced in March 1980 temporarily amplified the downturn, while the Monetary Control Act of 1980 broadened reserve ratios and accelerated deposit-rate deregulation.

National-account vintages differ at the margin: Maddison implies essentially unchanged output (0.05 percent), WDI a small contraction and the BIS contemporary real GNP estimate a fall of 0.2 percent. NBER announced the January peak in June 1980 and identified the July trough only in July 1981. Annual-average CPI inflation was 13.55 percent (12.4 percent December to December in the BIS) and unemployment 7.1 percent. The Federal Reserve imposed a three-point discount surcharge in March and removed it in May, cut the basic rate in stages to 10 percent in July, then raised it to 11 percent in September, 12 percent in November and 13 percent in December, when a three-point surcharge was reimposed; requests for 14 and 15 percent basic rates were rejected on 22 December. Credit restraint began on 14 March, was partly eased on 22 May and its phaseout was announced on 3 July.

At the micro level, interest-sensitive sectors such as housing, automobiles, and durable goods were under pressure. Energy investment, coal, oil services, and parts of the defense and electronics supply chains were stronger. The personal-computer industry remained small in macro terms but became symbolically important: Apple Computer went public in December 1980, showing that venture-backed electronics could access public equity capital at scale.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Banking and thrift institutions faced a mismatch between regulated deposit rates and high market rates. Household purchasing power was squeezed by food, fuel, and borrowing costs. Large manufacturers pushed cost control and productivity programs, while Sun Belt energy and aerospace regions generally outperformed older industrial centers. Contemporary private residential investment fell 18.6 percent. The Chrysler Corporation Loan Guarantee Act was approved on 7 January 1980, with a $1.5 billion ceiling on outstanding loan guarantees rather than a cash grant. Apple's 12 December IPO was priced at $22 per original share, and the 31 March Monetary Control Act broadened reserve arrangements and began gradual deposit-rate deregulation.

**Economic narrative:**

The Soviet economy was one of the largest in the world, but it cannot be ranked cleanly beside market economies using current-dollar exchange rates. Western reconstructions and Maddison-type estimates place it near the top of the global distribution, but the margin of error is large because prices, exchange rates, enterprise accounts, and consumer availability were administered. It is therefore included in the top 20 without a WDI-style dollar figure.

Maddison's former-USSR reconstruction shows nearly stagnant output (0.13 percent). President Carter's 4 January address withheld 17 million tonnes of additional grain above an existing eight-million-tonne commitment and restricted high-technology licensing, rather than prohibiting all grain shipments.

In 1980 the Soviet system was already in its late-stagnation phase. Heavy industry, energy, metals, machinery, defense production, and state construction remained the core. The invasion of Afghanistan in late 1979 sharpened Cold War trade and technology restrictions. High world oil prices supported hard-currency earnings, but the deeper micro problem was weak productivity, poor consumer-goods quality, and the difficulty of transmitting information through planning hierarchies.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** The most important sectoral mechanisms were energy exports, heavy industrial investment, and the military-industrial complex. Households faced queues and limited variety rather than market-price rationing. Innovation existed in selected scientific and defense fields, but diffusion into consumer and enterprise productivity was weak.

**Economic narrative:**

Japan entered 1980 as the leading high-growth industrial economy, but the second oil shock exposed the vulnerability of an import-dependent energy structure. Policy emphasized energy conservation, industrial upgrading, and export competitiveness. The economy continued to move from heavy industry toward automobiles, consumer electronics, precision machinery, and semiconductors.

Maddison growth was 2.82 percent, close to WDI, whereas the BIS contemporary real GNP estimate was 4.2 percent. Annual CPI inflation rose to 7.8 percent and unemployment was 2.0 percent. The Bank of Japan raised its eligible commercial-bill discount rate from 6.25 percent to 7.25 percent on 19 February and 9 percent on 19 March, then cut it to 8.25 percent on 20 August and 7.25 percent on 6 November. The annual-average rate rose 3.47 percent to 226.74 yen per dollar, a yen depreciation.

Micro performance was strongest in internationally competitive manufacturing. Japanese automakers benefited from fuel-efficient product lines just as U.S. consumers faced gasoline and credit constraints. Banks remained central to corporate finance, and industrial groups coordinated long-term investment through main-bank relationships. Real estate and equity-market imbalances that would dominate the late 1980s were not yet the defining issue.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Autos, electronics, machine tools, and semiconductors were key growth sectors. Steel and shipbuilding were more exposed to global overcapacity. Household savings remained high, supporting domestic investment and bank funding. Toyota produced 3,293,344 vehicles, up 9.9 percent, and exported 1,785,445, up 29.0 percent (54.2 percent of production), while domestic registrations fell 7.2 percent to 1,494,470.

### 5B. Corporate landscape

The largest corporate groups in 1980 were concentrated in oil, automobiles, banking, chemicals, utilities, and industrial conglomerates. Exact global top-10 revenue and market-capitalization tables are Data not available, and later rankings should not be retrofitted. What can be stated safely is that oil majors and state oil companies gained revenue scale from high oil prices, while autos and steel were under cyclical pressure. U.S., Japanese, and German manufacturers remained central to global industry.

Major corporate events included Apple Computer's 12 December 1980 IPO at $22 per original share, which became a marker of the emerging personal-computer capital market. Toyota produced 3,293,344 vehicles and exported 1,785,445. The Chrysler Corporation Loan Guarantee Act, approved on 7 January 1980, set a $1.5 billion ceiling on outstanding guarantees. Argentina's Banco de Intercambio Regional was liquidated on 28 March, followed by emergency liquidity arrangements on 3 April, and India nationalized six banks on 15 April. In traditional sectors, national oil companies and state-linked energy firms increased strategic importance. State enterprises remained large in France, Italy, India, China, and much of Latin America. Privatization was not yet the dominant global trend, but the UK policy shift foreshadowed later privatizations.

### 5C. Innovation & technology

The economic impact of 1980 technology came less from immediate GDP size than from future productivity channels. Semiconductors, personal computers, industrial robotics, numerical-control machine tools, telecom switching, and energy-exploration technologies were the most important areas. Japan was strong in consumer electronics, semiconductors, and autos. The United States led in computing, software-adjacent business models, and venture finance. Europe remained strong in telecom equipment, aerospace, chemicals, and precision machinery. China's communiqué recorded 107 state-approved inventions and 675 higher-education institutions with 1.144 million students.

China's special economic zones were an institutional innovation with major economic implications. Guangdong regulations approved on 26 August covered Shenzhen, Zhuhai and Shantou and set a 15 percent enterprise-income tax. They were not a technology breakthrough, but they created a policy technology: a way to test export-led production, foreign investment, local fiscal incentives, and labor practices without immediately rewriting the entire national system.

### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Advanced economies saw manufacturing employment pressure and service-sector resilience. The U.S. and UK showed the harshest visible adjustment in unemployment. Japan's enterprise labor practices and Sweden's active labor-market institutions limited open unemployment. In Latin America, official unemployment did not capture informal-sector adjustment; Mexico's minimum wage rose 17.7 percent and its manufacturing mean wage 23.4 percent. Poland's Gdansk agreement of 31 August marked the Solidarity breakthrough. In China and India, labor abundance, rural underemployment, and state or regulated job allocation were more important than open unemployment.

Union power remained significant in the UK, Italy, Germany, France, and parts of North America. However, 1980 marked a turning point in the bargaining environment: high inflation no longer guaranteed full indexation, and tight monetary policy weakened labor's macro leverage in recession-hit sectors.

### 6C. Currencies

The currency system was a managed float. The dollar was the reserve center, sterling was supported by high interest rates and North Sea oil, the yen and Deutsche mark reflected anti-inflation credibility and trade conditions, and many developing-country currencies were controlled or pegged. Devaluation pressure was strongest in high-inflation and deficit economies. Capital controls remained common outside the most liberal financial centers.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The active crises of 1980 were the oil shock, the Iran-Iraq War, industrial-country recession, and global inflation, together with Argentina's bank failure and Poland's labor crisis; martial law in Poland belongs to 1981. The near-miss risk was that policy tightening could trigger financial distress before inflation expectations were contained. In many countries, policymakers had incomplete information on whether the shock was mainly supply-side, monetary, fiscal, or wage-price institutional.

Systemic vulnerabilities building in 1980 included oil-importing developing-country current-account deficits, floating-rate dollar debt, weak bank risk assessment of sovereign borrowers, and the belief that high oil prices would make some public investment plans self-financing. BIS data show continuing international bank-credit expansion during 1980 rather than a financing stop. Hindsight label: these vulnerabilities became central to the Latin American debt crisis after Mexico's 1982 crisis, but in 1980 the timing and trigger were not yet obvious.

Another hindsight risk was deindustrialization. In the UK and parts of the United States, tight money, strong currencies, and global competition accelerated structural change. Some adjustment was necessary because older industries had low productivity and excess capacity. But the regional social cost was underestimated by many policymakers.

For the Soviet bloc, the visible risk was not a market crash but stagnation: weak productivity, poor consumer incentives, heavy military and industrial burdens, and limited innovation diffusion. In 1980 this was knowable to specialists but not yet widely interpreted as a terminal systemic threat.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1980 vs. 1979)

From 1979 to 1980, the oil shock moved from price surge to global macro adjustment. The average oil-price burden was much higher, and the Iran-Iraq War added a new supply-risk premium. Inflation was no longer treated as a temporary inconvenience. Monetary authorities in the U.S., UK, West Germany, and Japan put more weight on disinflation, even at recessionary cost.

Industrial production weakened more visibly than in 1979. Current WDI world real growth slowed by 2.33 percentage points, US unemployment rose 1.3 points and UK ONS unemployment 1.4 points, and US first-purchase oil prices rose 70.81 percent while US crude output rose only 0.80 percent. Mexico's current-account deficit widened by $1,758.4 million in Banco de México's preliminary table. Britain and Japan ended the year with lower policy rates than their within-year peaks, while the United States tightened again after midyear easing. The United States moved into recession during 1980, the UK downturn deepened, and world trade slowed. Oil-exporter surpluses widened, while oil-importing developing-country deficits and borrowing needs increased. Brazil and Mexico still grew fast, but their risk profile worsened because global interest rates and external debt service moved against them.

China's change from 1979 to 1980 was institutional rather than simply cyclical. Reform experiments gained formal geographic expression through special economic zones. In Europe, the European Monetary System moved from launch to operational reality. In global finance, the shift from inflation accommodation toward inflation-fighting was the most important regime change.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

**Units and vintages.** GDP levels and GDP per capita in the ranking are WDI current-US-dollar estimates and population is a rounded headcount, while growth figures are labelled by source: Maddison Project Database 2023 growth is measured in constant 2011 international dollars and can differ from WDI, IMF WEO or contemporary national-accounts growth, so the two are not mixed in a single calculation.

High-confidence statistics are the WDI current-US-dollar GDP figures for the United States, Japan, West Germany/Germany series, France, United Kingdom, Italy, Canada, Spain, Netherlands, Australia, Sweden, Belgium, and Switzerland. These countries had established national statistical systems, although revisions and territorial definitions still matter.

Medium-confidence entries include Brazil, Mexico, India, China, and Saudi Arabia. Their WDI GDP values are recorded, but exchange controls, administered prices, capital controls, and oil-price effects reduce comparability. China's nominal current-dollar GDP is especially sensitive to official exchange-rate conversion and should not be treated as a full measure of real scale. India's informal economy and administrative controls create similar caution, though less extreme than in a full planning system.

Low-confidence entries include the Soviet Union and Iran. The USSR has no WDI current-dollar country series comparable to the market-economy entries. Maddison Project and CIA-style reconstructions are essential, but they are estimates of real or reconstructed output under administered prices and should be treated with wide error bands. Iran's 1980 data were disrupted by revolution, nationalization, capital flight, sanctions, oil-sector disruption, and war.

Historical data conflict most strongly on three issues. First, nominal exchange-rate rankings differ from PPP or Maddison rankings. Second, unemployment is not comparable across welfare states, command economies, informal economies, and advanced labor-force surveys. Third, fiscal and debt measures are not globally consistent because state enterprises, directed credit, administered prices, and quasi-fiscal losses are treated differently.

Several estimates conflict across sources. US growth is 0.05 percent in Maddison, a small contraction in WDI and -0.2 percent in contemporary BIS real GNP; Japan's BIS real GNP growth of 4.2 percent compares with about 2.8 percent in Maddison and WDI; Mexico's contemporary 8.3 percent compares with 9.11 percent in Maddison; China's 6.9 percent national income growth is a material-product concept; Iran's contraction is about 14 percent in Maddison and about 21.6 percent in WDI and IMF WEO, and IMF WEO warns that pre-1990 accuracy for Iran cannot be confirmed. UK unemployment is 6.8 percent in ONS data, 3.88 percent in WDI and 7.13 percent in IMF WEO. Brazil's current-account deficit is 5.40 percent of GDP in WDI and 8.78 percent in IMF WEO. The Germany, Federal Republic block uses Federal Republic monetary, fiscal and external data; Maddison's 1.14 percent growth and 78.3 million population refer to combined German territory. France's and Britain's debt series change source in 1980, so their year-on-year changes are not computed. WDI exchange rates for Brazil and Mexico are in later redenominated units and are not shown.

Weakest data: exact country-level policy rates, fiscal balances, debt/GDP ratios, and current-account shares for many non-OECD economies. These are often marked Data not available. Important missing data include a matched 1979 hybrid top-20 ranking, standardized 1980 global equity-index returns, comparable real-estate prices beyond a few cities, agricultural benchmark prices, and a clean current-dollar Soviet GDP estimate.
