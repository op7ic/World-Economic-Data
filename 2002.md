# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 2002

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

2002 was a year of weak recovery rather than clean expansion. The world economy was climbing out of the 2001 slowdown, the dot-com and telecom investment bust, the post-September 2001 confidence shock, and the first phase of the corporate accounting crisis. The IMF assessed the recovery as underway from late 2001, led initially by the United States and helped by trade and industrial production, but also described the pace after the first quarter as disappointing. World nominal GDP was about $35.0 trillion in current dollars by World Bank WDI data, while the IMF's September 2002 projections put world real growth at about 2.8 percent and world trade volume growth at about 2.1 percent. The IMF's October 2026 database later estimates PPP-weighted world growth at 2.834 percent, and the WTO's 2003 statistics put merchandise-export volume growth at 3.0 percent after -0.5 percent in 2001; these are later estimates, not information available to policymakers at the time.

The dominant macro pattern was monetary accommodation in the advanced economies. The Federal Reserve cut the federal funds target to 1.25 percent in November 2002. The ECB cut its main refinancing rate to 2.75 percent in December. The Bank of England held its repo rate at 4.0 percent throughout the year, and the Bank of Japan remained in quantitative easing with overnight rates near zero. Easing was not universal: the Reserve Bank of Australia raised its cash-rate target to 4.75 percent by June, and Brazil's central bank raised the Selic target to 25 percent in December to defend the currency and inflation expectations. This policy backdrop supported housing, consumer credit, autos, and government bond markets, but it did not quickly revive capital expenditure. The year therefore combined recovering output with falling equity markets, subdued inflation, and a rising preference for safe fixed-income assets.

The top of the nominal GDP ranking was stable: the United States, Japan, Germany, the United Kingdom, France, China, and Italy remained the largest economies. The more important structural change was not rank reshuffling, but the speed of China's integration after WTO accession. 2002 was China's first full year as a WTO member, and Chinese trade, manufacturing investment, foreign direct investment, and coastal industrialization became even more central to global supply chains. Emerging Asia recovered more strongly than Latin America, where Argentina's crisis and Brazil's election-year market stress exposed regional vulnerabilities. On the Maddison Project Database 2023 constant-2011-international-dollar measure, China ranked second, India fourth, and Indonesia, Turkey, Iran and Thailand were in the top twenty in place of Switzerland, Taiwan, Sweden and Belgium.

Two monetary transitions need careful chronology. Euro notes and coins entered circulation in twelve countries on 1 January 2002, three years after the start of the single monetary policy; the changeover was a cash and payments event, not a new monetary regime. Argentina, after the late-2001 default, ended convertibility by law in January and converted domestic bank deposits and debts into pesos at different rates in February, a reallocation of losses between banks, depositors, debtors and the state.

At the micro level, 2002 was defined by a corporate governance reset, a technology and telecom balance-sheet shakeout, strong housing and consumer-credit channels in several advanced economies, and the continued shift of manufacturing and electronics supply chains toward China, Korea, Taiwan, and other Asian hubs. Sarbanes-Oxley became law in the United States on 30 July after Enron and WorldCom. WorldCom's transfer of operating costs into capital accounts had inflated both its reported profits and its reported investment; its audited FY2002 statements, filed only in 2004, show a net loss of US$9,173 million. WorldCom restatements, Arthur Andersen's collapse, United Airlines' bankruptcy, HP's acquisition of Compaq, and eBay's acquisition of PayPal captured the transition from late-1990s exuberance to balance-sheet repair and platform consolidation.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 2002

**1. A fragile global recovery after the 2001 slowdown.** The world economy did not return to the late-1990s expansion path. Output and trade recovered, but investment remained weak and financial markets remained risk-averse. The recovery was strongest in North America early in the year and in emerging Asia, while the euro area and Japan lagged. WTO regional tables show export volume up 10.5 percent in Asia but down 3.0 percent in North America excluding Mexico. The forward linkage is direct: the same weak investment and easy-money setting shaped 2003, while later hindsight shows that some support came through housing and leverage rather than a broad productivity-led capital spending boom.

**2. Monetary accommodation became the main stabilizer.** Central banks kept or moved policy rates low because inflation was subdued and output recovery was uncertain. The Fed's November cut to 1.25 percent, the ECB's December cut to 2.75 percent, the Bank of England's 4.0 percent stance, and the Bank of Japan's quantitative easing framed global financial conditions. Australia's tightening and Brazil's defensive Selic increases showed that a shared global shock did not imply identical domestic policy reactions, and euro-area members sharing one ECB rate still faced different real borrowing costs because local inflation differed. Low rates supported households, property, autos, and government bonds, while the weak corporate investment cycle persisted. The forward linkage is ambiguous: at the time, easing was a response to downside risk; with hindsight, the same conditions helped build housing and credit vulnerabilities in the United States, Spain, the United Kingdom, Australia, and parts of Europe.

**3. The U.S. dollar turned from strength to depreciation.** BIS described dollar weakening as the salient foreign-exchange feature of 2002 and early 2003. The dollar fell especially against the euro and, to a lesser extent, the yen, reflecting shifting capital flows, lower U.S. rates, falling equity prices, and concern over the U.S. current account deficit. This eased pressure on some exporters to the United States, supported some non-U.S. asset markets, and marked the beginning of a multi-year adjustment in global currency relationships.

**4. China moved from large economy to supply-chain anchor.** China's nominal GDP was already close to France in current dollars and much larger in PPP terms. In 2002 it combined rapid real growth, deflationary price pressure, a tightly managed exchange rate near RMB 8.28 per dollar, strong FDI, and accelerating WTO-related integration. WTO trade tables put China's 2002 merchandise exports at US$325.6 billion and imports at US$295.2 billion. Original Chinese statistics (8.0 percent growth) and later revised estimates disagree numerically, which limits precision about the size of the expansion but not its direction. The forward linkage was decisive: China's industrial expansion became a central force behind global manufacturing relocation, commodity demand, Asian electronics supply chains, and later trade tensions.

**5. Latin America faced acute financial stress.** Argentina's crisis deepened after the end of convertibility, output collapsed, inflation returned, and unemployment surged. Brazil faced election-year market pressure, currency depreciation, and rising sovereign spreads before stabilizing policy commitments helped reduce fears. Mexico was more stable but weak because of U.S. manufacturing dependence. Currency depreciation redistributed gains between exporters, consumers, banks and foreign-currency debtors and mechanically altered nominal-dollar GDP rankings. The forward linkage was a new round of IMF programs, debt restructurings, debates on exchange-rate regimes, and stronger emerging-market reserve accumulation in later years.

**6. Corporate governance and accounting became macro-relevant.** The accounting scandals were not just firm-level events. They damaged equity valuations, increased risk premia, changed audit regulation, and forced a reassessment of reported profits. Sarbanes-Oxley, signed on 30 July, created the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board and increased executive certification duties; enactment, implementing rules and compliance dates were separate events. The forward linkage was a lasting change in U.S. public-company compliance, audit markets, and investor expectations.

**7. Sectoral divergence widened.** Housing, autos, consumer credit, defense/security spending, public services, and parts of energy were resilient. Telecom equipment, internet-related capital spending, corporate IT investment, Japanese banking, German construction, and Latin American sovereign credit were weak. This divergence explains why headline GDP improved while equity markets and business investment remained under stress.

Equity markets were in bear-market conditions. The IMF described sharp industrial-country equity declines after March. The S&P 500, Nasdaq, DAX, CAC 40, FTSE 100, and Nikkei 225 all ended the year lower. Bond markets were stronger because inflation expectations and expected policy rates fell. Yield curves in the United States and several other advanced markets were generally positive, reflecting low short rates and uncertainty over recovery.

Corporate and household credit diverged. The BIS 73rd Annual Report describes mortgage refinancing, lower borrowing costs and household spending as important supports even while business investment was weak, and it viewed the transfer of credit risk through markets as a potential stabilizer. That was the contemporary assessment; the later securitization crisis was not foreseen in it.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The multilateral trade regime was shaped by the Doha Round, but 2002 also saw stronger protectionist pressure. The U.S. Farm Bill and steel safeguard measures were controversial. The European Union continued regional enlargement preparations for the 2004 accession round. China completed its first full year after WTO entry, while the United States and China began a deeper trade relationship that would later become politically contentious. WTO tables report 2002 export-volume growth of 10.5 percent for Asia and -3.0 percent for North America (excluding Mexico); China's merchandise exports were US$325.6 billion and imports US$295.2 billion in value terms. Merchandise trade values are not interchangeable with a current account, which also includes services, income and transfers. Geopolitics mattered through oil and risk premia. The Afghanistan war continued after 2001. Iraq-related uncertainty rose through 2002, affecting oil-price expectations and defense/security spending; the invasion itself belongs to 2003. South Asian security tensions also affected India and Pakistan. Argentina's crisis and Brazil's election-year stress shaped capital flows to Latin America. In Indonesia, the October Bali bombing hit tourism, and in Iran the authorities moved to unify the exchange rate, a reform the IMF's July 2002 mission commended alongside private-bank licensing and tax and foreign-investment reforms.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor markets were weak in several advanced economies even where GDP had stopped contracting. U.S. unemployment rose to about 5.8 percent. Japan's unemployment was near a postwar high around 5.4 percent. Germany, France, Italy, Belgium, and Spain had persistent high unemployment by OECD definitions, while the United Kingdom and the Netherlands had lower measured unemployment but rising concerns about manufacturing and pensions. The country tables use ILO-modeled unemployment from WDI, which differs from registered and national-survey concepts: China's NBS reported a year-end registered urban unemployment rate of 4.0 percent and counted laid-off workers who had not been re-employed separately, so a low registered rate does not establish the absence of labor-market distress.

Demographically, the major structural forces were aging in Japan and Europe, urbanization in China and other emerging economies, and migration into the United States, Spain, the United Kingdom, Canada, and Australia. Urbanization and migration were microeconomic demand drivers for housing, transport, services, and construction. Wage growth was generally moderate in advanced economies, while productivity differences mattered: the United States retained stronger productivity momentum than much of Europe and Japan, even as business investment remained weak. Employment adjustment also operated through layoffs, reduced hours, household debt and migration. **Economic narrative:**

The United States was the main cyclical engine of the early 2002 recovery. Growth was supported by inventory rebuilding, household spending, autos, housing, and past monetary easing. Business fixed investment remained weak because the technology and telecom overinvestment cycle had not fully cleared. The current account deficit widened and became a central global imbalance.

Policy was unusually accommodative. Monetary easing continued even though the formal 2001 recession had ended, because payrolls, profits, equity markets, and investment remained weak. The 6 November FOMC decision cut the federal funds target by 50 basis points to 1.25 percent and set the discount rate at 0.75 percent. Fiscal policy had moved from late-1990s surplus toward deficit through tax cuts, automatic stabilizers, and security-related spending: the IMF general government balance swung by 3.28 points of GDP to a deficit of 3.82 percent.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Residential real estate, mortgage finance, autos, discount retail, health care, and defense/security spending were resilient. Telecom equipment, internet infrastructure, airlines, and corporate IT were weak. WorldCom's accounting scandal, Sarbanes-Oxley (signed 30 July), Arthur Andersen's collapse, and United Airlines' bankruptcy made the corporate-credit and governance channel central to macro risk; WorldCom's June disclosure covered US$3.055 billion of 2001 and US$797 million of first-quarter 2002 expense transfers. eBay's PayPal acquisition and HP's Compaq acquisition signaled platform consolidation and hardware-sector restructuring.

### 5C. Innovation & technology

The innovation story of 2002 was not a single breakthrough but a shift in business models. E-commerce moved from speculative dot-com stories toward platforms with payment integration, marketplace scale, and cash-flow discipline. Enterprise technology spending remained cautious, but outsourcing and software services grew, especially in India. Semiconductor demand recovered from the 2001 downturn, benefiting Korea and Taiwan. Broadband, mobile data, and 3G were still early and capital-intensive, and telecom operators were digesting debt from spectrum auctions and network build-outs. China's NBS reported 252,632 patent applications and 132,401 patent authorizations in 2002 across its combined patent categories; these are not invention patents only and are not directly comparable with USPTO grants. ### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

The global labor market had three major patterns. First, advanced-economy unemployment rose or stayed high even as output recovered, because firms remained cautious after the investment bust. Second, emerging Asian manufacturing and services employment expanded, especially in China, Korea, India, and Taiwan-linked supply chains. Third, migration and urbanization drove construction, household services, transport, and informal-sector activity in the U.S., Spain, Australia, China, and India. Capital-expenditure cuts in telecom reduced supplier orders and skilled employment even as subscriber usage rose, while housing credit supported construction and retail jobs but left tradable-sector labor demand weak.

Skill-biased demand continued. Software, engineering, finance, health, and advanced manufacturing skills were rewarded. Low-skill manufacturing in advanced economies faced pressure from import competition and relocation. Union and labor-market institutions mattered, particularly in Germany, France, Italy, Belgium, and Korea. In Germany, the Hartz process signaled labor-market reform; in Korea, post-crisis restructuring continued to affect employment security. ### 6C. Currencies

The dollar weakened against the euro and yen. The euro moved from below parity to above parity versus the dollar around the turn of 2002-2003. The yen strengthened enough to prompt Japanese official concern. Sterling remained firm. The Swiss franc appreciated on safe-haven demand. Brazil's real depreciated sharply under election and debt stress. China's RMB remained stable near 8.28 per dollar, and Mexico's peso weakened moderately.

In WDI official annual averages, euro units per dollar fell 5.28 percent to 1.0576, sterling units per dollar fell 3.95 percent to 0.6672 and Australian dollars per US dollar fell 4.80 percent to 1.8406, while yen per dollar rose 3.18 percent to 125.39, Brazilian reais per dollar rose 24.29 percent to 2.9204 and Mexican pesos per dollar rose 3.36 percent to 9.656. The yen's annual average weakened even though it strengthened later in the year, so annual averages are not end-year moves or investor returns. Turkey's WDI series is in later redenominated lira, and Iran's official-rate change of about 294 percent is diagnostic only: exchange-rate unification and multiple rates mean it is not a clean market-depreciation measure.

Euro notes and coins were introduced in twelve countries on 1 January 2002; euro-area monetary policy had begun in 1999, with Greece joining in 2001. After a dual-circulation period, the euro was sole legal tender by 1 March. The cash changeover was a payments and retail-pricing event, not a new interest-rate or exchange-rate regime. Currency regimes differed widely. The euro-area countries had a common currency and floating external exchange rate. China maintained a tightly managed exchange rate. Brazil, Mexico, Korea, Australia, Canada, Sweden, Switzerland, Japan, and the UK had floating or managed-floating regimes with varying degrees of intervention.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

**Argentina and regional contagion.** Argentina's crisis was the most severe macro collapse of the year. The end of the currency board, default aftermath, inflation return, unemployment surge, and output collapse affected Uruguay and Paraguay and raised investor concern across Latin America. The sovereign default began in late 2001. Law 25.561, enacted on 6 January 2002 and published on 7 January, removed the key convertibility provisions. Decree 214, dated 3 February and published 4 February, converted bank deposits at ARS 1.40 per US dollar and bank debts at ARS 1 per dollar, with CER inflation-adjustment provisions and a Treasury bond mechanism for the resulting bank imbalance; these were domestic contract-conversion terms, not market exchange rates or the external-debt restructuring. Decree 260 of 8 February established a unified free exchange market. The IMF's July 2003 assessment estimated the 2002 real GDP decline at about 11 percent, with average CPI inflation of 25.9 percent and end-period inflation of 41.9 percent, and attributed the swing to external surplus principally to collapsing imports; the October 2026 WEO reports -10.894 percent real growth. Argentina is outside the 2002 top twenty on both the nominal and MPD measures.

**Brazil election risk.** Brazil's market stress was a near-systemic emerging-market risk because of debt size, foreign-currency exposure, and the possibility of policy discontinuity. The risk did not become a full default, partly because policy commitments and IMF support helped restore confidence, and the December Selic increase to 25 percent showed the cost of defending the currency.

**Corporate accounting and governance crisis.** Enron's 2001 collapse carried into 2002 through audit-market disruption, while WorldCom made accounting credibility a macro-financial issue. This was a realized shock, not merely a risk.

**Japan deflation and banking stress.** Japan's long stagnation remained a systemic risk. The immediate global spillover was limited by Japan's external surplus and low rates, but the domestic banking problem and deflation constrained the world's second-largest economy.

**Bank repair in Turkey, Thailand and Indonesia.** Turkey's bank-repair programme, announced on 11 January, addressed losses and capital shortfalls rather than only liquidity: loans were to be revalued, existing owners were to bear losses and add capital, and the Savings Deposit Insurance Fund could match with equity or convertible subordinated loans, linking bank solvency to fiscal risk. Thailand grew 5.3 percent in the IMF's 2003 consultation (6.149 percent in the October 2026 WEO), but the IMF distinguished approved restructuring from actual cash recovery and completed foreclosure at the Thai Asset Management Corporation, while state specialized institutions expanded credit and home-ownership programmes. Indonesia's March 2003 memorandum with the IMF linked weak tourism to the October 2002 Bali bombing and scheduled Bank Danamon and Bank Mandiri divestment steps for 2003, so those were not completed 2002 privatizations.

**Iran's exchange-rate unification.** The IMF's July 2002 mission commended unification of the exchange rate, private-bank licensing and tax and foreign-investment reforms, and warned that bringing implicit exchange subsidies into the budget could raise measured spending without a new real resource transfer. Its 5.8 percent growth projection for the Iranian year 1381 was a projection, not a calendar-2002 outturn; a EUR 500 million Eurobond was a separate financing event.

**Oil and Iraq risk.** Iraq-related uncertainty raised the risk of oil spikes and confidence shocks. In 2002 this was mostly a risk premium and planning shock; the direct war shock came in 2003.

**Hindsight-labeled vulnerabilities.** Low rates, rising property prices, and household credit growth in the U.S., Spain, the UK, Australia, and Korea were visible in 2002, but the full systemic consequences were not yet broadly understood. With hindsight, 2002 sits inside the early build-up of later housing, mortgage, and household-credit risks. The BIS view at the time that market risk transfer could stabilize the system reflects contemporary beliefs, not foreknowledge of the later securitization crisis. The causes of Argentina's collapse, including fiscal fragility, exchange-rate rigidity, external shocks and a costly dollarized exit, remain debated; the IMF's assessment is one institutional interpretation.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (2002 vs. 2001)

The most important change from 2001 was that output stopped deteriorating globally, but the recovery was weaker and narrower than policymakers hoped. Trade and industrial production recovered from the 2001 contraction, yet investment remained weak and equity markets fell again. This created an unusual combination: positive GDP growth with negative financial-market sentiment. WTO merchandise-export volume growth improved by 3.5 percentage points, from -0.5 to +3.0 percent; the IMF's later PPP-weighted world growth rose by 0.350 point and its market-weighted measure by 0.221 point, the difference reflecting weights.

Monetary policy moved from emergency post-2001 easing into prolonged accommodation. In 2001, rate cuts responded to recession, the technology bust, and September 11. In 2002, central banks kept policy easy because the recovery lacked self-sustaining investment momentum. This was a structural pivot toward lower-for-longer conditions in the main advanced economies, although Australia and Brazil raised rates. Euro cash entered circulation without any change in the monetary-union regime.

Economic power shifted incrementally toward emerging Asia. China did not suddenly become a top economy in 2002, but WTO accession made its existing scale more globally consequential. Korea and Taiwan rebounded with the semiconductor cycle; Korean WDI growth accelerated by 3.0 points and Chinese by 0.9 point, while Germany's moved from positive growth to a small contraction. India showed that services and software could cushion an agricultural shock, though fiscal and infrastructure constraints remained severe.

Latin America moved in the opposite direction. Argentina fell out of the top 20 nominal-dollar ranking after devaluation and output collapse, moving from the late-2001 default to a 2002 currency and contract regime break. Brazil remained in the top 20 but experienced acute financial pressure. Mexico stayed stable but weak because of U.S. dependence and China competition.

Corporate risk changed from speculative overvaluation to trust and balance-sheet repair. In 2001, the main story was the dot-com bust and recession. In 2002, the story became accounting credibility, audit regulation, debt overhang, and the re-pricing of equity risk; WorldCom's final audited restatements arrived only in 2004. Fiscally, the U.S. general government balance deteriorated by 3.3 points of GDP and Brazil's gross debt rose by 8.8 points, while Spain's current-account deficit narrowed by 0.7 point.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

High-confidence data: nominal GDP rankings for IMF-covered countries, world GDP from World Bank WDI, broad IMF WEO global aggregates, major central-bank rate decisions with their dates, Argentina's legal texts and dates, WTO trade tables, WorldCom's filed financial statements, and broad descriptions of global recovery, equity weakness, dollar depreciation, and Latin American stress.

Medium-confidence data: country-level inflation, current accounts, fiscal balances, debt ratios, and population estimates. These are generally available from IMF WEO, World Bank WDI, OECD, national statistical agencies, and central banks, but revisions and definition differences mean that rounded estimates are safer than false precision. The MPD is a retrospective reconstruction, and the IMF's current-vintage history mixes national records and staff estimates; WDI and IMF values share national sources, so agreement between them is not independent corroboration. Fiscal ratios use their own nominal GDP denominators and reporting calendars (April-March for India), never the PPP levels.

Lower-confidence or non-comparable data: unemployment across countries, especially China, India, Mexico, Brazil, and Taiwan. Registered, survey, open-unemployment, metropolitan, ILO-modeled and OECD harmonized measures are not directly comparable. The country tables use WDI ILO-modeled rates and flag national measures in the Notes.
