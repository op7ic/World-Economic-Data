# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1971

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1971 was a hinge year in postwar economic history. The most visible break was monetary: on 15 August, the United States suspended official dollar convertibility into gold, froze wages and prices for 90 days, and imposed a temporary 10 percent import surcharge. The act did not instantly create the floating-rate world, but it made the Bretton Woods gold-dollar system unworkable in its old form. The 18 December Smithsonian Agreement established new central or middle exchange rates with wider margins of 2.25 percent either side, while the United States undertook to change the dollar's gold parity. The $38-per-ounce parity was legislated in March 1972 and became effective on 8 May 1972, so gold-market quotations, the statutory parity and the dollar's exchange value followed three separate chronologies. The repair was fragile from the start.

The real economy was not collapsing. The current World Bank reconstruction puts world real growth at 4.11 percent, against 3.77 percent in 1970, and an earlier vintage of the same aggregate gave about 4.27 percent; either way the year was well above later recession years. The problem was the changing quality of growth. Advanced economies were dealing with simultaneous inflation pressure, weaker productivity momentum, labor conflict, and balance-of-payments tension. Inflation persisted despite spare capacity: U.S. unemployment averaged 5.9 percent while consumer prices rose 4.3 percent, and the Japanese slowdown exposed inventories and excess manufacturing capacity. The earlier postwar formula of fast growth, stable exchange rates, and moderate inflation was becoming harder to maintain.

The microeconomic story was equally transitional. Japan remained one of the fastest growing large industrial economies, although its capital spending cycle began to cool. Brazil, Iran, Turkey, South Korea, and several other middle-income economies expanded rapidly. The United States still dominated global output and corporate scale, but the world was no longer a simple U.S.-led reconstruction economy. European and Japanese exporters had become strong enough to destabilize the reserve-currency bargain that had supported postwar trade. India's agricultural gains, Mexico's weaker investment-led expansion and Argentina's sharp cost-of-living increases show that the year cannot be reduced to one advanced-economy cycle.

Energy and commodities began to move toward the center of macroeconomic risk. OPEC had not yet produced the 1973 oil shock, but the Tehran (14 February) and Tripoli (2 April) agreements showed that oil exporters were gaining bargaining power over posted prices, tax terms, and profit sharing. U.S. first-purchase crude prices rose 6.6 percent to $3.39 per barrel. Gold traded above the old official price in private markets well before the August announcement; the London annual average was about $40.80 per troy ounce. Copper moved the other way: the U.S. producer price averaged about 52.09 cents per pound, and the USGS copper unit value fell about 10 percent from 1970.

For this document, the top 20 ranking uses Maddison Project Database 2023 total GDP estimates in 2011 international dollars. This is a real purchasing-power-based ranking, not a nominal current-dollar ranking. It is used because nominal exchange-rate GDP is not comparable for the Soviet Union, China, India, and other non-market or heavily controlled economies in 1971. Germany combines territories governed by two states, and former-USSR estimates are reconstructions. World Bank and FRED current-dollar data are used as cross-checks where available, especially for the world aggregate and market economies. Soviet bloc estimates are approximate and should be treated as having wide error bands.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1971

**1. The end of the credible gold-dollar anchor.** The central economic theme of 1971 was the collapse of confidence in the dollar's gold convertibility. Bretton Woods had worked while U.S. gold reserves, U.S. output dominance, and foreign confidence were sufficient to support the promise of converting official dollars into gold at $35 per ounce. By 1971, foreign official dollar claims had grown, U.S. inflation had risen, Europe and Japan had become export powers, and U.S. balance-of-payments deficits looked persistent. The United States chose suspension over a forced gold drain. Official conversion ended in August; temporary floats, controls and December central rates followed, while Japan and West Germany faced appreciation pressure and reserve holders accumulated dollar exposure. The forward link is direct: the Smithsonian realignment failed within roughly fifteen months, and generalized floating arrived in 1973.

**2. Inflation before the oil shock.** 1971 is often remembered through the later 1973 oil shock, but inflation pressure was already present. U.S. wage-price controls were a direct response to domestic inflation and political pressure. Major central banks reduced discount rates during the year, yet prices and unemployment rose together in several economies: annual CPI inflation was 9.4 percent in the United Kingdom, 7.5 percent in the Netherlands, 6.4 percent in Japan and 5.4 percent in France. Britain changed its credit-control machinery through Competition and Credit Control. This matters historically because the 1970s inflation was not solely an oil story. Oil amplified an existing policy and wage-price problem.

**3. Exporter power shifts: Europe, Japan, and OPEC.** Western Europe and Japan had recovered from postwar reconstruction and were now strong exporters. Their competitive position weakened the U.S. external balance and made the fixed-dollar system harder to defend. The U.S. surcharge, exchange-rate realignment and European and Japanese resistance linked trade bargaining to monetary adjustment. At the same time, OPEC exporters negotiated higher posted oil prices and more favorable fiscal terms. The shared thread was a redistribution of bargaining power away from the post-1945 U.S. core toward exporters of manufactured goods and strategic commodities.

**4. High-growth industrialization outside the old core.** Brazil, Iran, Turkey, South Korea, Mexico, and Indonesia showed that development momentum was not confined to the North Atlantic or Japan. Brazil's real GDP expansion was especially rapid in Maddison estimates (11.3 percent), and Iran's reached 12.4 percent on oil-sector strength and state-led development. South Korea did not yet rank in the top 20 on the Maddison aggregate used here, but it was already a major high-growth manufacturing case. The forward link is the 1970s debt-and-development cycle: fast growth encouraged borrowing and state-led industrial projects, which later became vulnerable to interest-rate and commodity-price shocks.

**5. Consumer supply inside planned economies.** Contemporary Bank for International Settlements reporting emphasized Soviet unfinished investment and consumer-goods imbalances, and Poland's reversal of food-price rises, higher wages and increased consumer imports after the 1970 unrest. Net material product is not GDP, and administered-price stability is not proof that shortages vanished.

**6. Technology as a small sector with large future consequences.** The commercial debut of Intel's 4004 microprocessor in November and IBM's first floppy-drive sales were not large macro events in the year itself. Their importance was microeconomic and forward-looking. They signaled the beginning of programmable semiconductor logic and portable data storage as commercial building blocks. Nasdaq also began operations on 8 February 1971 as an electronic quotation system, laying groundwork for later market structure change and technology listings.

**7. Geopolitical shocks with economic spillovers.** The South Asian crisis led to Bangladesh's independence and major disruption in Pakistan and India, through refugee movement, relief and defense spending, and interrupted production and transport. The Cold War shaped trade, aid, defense expenditure, technology transfer, and the statistical opacity of non-market economies. In Europe, monetary instability pushed the European Community toward tighter internal currency cooperation. These developments reinforced the idea that macroeconomic history in 1971 cannot be separated from geopolitics.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The U.S. policy environment was easier than in the late 1960s, but the monetary issue was dominated less by domestic interest-rate settings than by the international dollar crisis. Federal funds observations were around 4 percent in early 1971 after the 1970 easing cycle. The decisive policy act was not a rate change, but suspension of gold convertibility and the wage-price-control program. The annual average three-month Treasury bill rate fell from 6.458 percent to 4.348 percent, and the long taxable Treasury series (bonds not due or callable within ten years) fell from 6.58 percent to 5.74 percent. Annual Moody's Aaa and Baa averages were 7.39 and 8.56 percent, a 1.17-point spread against 1.07 points in 1970.

At their final 1971 settings, the U.S. discount rate was 4.50 percent from 17 December, UK Bank Rate 5.00 percent from 2 September, the Bundesbank discount rate 4.00 percent from 23 December and the Bank of Japan discount rate 4.75 percent from 29 December, down 1.00, 2.00, 2.00 and 1.25 percentage points from their 1970 closing settings. These are dated policy instruments, not annual average lending rates, and the U.S. path included a July increase between earlier and later reductions. Bank of England historical data show multiple Bank Rate changes in 1971, reflecting inflation, sterling management, and broader monetary instability. Japan cut its official discount rate in stages as industrial production and investment cooled. West Germany, Switzerland, and other strong-currency economies faced appreciation pressure and large capital inflows as investors moved out of dollars.

Britain's Competition and Credit Control reform changed the mechanism as well as the price of credit. The Bank of England's 10 November lecture described the removal of discount-market agreements from mid-September and a move toward dealing prices and common minimum reserve ratios instead of quantitative lending ceilings; the future role of Bank Rate was still under consideration at the time.

Exchange rates were formally fixed but increasingly unstable until August. After the Nixon measures, several major currencies appreciated against the dollar or were managed under temporary arrangements. The Smithsonian Agreement restored central rates on paper, with wider margins and a commitment to devalue the dollar against gold. Stock markets generally recovered from 1970 weakness. The S&P 500 rose about 10.79 percent in 1971 on a year-end basis, the Dow Jones Industrial Average about 6.11 percent, the Nikkei 225 about 36.57 percent, and the FT 30 calculation used in UK annual-return histories about 41.93 percent.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The largest trade-policy shock was the U.S. 10 percent import surcharge announced with the Nixon program. It was a temporary measure, not a permanent across-the-board tariff regime, meant to force trading partners to revalue currencies and lower trade barriers, and the Smithsonian bargain linked its removal to currency adjustment. The measure signaled how quickly monetary disputes could become trade disputes. GATT's role remained important, but the fixed-rate system that had supported postwar trade was now under strain. The EEC remained a six-member community in 1971; British accession negotiations concerned a future membership change.

Cold War segmentation mattered. The Soviet Union, China, and Eastern Europe were large real economies, but their international price systems, trade channels, and reported statistics were not directly comparable with market economies. The South Asian war affected Pakistan, India, and the new Bangladesh through refugee flows, fiscal pressure, infrastructure disruption, and trade disturbance. The UN ECAFE survey's fiscal 1970/71 chapter on Pakistan describes cyclone, flood and drought effects, import shortages and reduced public development spending, observations that precede or straddle the territorial separation; no reliable calendar-1971 GDP exists for whole Pakistan or for either successor state. European integration gained a monetary dimension as the EC tried to limit internal exchange-rate volatility after the dollar shock.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil was still cheap compared with later 1970s levels, but the pricing regime was changing. OPEC producers won higher posted prices and better terms in 1971, including the Tehran and Tripoli settlements, and the BIS attributed part of the year's oil-import value growth to them. This was a bargaining-power shift before it was a supply shock. The 1973 oil crisis did not come out of nowhere; 1971 showed the institutional route by which producer governments could translate resource control into price and fiscal leverage.

Gold moved from official anchor to market signal. The London annual average of about $40.80 per ounce was above the old official $35 level, and the BIS reported London fixing quotations near $41.75 on 14 May, nearly $44 on 9 August and about $43.50 late in December. ### 3E. Labor & Demographics

World population was about 3.77 billion. Demographic pressure remained strongest in Asia, Africa, and Latin America, while many high-income countries were beginning to experience the later stages of postwar baby-boom labor-market entry. In the United States and Europe, labor bargaining and wage indexation contributed to inflation persistence. Unemployment definitions varied sharply by country, so direct comparisons across the top 20 are not reliable: the U.S. 5.9 percent average and Japan's 1.2 percent historical rate do not imply equivalent sampling, Britain's 3.4 percent in the Macrohistory Database differs from the ONS retrospective 4.1 percent, and ECAFE reports Iran's fiscal 1970/71 unemployment at 10.7 percent excluding underemployment. Refugee movements in South Asia affected labor supply and public services; a worldwide migration total is not available.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States remained the world's largest economy by a wide margin. Its 1971 story was not weak output, but weakening monetary credibility. The U.S. economy was recovering from 1970 sluggishness while inflation and external deficits created a political crisis around the dollar. Employment stayed weak: the Economic Report of the President gives an unemployment average of 5.9 percent against 4.9 percent in 1970, while annual Treasury bill yields fell.

The Nixon program of 15 August combined domestic stabilization, tax support, wage-price controls, dollar defense, and trade pressure. Microeconomically, controls altered pricing behavior in retailing, manufacturing, and labor bargaining. Treasury borrowing conditions eased on annual averages, yet the Aaa-Baa corporate spread widened slightly. Federal fiscal flows in the Macrohistory Database were $187.1 billion revenue and $210.2 billion expenditure; they are on a fiscal-year basis and are not converted into a calendar-year budget ratio. The Smithsonian central-rate bargain did not restore gold convertibility.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Autos and steel were mature and internationally challenged; aerospace, computing, electronics, finance, and consumer durables remained globally important. Nasdaq began operations on 8 February as an electronic quotation system, Intel commercialized the 4004 in November, and IBM began floppy-drive sales. Housing and household credit were sensitive to interest-rate conditions; newspaper asking-price indices rose 5.9 percent in Boston, 1.8 percent in New York City and 6.1 percent in San Francisco. Corporate America still dominated global revenue rankings, but Japanese and European competition was no longer secondary.

The Soviet Union was the second-largest real economy in the Maddison ranking, but this figure should be read with caution. Output was planned, prices were administered, and military, heavy industry, and agriculture were measured through systems not directly comparable with market GDP. The economy was geopolitically powerful but statistically opaque.

The most important failure case was Rolls-Royce. On 4 February the government told Parliament that RB211 commitments and termination losses threatened the company's tangible resources; the board sought a receiver, and the state proposed acquiring the essential engine assets. Other corporate and market-structure developments included the opening of Nasdaq on 8 February as an electronic quotation system (not yet a fully electronic execution model), the Intel 4004 microprocessor, IBM floppy-drive commercialization, and continued expansion of Japanese auto and electronics firms. Intel's acquisition of broader commercialization rights in its Busicom relationship was the business-model mechanism behind the 4004. These did not dominate 1971 GDP, but they reshaped the production frontier for later decades.

### 5C. Innovation & technology

The Intel 4004 was a programmable logic microchip with long-run importance for computing, embedded systems, and semiconductor business models; it became commercially available in November, and its calculator-related development and reuse rights explain how a specialized contract could support a broader product. IBM's floppy disk helped make data loading and storage more practical; IBM dates drive sales to 1971 and patents to 1972. Nasdaq's electronic quotation model changed securities market information flows. The economic significance in 1971 was mostly option value: these technologies created platforms that would matter far more in the 1980s and 1990s. ### 6B. Fixed income

U.S. Treasury yields remained high relative to the immediate postwar decades, reflecting inflation and monetary uncertainty, but fell on annual averages: three-month bills 4.348 percent and long taxable Treasuries 5.74 percent. December 1971 monthly means were 4.023 percent for bills, 5.62 percent for long Treasuries, 7.25 percent for Moody's Aaa and 8.38 percent for Baa; these are monthly averages, not year-end closes. The annual Aaa-Baa spread widened to 1.17 points from 1.07. Spain's long rate of 3.37 percent in the Macrohistory Database is a perpetual-debt current yield and is not comparable to a ten-year benchmark. A precise cross-country 10-year government bond table for all major economies is not available. Sovereign-debt stress was not the central story in 1971, but external borrowing channels for developing economies were expanding and would matter later in the decade.

### 6C. Currencies

Currencies were the main financial-market story. The U.S. closed the gold window in August. The Smithsonian Agreement of 18 December set central or middle rates of 308 yen, 3.2225 Deutsche marks, 5.1157 French francs, 581.50 lire and 3.2447 guilders per dollar, with sterling at $2.60571 per pound; permitted margins were 2.25 percent either side of central rates. These are settlement rates, not annual averages or year-end market closes. The agreement committed the United States to raise the official gold price to $38 per ounce, which took legal effect in May 1972. Germany, Japan, and other strong exporters faced appreciation pressure. Canada remained floating at 99.79 U.S. cents at year-end. Argentina introduced a dual market in September with a commercial rate of 5 pesos per dollar, and Indonesia adjusted its rupiah rates on 23 August. Some authorities used intervention or capital controls to slow destabilizing inflows.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The largest active crisis was the Bretton Woods breakdown. At the time, policymakers framed the August measures as temporary and the Smithsonian Agreement as a major repair. With hindsight, the deeper risk was that the fixed-rate system required a level of U.S. external discipline and global confidence that no longer existed. Export contracts, short-term capital movements and reserve accumulation transmitted pressure across currencies.

The second major risk was inflation becoming embedded in wage and price behavior. Wage-price controls could temporarily suppress measured inflation, but they did not solve monetary, fiscal, productivity, or bargaining causes. With hindsight, controls delayed some price adjustment and complicated later policy. Inflation coexisting with weak employment was already visible.

The third risk was energy bargaining power. 1971 did not produce the 1973 oil embargo, but it showed that producer governments could coordinate to raise posted prices and fiscal take. This was a structural shift in global income distribution.

The fourth risk was corporate and geopolitical disruption. Rolls-Royce exposed the interaction of technical development risk, fixed contractual prices, strategic supply chains and state support; contemporary ministers and critics disagreed about responsibility and the appropriate rescue. The Bangladesh war, Cold War alliances, and the China-U.S. diplomatic opening affected trade, aid, refugee flows, and defense budgets. These risks materialized unevenly, but they made the world economy less predictable than the 1960s growth model suggested.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1971 vs. 1970)

The key structural break from 1970 to 1971 was the move from strained Bretton Woods to suspended convertibility. In 1970, fixed exchange rates were under pressure but still formally anchored by the dollar-gold promise. In 1971, that anchor was suspended, and exchange-rate bargaining became explicit; the December settlement was a new arrangement, not a completed statutory gold-price change.

World output growth improved slightly compared with 1970: from 3.77 percent to 4.11 percent in the current WDI reconstruction (an earlier vintage gave about 3.75 to 4.27 percent). The current-dollar GDP rise of 10.51 percent is a different measure. The year was not a global recession; the deterioration was institutional rather than simply cyclical. U.S. unemployment rose from 4.9 to 5.9 percent while annual bill yields fell, so recovery and labor-market slack coexisted. Mexico's slowdown is measured against revised 1970 growth of 6.9 percent, not 7.7 percent, and Argentina's annual cost-of-living increase rose from 13.6 to 34.7 percent.

Commodity power also shifted. Oil producer negotiations in 1971 were more assertive than in 1970, and U.S. first-purchase crude prices rose 6.6 percent. Gold rose from a 1970 annual average near $35.94 to about $40.80, reflecting the weakening of the old parity, while USGS copper and silver unit values fell 10.2 and 12.4 percent, so there was no single commodity direction. Equity markets generally recovered from the 1970 downturn, especially in Japan and the United Kingdom.

Within the top 20 Maddison ranking, France moved ahead of the United Kingdom. Turkey entered the top 20 versus 1970, replacing Venezuela. The United States remained first; the Soviet Union remained second; Japan remained third. The biggest growth rates among the top 20 were Iran, Brazil, Turkey, and Poland, though the Polish and Soviet-bloc numbers require caution. Spain's debt series changes source in 1971, so its cross-year movement is not computed, and the South Asian territorial break prevents unqualified successor-state growth comparisons.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

High-confidence data: U.S. GDP, world GDP in current U.S. dollars, broad World Bank WDI aggregates, Federal Reserve historical descriptions of the Nixon shock, dated central-bank policy-rate actions, the Smithsonian central rates, Economic Report of the President interest-rate and equity-average tables, gold annual average price, and major U.S. and Japanese equity-index annual returns.

Medium-confidence data: Maddison Project real GDP rankings for market economies. These are reconstructed estimates, but the methodology is transparent and widely used. Macrohistory Database debt, unemployment and current-account series use national historical definitions; WDI and Macrohistory current-account ratios are drawn from different sources and may share underlying national inputs.

Low-confidence data: Soviet Union, China, Poland, and other non-market economies. Maddison estimates are useful for scale but should be treated as approximate. Error bands of 20-30 percent are plausible for some non-market economies and may be larger for some indicators. BIS net material product estimates for the USSR, Poland and East Germany are a different concept from GDP. Germany's GDP covers two states, so West German indicators are given only as notes.

Major gaps: unemployment rates are not comparable across countries in 1971; government debt-to-GDP, current-account and CPI data are missing for several non-OECD members of the top 20; budget balances are not harmonized because fiscal years and government coverage differ from calendar GDP; corporate revenue and market-cap rankings, M&A and IPO lists are not available from a reliable global source; global agricultural prices, R&D and patent comparisons, and real estate prices by major city are not available in a harmonized global source. Indian, Iranian, Indonesian and Pakistani data in the ECAFE survey are fiscal-year and partly preliminary, and their 1971/72 outlook statements are forecasts.

Conflicting data: nominal GDP rankings differ from Maddison PPP rankings because official exchange rates, price levels, and non-market valuation methods differ. World Bank/FRED current-dollar data put the United States first and show Japan and Germany as close, while Maddison PPP estimates put Japan ahead of the combined-territory Germany estimate. This document ranks by Maddison real PPP output because it is the only practical way to include the USSR, China, and India on a comparable historical basis. World real growth is 4.11 percent in the current WDI vintage against about 4.27 percent in an earlier one. The S&P 500's +10.79 percent year-end change and the +18.11 percent rise in its annual average measure different things. UK unemployment is 3.4 percent in the Macrohistory Database and 4.1 percent in the ONS retrospective series. Spain's government debt is 14.9 percent of GDP in the AMECO-based series, while the WDI series returns an implausible 1,768.6 percent, which is treated as a definitional anomaly and not used. The $38 gold parity agreed in December became legally effective only on 8 May 1972; the presidential signing statement is dated 3 April 1972, after the statute's 31 March approval date, which governs. Copper prices fell rather than strengthened relative to 1970 on the USGS unit-value benchmark. Mexico's 1970 growth was revised from 7.7 to 6.9 percent in the Banco de México report.
