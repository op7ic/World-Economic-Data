# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1941

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1941 was defined economically by the global transition from partial war mobilization to a truly worldwide war economy. Before December, the European war, the Sino-Japanese War, Lend-Lease, and embargoes had already reorganized trade, finance, shipping, industrial allocation, and commodity flows. After Japan attacked U.S. and British positions in December 1941, the economic center of gravity shifted further toward the United States and toward total-war mobilization across the Atlantic and Pacific.

The United States was the dominant growth surprise of the year. Its real output rose at an extraordinary rate as defense procurement, shipbuilding, aircraft, metals, and machinery absorbed unemployed labor and idle plant. The U.S. was not yet operating at full 1943-1944 mobilization, but the 1941 surge made clear that American industrial capacity would become the decisive economic variable in the war.

Germany, Britain, Japan, Italy, and the Soviet Union were all large war economies, but their constraints differed sharply. Germany expanded output but deepened its dependence on occupied resources and faced strategic bottlenecks in oil, rubber, transport, and labor. Britain survived through controls, imports, and U.S. support. Japan entered the Pacific War because embargoes and resource scarcity made its existing trajectory unsustainable. The Soviet Union suffered a catastrophic invasion shock, with output, population, and capital moving east under emergency conditions.

The year also marked the fragmentation of the world trading and financial system. There was no Bretton Woods system yet. Exchange controls, blocked balances, sterling-area arrangements, dollar scarcity, gold, and bilateral clearing were more important than free capital mobility. Commodity markets did not behave as normal peacetime markets: oil, rubber, tin, wheat, metals, and shipping capacity were strategic assets.

At the micro level, the mechanisms of macro change were sectoral: aircraft plants, shipyards, railways, steel mills, mines, oilfields, food systems, and logistics networks. Consumer choice narrowed in most belligerent economies, while government procurement became the largest demand source for heavy industry. Corporate performance depended less on market expansion than on access to contracts, materials, labor, and state allocation.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1941

**1. Total war replaced recession recovery as the central macro regime.** In 1940, several economies were still best described as recovering from the Depression or adapting to European war. In 1941, especially after December, fiscal demand, procurement, and material allocation became the defining macro regime. The United States, Canada, Australia, and Britain saw rapid mobilization, while Germany and Japan intensified already militarized systems. The forward linkage was the 1942-1944 peak of war production.

**2. The United States became the balance-sheet and production anchor of the Allied economy.** Lend-Lease, defense contracts, and Treasury-Federal Reserve coordination shifted the U.S. from supplier and creditor toward active belligerent. The 1941 U.S. GDP surge, the fall in unemployment, and the rise in CPI inflation all show the move from slack absorption to bottleneck pressure. The forward linkage was the wartime system of bond finance, production boards, price controls, rationing, and yield support.

**3. Resource geography became strategic macroeconomics.** Japan's oil and raw-material constraints, Germany's fuel and transport limits, Britain's import dependence, and the Dutch East Indies' oil position show that commodities were not peripheral. Oil, rubber, tin, copper, steel, wheat, coal, and shipping were macro constraints. The forward linkage was the Pacific War's resource geography and the Allied strategic focus on logistics.

**4. Occupation economies distorted GDP, prices, and welfare.** France, the Netherlands, Belgium, Austria, and large parts of Eastern Europe were not normal national economies. Output may have continued in factories and farms, but the distribution of benefits, forced transfers, labor allocation, and rationing made GDP a weak welfare measure. The forward linkage was deeper extraction, forced labor, shortages, and postwar reconstruction challenges.

**5. Non-market and colonial economies require special caution.** Soviet GDP, Chinese output, Indian wartime supply, and Dutch East Indies commodity production were all central to the global picture, but the data are weaker than for the U.S. or U.K. Definitions of unemployment, inflation, current accounts, and fiscal balances are not comparable. The forward linkage was postwar decolonization, state planning, and new national-account systems.

**6. Finance moved from market pricing toward administrative control.** Bond markets, exchange rates, and credit allocation were increasingly controlled by governments. The U.S. had not yet formalized the full 1942 yield-curve peg, but the direction was visible. Britain, Germany, Japan, and many occupied economies already had deep controls. This matters because stock, bond, currency, and interest-rate data from 1941 cannot be interpreted like modern market-clearing prices.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

World GDP: Data not available. Maddison 2010 has no 1941 "World Total" value; the same source gives 1940 world GDP at 4,502.6 billion 1990 international dollars and 1950 world GDP at 5,335.9 billion 1990 international dollars. Because major 1941 wartime series are missing or uncertain, no 1941 world total is estimated.

World population: about 2,304.4 million in the Gapminder v8 reconstruction, up from 2,284.2 million in 1940 (+0.88%). This is a smoothed reconstruction, not a census count, and does not measure war deaths or refugee movements within the year.

World growth rate: Data not available. The leading observation is dispersion rather than a single average: the United States, Canada, Australia, Mexico, Brazil, and the United Kingdom grew strongly, while the Soviet Union and occupied France contracted sharply.

Global trade volume: Data not available as a single comparable number. Trade was constrained by war, convoy risk, submarine warfare, blocked ports, exchange controls, embargoes, and requisitioning. Measured trade values are hard to compare because prices, insurance, freight, and official controls changed substantially.

Reserve currency dynamics: No Bretton Woods system existed in 1941. The dollar and gold were central to Allied finance, while sterling-area controls and blocked balances remained crucial. The U.S. gold price remained fixed at $35 per troy ounce under the post-1934 U.S. gold regime, but private convertibility and international access were controlled. The U.S. monetary gold stock rose by about USD 742 million in 1941, against USD 4,351 million in 1940, while India's Reserve Bank accumulated sterling as Britain financed war spending there.

Global inflation trends: Inflation pressure increased in many countries because war demand collided with constrained civilian supply. The U.S. CPI rose 5.1 percent on an annual-average basis. Consumer-price indices in the Macrohistory Database ranged from 1.4% in Japan and 2.3% in Germany, where controls suppressed measured prices, to 10.8% in Britain, 13-17% in Sweden, the Netherlands, Italy and France, and 19.8% in Spain. ### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

United States: The Federal Reserve's 1941 annual report shows a system increasingly oriented toward wartime finance, government securities, currency demand, fiscal agency work, and banking operations. Credit conditions were easy, but the key policy variable was Treasury financing and the administrative preparation for war finance rather than a modern inflation-targeting rate. The New York Reserve Bank discount rate stayed at 1%. Required reserve ratios were raised effective 1 November, absorbing an estimated USD 1.2 billion of excess reserves, and Regulation W imposed consumer instalment-credit controls from 1 September.

United Kingdom: Monetary policy was subordinated to war finance, exchange control, rationing, and debt management. London markets remained open but heavily influenced by taxation, capital controls, and the war. Bank Rate stayed at 2% and Treasury bills averaged 1.01%; net government borrowing in calendar 1941 was GBP 2,745 million against GBP 2,076 million in 1940.

Germany, Italy, and Japan: Monetary systems were state-directed. Interest rates, exchange rates, and credit were instruments of war finance and allocation, not normal market prices. Official discount rates were unchanged at 3.5% for the Reichsbank, 4.5% in Italy and 3.285% for the Bank of Japan's commercial-bill rate. Japan abolished its metallic and fiduciary note-issue distinction on 1 April and set a normal issue limit of 4,700 million yen, which was exceeded by November.

Other central banks eased: France cut its discount rate to 1.75% on 17 March, Mexico to 4% on 2 January, Sweden to 3% on 29 May and the occupied Netherlands to 2.5% on 27 June.

Soviet Union: The monetary and credit system operated through plan priorities and state finance. Market-comparable rates are Data not available.

Exchange-rate regime: The year was a wartime control regime. Bretton Woods was still in the future. Currencies were managed, blocked, controlled, or subject to multiple official and unofficial rates.

Stock markets: The Dow Jones Industrial Average declined 15.38 percent in 1941 according to historical annual-return series. London share prices had been depressed by war taxation and uncertainty. Japanese equity index history for the later Nikkei series reports an unofficial 1941 gain, but the modern Nikkei 225 was not officially launched until 1950, so 1941 Japanese stock-index data must be treated as retrospective reconstruction. Many occupied or belligerent markets cannot be compared reliably. BIS share-price indices (January-June 1939=100) show New York falling from 96 to 81 while controlled or inflating continental markets rose, Brussels from 150 to 322 and Paris (Lyons) from 191 to 353.

Bond markets and yields: Data not available for a harmonized 10-year global table. Annual-average yields on named government instruments fell in most markets, for example British Consols from 3.41% to 3.13% (section 6B). The key analytical point is that sovereign bond markets were increasingly administered, captive, or shaped by patriotic savings and central-bank support.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

Trade in 1941 was dominated by war restrictions rather than liberal agreements. The U.S. Lend-Lease Act shifted Allied supply from ordinary trade finance toward official transfer and credit mechanisms. Embargoes against Japan, especially involving oil and strategic materials, were economically decisive. German occupation redirected European resources through coercive and administrative channels. Britain relied on imperial, sterling-area, and U.S. supply links, while shipping losses and convoy protection shaped real availability.

Regional integration in the modern sense was limited. There was no EEC, no ASEAN, and no postwar GATT framework. Bilateral clearing, imperial preference, colonial control, wartime procurement, and sanctions were more important than multilateral trade liberalization.

The Lend-Lease Act (55 Stat. 31) was approved on 11 March 1941, and on 7 November Roosevelt extended Lend-Lease to the Soviet Union. Latin American trade balances moved apart. Argentina's merchandise exports were 1,640 million paper pesos against imports of 1,277 million (1940: 1,629 million and 1,499 million), so its surplus widened mainly through import contraction; Brazil's exports rose to 6,729 million milreis against imports of 5,514 million (1940: 4,961 million and 4,964 million). The U.S.-Mexican financial accord of 19 November combined a USD 40 million stabilization provision with a claims settlement and silver purchases. These are nominal customs values, not trade volumes.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil was the most strategically important commodity of the year. The U.S. was the leading oil producer, the Dutch East Indies was a critical Asian source, and Japan's access to oil was threatened by embargoes. Germany depended on synthetic fuel, Romanian oil, stocks, and constrained logistics. Britain required seaborne oil under convoy risk.

Metals and minerals mattered through steel, copper, aluminum, tin, iron ore, coal, and bauxite. Aircraft and shipbuilding raised demand for aluminum and high-quality metals. Sweden's iron ore, Latin American minerals, Southeast Asian tin and rubber, and North American metals gained strategic value.

Agriculture was equally strategic. Britain, India, China, the Soviet Union, and occupied Europe faced food-supply constraints of different types. Wheat, rice, meat, sugar, and edible oils were subject to procurement, rationing, shipping losses, and regional shortages. Canada's wheat harvest fell from 551 million to 303 million bushels.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor markets changed through mobilization. The United States absorbed unemployment rapidly; BLS annual averages report a 9.9 percent unemployment rate in 1941, down from Depression-era levels and on the way to much lower wartime levels. Britain, Germany, Japan, and the Soviet Union used direct labor allocation to varying degrees. Occupied Europe faced forced labor, requisitions, and distorted incentives. The Federal Reserve estimated about 49.5 million people employed outside the armed forces at the end of 1941, about 3.5 million more than a year earlier. Recorded unemployment also fell in Canada from 9.67% to 4.62%, in Australia from 9.0% to 4.9%, in Britain from 3.3% to 1.2% and in the Netherlands from 11.39% to 8.47% (Macrohistory Database national definitions).

Migration had economic significance through evacuation, refugee flows, military mobilization, factory relocation, and rural-urban shifts. The Soviet industrial evacuation eastward was one of the most important labor and capital reallocations of the war. In the United States and Canada, workers moved toward shipyards, aircraft plants, and defense-industrial cities.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States was the decisive growth economy of 1941. Before Pearl Harbor, production was already being pulled into the defense program through Lend-Lease, naval expansion, aircraft orders, shipbuilding, and munitions contracts. After 7 December 1941, the same economy shifted from preparedness to total war mobilization. The central macro fact was not a normal business-cycle recovery but an administered surge in government demand, industrial capacity utilization, and labor absorption.

Fiscal and industrial policy dominated monetary policy. Federal Reserve operations were increasingly geared to servicing Treasury finance, managing government securities markets, currency needs, and the enlarged fiscal agency role of the Reserve Banks. Short-term credit remained easy, but private consumption began to meet rationing, installment-credit controls, and shortages as resources moved toward war production. The unemployment rate fell rapidly through the year, while CPI inflation rose as civilian demand met bottlenecks and price controls had not yet reached full wartime intensity.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Aircraft, shipbuilding, steel, aluminum, machine tools, rubber substitutes, and chemicals expanded sharply. Automobile producers were moving from civilian vehicles toward military and industrial production. Household durables demand was still visible early in the year but became constrained by material priorities. Banking liquidity was strong, credit risk was increasingly tied to government procurement, and housing construction began to shift toward defense-worker needs. The Federal Reserve industrial production index (1935-1939=100) rose from 123 to 156, durable manufacturing from 138 to 193 and nondurables from 113 to 135; factory employment (1923-1925=100) rose from 107.5 to 127.7 and nominal factory payrolls from 105.4 to 148.8. Bank loans rose about USD 3 billion, about two-thirds of it commercial and industrial lending, while bank holdings of government securities rose about USD 4 billion. Regulation W on consumer instalment credit, issued on 21 August under Executive Order 8843 of 9 August, took general effect on 1 September. Fifteen insured banks were closed or merged with FDIC financial aid. Chicago newspaper asking prices for houses rose 17.34% and asking rents 8.48%.

**Economic narrative:**

Germany's 1941 economy was a militarized command economy that expanded output while also stretching its resource base. The invasion of the Soviet Union in June 1941 changed the economic problem from a European occupation economy to a continental war of attrition. Germany extracted food, raw materials, labor, and fiscal resources from occupied territories, but the domestic GDP figure above does not include the full value of controlled foreign output.

The economy appeared powerful but had growing structural vulnerabilities: oil and rubber scarcity, transport strain, reliance on imported or occupied raw materials, and tensions between army, air force, navy, and civilian-sector priorities. Hindsight makes those vulnerabilities obvious. In 1941 they were already visible to planners, but the immediate campaign expectations made the sustainability problem easy to underweight.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Steel, chemicals, synthetic fuel, rail logistics, aircraft, armaments, and construction for military infrastructure were the central sectors. Private consumer markets were compressed by rationing and allocation. Large firms such as IG Farben, Krupp, Siemens, Daimler-Benz, and major banks operated inside a state-directed procurement and forced-allocation system. Corporate profitability cannot be interpreted like a peacetime market outcome. A dividend limitation of generally 6% was imposed on 12 June 1941, and from 1 November all purchase and sale orders had to pass through the official bourse. The Berlin share-price index (January-June 1939=100) rose only from 135 to 144, and the flat yield on 4.5% public bonds eased from 4.48% to 4.31%.

**Economic narrative:**

The United Kingdom in 1941 was a fully mobilized war economy. Output grew as unused resources were drawn into military production and civilian consumption was compressed. Imports, shipping, and access to dollar finance were central constraints. Lend-Lease changed the strategic economics of the war because it reduced the immediate need to finance U.S. supplies through gold or dollar assets.

The domestic policy regime was a controlled economy: rationing, labor direction, capital controls, high taxation, and direct industrial allocation. The Blitz damaged housing, infrastructure, and urban commerce, but the macro system remained organized around survival, naval protection of trade routes, and maintaining imports of food, oil, and raw materials.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Aircraft production, radar-linked electronics, ship repair, coal, steel, food distribution, and civil defense services were crucial. Retailing and housing were constrained. Household savings rose partly because goods were unavailable. Financial markets operated under war conditions with strong government influence over yields and capital allocation. Savings-bank funds were channelled into war debt through GBP 120 million of National Defence Loan in the first half of the year and a GBP 120 million 3% Funding Loan in November. The London share-price index (January-June 1939=100) rose from 87 to 98, and the equity total return was +22.73%.

**Economic narrative:**

The Soviet economy suffered the largest great-power shock of 1941. Germany's June invasion led to loss of industrial regions, agricultural land, coal and metals capacity, transport links, and millions of workers and soldiers. The measured GDP decline captures only part of the disruption because it cannot fully value evacuation, military losses, forced relocation, and the transition from normal production to emergency war output.

The central policy success of 1941 was not growth, but survival and relocation. Factories, workers, and machinery were moved eastward; inventories and scarce rolling stock were rationed; and agriculture and industry were reorganized around army supply. Hindsight emphasizes the importance of these 1941 emergency measures for the Soviet rebound in later years.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Heavy industry, railways, armaments, coal, steel, and machine tools were the essential sectors. Consumer goods production collapsed relative to military needs. Regional economics mattered more than national averages: western losses contrasted with Urals, Siberian, and Central Asian expansion. Loan receipts in the pre-invasion budget estimate were 13.2 billion rubles, and a 4% twenty-year loan issued in June reportedly raised 10.8 billion rubles. On 7 November Roosevelt determined Soviet defence vital to U.S. defence and directed Lend-Lease transfers to the Soviet Union, an authorization rather than a count of shipments.

### 5C. Innovation & technology

The economically important innovations were applied and military-administrative: radar deployment, aircraft production methods, shipbuilding techniques, code and communications systems, synthetic materials, industrial chemistry, and early computing-related research. The U.S. and U.K. benefited from university-industry-government links. Germany had strong engineering and chemical capacity but resource and strategic constraints. Japan had important naval and industrial capabilities but lacked the U.S. scale of standardized mass production.

R&D spending trends and patent activity: Data not available as a comparable global table. The qualitative trend was clear: research resources moved from civilian experimentation toward military, communications, aviation, medicine, and materials problems.

Infrastructure megaprojects included airfields, shipyards, defense housing, rail expansion, industrial evacuation routes in the Soviet Union, and port/convoy systems. Many were not recorded as "megaprojects" in modern investment datasets.

### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Sectoral employment shifted toward manufacturing, logistics, armed forces, government administration, and war services. In the United States, unemployment was still 9.9 percent on average for 1941, but the monthly trend moved down sharply. In Britain and Germany, labor allocation was already deeply managed. In the Soviet Union, labor and capital evacuation reshaped the geography of production.

Wage dynamics are difficult to compare because wage controls, rationing, military service, forced labor, and informal markets changed real compensation. Education and training became more applied: mechanics, pilots, radio operators, engineers, nurses, machinists, welders, and clerical administrators became scarce skills. Unions were important in the United States, Britain, Canada, and Australia, but wartime no-strike expectations and labor boards changed bargaining. U.S. factory payrolls (1923-1925=100) rose from 105.4 to 148.8 against factory employment from 107.5 to 127.7, so payroll growth combined more workers, longer hours and higher pay rather than measuring wage rates. British Indian railways employed 666,365 people in 1940-41, and July 1941 awards raised wages for about 70,000 Cawnpore mill workers.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The most important realized crisis was the German invasion of the Soviet Union. Economically, it destroyed or captured major productive regions, triggered factory evacuation, changed labor allocation, and turned a European war into a war of continental industrial endurance.

The second realized crisis was Japan's entry into war with the United States and Britain. It converted commodity embargoes and diplomatic breakdown into a Pacific economic war over oil, rubber, tin, shipping, and island logistics.

The third crisis was the deepening occupation economy in Europe. France, the Netherlands, Belgium, and other occupied economies experienced extraction, rationing, administrative controls, and black markets. GDP did not measure welfare well.

Near-misses and vulnerabilities included U.S. inflation acceleration, British shipping losses, Japanese oil exhaustion, German transport constraints, Soviet food and rail breakdown, and Chinese fiscal inflation. Hindsight shows that U.S. production capacity and Soviet evacuation were decisive positive surprises for the Allies, while Axis resource constraints were more binding than many Axis planners assumed.

Policy mistakes visible in retrospect: Japan underestimated the long-run industrial imbalance with the United States; Germany underestimated the economic depth and logistical difficulty of the Soviet war; Britain and the United States were still building the institutional machinery for full price and production control; colonial food-market risks in Asia were not addressed with adequate urgency.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1941 vs. 1940)

The largest structural break from 1940 to 1941 was the widening of the war from a primarily European conflict into a global conflict. In 1940 the fall of France and the Battle of Britain dominated economic interpretation. In 1941, Lend-Lease, Barbarossa, embargoes on Japan, and Pearl Harbor changed the scale and geography of mobilization.

The United States shifted from recovery and preparedness to direct war mobilization. Real GDP growth accelerated, unemployment fell, inflation rose, and financial institutions prepared for much larger Treasury financing. Canada and Australia also moved deeper into war production.

The Soviet Union moved from a large planned industrial economy into an emergency survival economy. The Maddison 2010 GDP estimate falls by about 20.6 percent from 1940 to 1941 for Total Former USSR. This was not a cyclical recession; it was a territorial, demographic, industrial, and logistical shock.

Germany's output still grew, but the year created the resource commitments that later became unsustainable. Japan's growth was modest, while strategic resource stress intensified. Britain moved from isolation after 1940 toward deeper dependence on U.S. support and an increasingly integrated Allied supply system.

Official discount rates were mostly unchanged or lower, while government debt ratios rose sharply in the belligerents: by 30 percentage points of GDP in Germany, 15.62 in Italy, 14.38 in Japan and 12.63 in Britain, against a fall of 3.92 points in the United States as nominal GDP grew faster than federal debt.

Economic power shifted toward the United States. This was visible not only in GDP but in the mechanisms of power: finance, oil, steel, shipbuilding, aircraft, food, and the ability to absorb labor into mass production.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

High-confidence data: U.S. unemployment, U.S. CPI annual average, U.S. real GDP growth from BEA/FRED, and the existence of Fed wartime finance operations are well documented. Maddison 2010 GDP and population values are usable for ranking where exact 1941 entries exist.

Medium-confidence data: GDP rankings for market economies with stable national data, such as the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, Sweden, and Argentina. Even there, wartime controls mean GDP is not a complete welfare measure.

Low-confidence data: Soviet GDP, occupied France, Netherlands, Belgium, Austria, and Japan's controlled wartime economy. The GDP estimates are useful but should not be overread. Non-market prices, forced labor, occupation transfers, and rationing distort measurement.

Weakest ranking evidence: China. Maddison 2010 gives no exact 1941 GDP value, and the 1940 Chinese figure is an interpolation between the 1938 and 1950 benchmarks. Surrounding benchmarks make clear that China belongs in the top 20, but the exact 1941 value and whether it ranks just above or below India cannot be established.

Important missing metrics: global 1941 GDP total, global growth rate, global trade volume, comparable inflation rates for all top 20 economies, comparable unemployment, policy rates, 10-year bond yields, current-account balances, government debt/GDP, budget balances, and detailed corporate rankings. In the country tables, consumer prices are not available for China, the Soviet Union, British India, the Dutch East Indies, Argentina, Brazil, Mexico, Belgium and Austria; unemployment for every economy except the United States, Britain, Canada, Spain, Australia, the Netherlands and Sweden; policy rates for China, the Soviet Union, the Dutch East Indies, Australia and Brazil; debt ratios for the Soviet Union, China, British India, France, the Dutch East Indies, Argentina, Brazil, Mexico, the Netherlands, Belgium and Austria; current accounts for those economies and Germany; and war-inclusive budget balances for every economy except Britain. Many of these data either did not exist in modern form or cannot be compared because of war controls.

Historical data conflicts: Maddison 2010 uses 1990 international dollars, while MPD 2023 and OWID use 2011 international dollars and updated benchmarks. The ranking keeps 1990 international dollars, consistent with the 1940 levels, and the 1941 levels match the 1940 levels and the stated growth rates; MPD 2023 growth appears only in the indicator Notes. The two vintages differ materially for some economies: Spain -1.6% against +1.51%, the United States 18.2% against 13.99%, Japan 1.4% against 3.58% and Sweden -1.5% against -2.44%. MPD 2023's India and Indonesia series cover modern territories and are shown only as comparators, and its separate Austria record does not resolve the annexation perimeter. For the Soviet Union, occupied Europe, China, colonial economies and command economies the GDP estimates are rough approximations, with an uncertainty band often of plus or minus 20 to 30 percent and wider for sector-level claims.

Indicator conflicts and definitions: U.S. CPI inflation is 5.1% in the Minneapolis Fed table against 5.0% from the BLS index levels and the Macrohistory Database. British debt is 133.7% of GDP in the Macrohistory Database against 160.06% in the Bank of England millennium dataset, and the current account -9.90% against -9.76%; the British budget ratio is a Bank of England public-sector figure, not a consolidated war-inclusive account. Consumer prices for Germany, Japan, Italy, France and the Netherlands are official controlled-price indices that miss black markets, and Belgium's Macrohistory consumer-price series is an implicit index derived from share prices, so it is not used as a CPI. Exchange rates other than Mexico's (a Banco de México December selling rate) are Macrohistory Database quotations whose market jurisdiction under wartime controls is not fully reconciled; the German and French series change source in 1941, so no change is computed. The Macrohistory Database gives the French and Australian rates in later currency units (new-franc equivalents and Australian dollars), and the Value cells express them in 1941 francs and Australian pounds at the fixed redenomination ratios. The British 1940 comparator is withheld because the Macrohistory 1940 quote (0.261 GBP per USD, USD 3.83) conflicts with the about USD 4.03 per pound given in the 1940 assessment. Equity measures disagree for the Netherlands and Japan, where BIS price indices rose 22.83% and 13.33% while Macrohistory total returns were -27.70% and -17.92%; only the BIS indices are reported for those markets. Policy rates are official central-bank discount rates; where none existed, as in Australia and Brazil, the value is Data not available and any market rate is shown only as context.
