# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1907

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1907 was defined by the collision of first-globalization optimism with the fragility of the classical gold-standard financial system. The world economy was still highly integrated by the standards of the pre-1914 era: trade, migration, shipping, telegraphy, and London-centered capital markets linked commodity exporters, industrial powers, and colonial economies. Yet the year exposed a central weakness of that system: liquidity could move quickly across borders, but crisis-management institutions were uneven. In the United States, the absence of a central bank made the Panic of 1907 a private-sector rescue operation coordinated by J. P. Morgan and other financiers rather than a public lender-of-last-resort response.

The dominant global event was the financial panic that began in New York in October and intensified a recession already under way in the United States. The panic spread through equity markets, call money markets, trust companies, and banks; Knickerbocker Trust suspended payments on 22 October. It also drew on earlier global stresses: gold flows after the 1906 San Francisco earthquake, Bank of England tightening, pressure in commodity and railway securities, and heavy dependence on short-term credit. The crisis was not only American. It interacted with London money-market conditions and with financial disturbances in places such as Japan, Egypt, Germany, Chile, Canada, Sweden, Denmark, and Italy, where a rescue agreement for the Societa Bancaria Italiana was reached on 11 October, before the Knickerbocker suspension, so not every European disturbance was contagion from New York. The year was nonetheless not a uniform annual depression: the Maddison Project Database 2023 shows U.S. real GDP up 1.53% and German real GDP up 4.43%, even as a reconstructed U.S. share-price sample fell 33.23%. In the Federico-Tena reconstruction, world merchandise export volume rose only 1.26%, against 5.50% growth in current-dollar exports. With hindsight, 1907 is one of the clearest pre-1914 examples of how a gold-standard shock could transmit internationally through gold flows, interest rates, and investor confidence.

Structurally, 1907 was still an industrialization year. Germany, the United States, Japan, and parts of the Habsburg Empire expanded heavy industry, chemicals, electricity, railways, and shipping; German crude-steel output rose 8.60% and U.S. coal output about 16%. The British economy remained the central financial node even as relative industrial leadership shifted toward the United States and Germany. China, India, Indonesia, and much of the Ottoman and Russian worlds remained overwhelmingly agrarian, but they were increasingly connected to global markets through railways, ports, treaty ports, colonial systems, and foreign loans. This produced growth channels, but also made fiscal and commodity systems vulnerable to foreign exchange, silver, and capital-market movements. Household experience diverged: the Maddison record for undivided India shows output falling 5.92% while the official Indian retail rice-price index rose 11.74%; Java's reporting sugar factories raised weighted output 11.68% and De Javasche Bank cut its bill rate to 5.5%; and Mexico City consumption prices rose 4.71%.

The microeconomic story was therefore not an afterthought. Corporate concentration, trusts, universal banks, railways, oil companies, shipping lines, colonial plantation firms, and mining houses transmitted the macro shock to households and workers. Royal Dutch and Shell combined their operating interests in 1907 under a 60:40 arrangement, forming the Royal Dutch Shell Group, a major step in the international oil industry's consolidation. U.S. Steel's emergency acquisition of Tennessee Coal, Iron and Railroad during the panic showed how corporate consolidation could be justified as crisis stabilization. Leo Baekeland's 1907 laboratory work on Bakelite, publicly announced in February 1909, pointed toward synthetic materials and the later plastics industry. Tata Iron and Steel was registered on 26 August and raised its capital within three weeks, years before producing steel, and the Swiss National Bank opened on 20 June. These events show 1907 as both a crisis year and a technology-and-scale year.

The largest data problem is that modern national accounts did not exist in 1907 for most countries. GDP, population, GDP per capita, and real growth are best treated as historical estimates. Maddison Project Database and Our World in Data's Maddison processing are the main reference framework; the 2023 release contains only 38 country records for 1907 and no GDP value for China or for the Russian, Ottoman and Habsburg empires as historical units. Soviet bloc terminology is not applicable because the USSR did not yet exist; the relevant unit is the Russian Empire. Non-market and colonial economies, especially China, India, Indonesia, and the Ottoman Empire, have wider error bands. Retrospective CPI, unemployment, debt, current-account and budget series exist for the main Western economies and Japan, mainly in the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database and the Bank of England's millennium dataset; elsewhere such indicators are marked as Data not available rather than inferred.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1907

**Theme 1: The Panic of 1907 and the limits of private financial rescue.** The Panic of 1907 was the defining economic shock of the year. It turned a weakening U.S. business cycle into a severe financial contraction and helped trigger the political process that eventually produced the Federal Reserve System. The crisis began in a financial system without a U.S. central bank, with trust companies taking large maturity and liquidity risks under looser regulation than national banks. Runs on trust companies, forced liquidation of collateral, and spikes in call money rates pushed the crisis into securities markets and commercial credit. The Comptroller of the Currency's December report described failures to remit funds, hoarding of reserves, cancelled orders and closing factories, which impeded commercial collections and the movement of crops. Knickerbocker Trust's October suspension was not a permanent liquidation; the company later reopened.

The countries most affected were the United States and economies financially linked to New York and London, including Canada, the United Kingdom, Japan, Germany, and commodity exporters that depended on credit-market access. The forward linkage is direct: the Aldrich-Vreeland Act of 1908 and the National Monetary Commission came after the panic, and the Federal Reserve Act followed in 1913. With hindsight, 1907 was the institutional bridge between nineteenth-century private clearinghouse crisis management and twentieth-century central banking.

**Theme 2: Gold-standard discipline and international liquidity transmission.** The classical gold standard provided exchange-rate stability but constrained domestic monetary flexibility. Gold parity made currency values predictable and supported long-distance lending, but it also meant that gold outflows could force interest-rate increases. The Bank of England's gold-defense actions after heavy insurance-related payments from the San Francisco earthquake helped tighten global liquidity; Bank Rate ended 1907 at 7%, one point above its 1906 close, although its calendar-year average was 4.93%. The Bank of Japan raised its commercial-bill rate from 6.57% to 7.30% effective 4 December, and the Riksbank tightened its discount in November as foreign credits were cancelled. Tightening was not universal: De Javasche Bank cut its bill rate twice in the spring, to 5.5%. In 1907, this system transmitted stress through gold flows, discount rates, and confidence.

The United Kingdom, United States, Germany, France, Japan, Canada, Australia, and several Latin American economies were affected most directly. Economies outside full gold-standard participation, including China with its silver-linked monetary environment, were affected indirectly through trade prices, foreign loans, and exchange-rate uncertainty. The forward linkage is the pre-1914 gold standard's reputation for stability, followed by its breakdown in World War I and the failed interwar attempt to restore it.

**Theme 3: Industrial concentration, trusts, and universal banks.** Large firms and financial groups were central to 1907. In the United States, trusts and railways were both growth engines and sources of systemic fragility. U.S. Steel, Standard Oil, the railroads, and large New York banks connected securities prices to credit availability; U.S. Steel's purchase of Tennessee common stock cost $35.32 million and was financed largely with securities rather than cash. In Germany, universal banks helped finance heavy industry, chemicals, and electrical equipment. In Japan, zaibatsu groups and state-guided finance supported industrialization after the Russo-Japanese War.

The theme's significance is that firm scale was becoming a macroeconomic variable. Corporate balance sheets and financial networks could stabilize or destabilize entire markets. The forward linkage was stronger antitrust politics, the Pujo Committee's later investigation of the "money trust," more central-bank thinking, and continued consolidation in oil, steel, chemicals, and electrical machinery.

**Theme 4: Commodity-export growth under fragile credit conditions.** Many economies outside the industrial core depended on commodity exports: wheat and beef in Argentina, wheat and timber in Canada, wool and gold in Australia, coffee in Brazil, sugar and oil in the Dutch East Indies, cotton and jute in British India, nitrates and copper in parts of Latin America, and grain in Russia. Strong global trade gave these economies market access, but the 1907 credit shock revealed that export prosperity depended on shipping, credit, bills of exchange, and London or New York financing. Physical output and financial prices separated: U.S. coal output rose about 16% while U.S. crude steel was almost flat, Newcastle steam coal was 38.10% dearer on an annual-average basis, and Chilean copper bars averaged only 1.14% less than in 1906 despite the late-year copper slump.

The countries most affected included Argentina, Canada, Australia, Brazil, Mexico, Russia, India, Indonesia, and the Ottoman Empire. The forward linkage was the increasing pre-1914 dependence of peripheral economies on commodity cycles, foreign capital, and railway buildout. This dependence became a key vulnerability when World War I disrupted trade and capital flows.

**Theme 5: Late imperial reform and fiscal pressure.** China, Russia, the Ottoman Empire, British India, and Austria-Hungary all had economic systems shaped by imperial administration. In China, the Qing state pursued late reforms, railway projects, and fiscal reorganization while facing indemnities and foreign concessions. Russia pursued Stolypin agrarian reforms after the 1905 revolution; the abolition of peasant redemption payments took effect at the beginning of 1907 under the manifesto of 3 November 1905. The Ottoman Empire remained constrained by debt administration and foreign concessions and negotiated with the powers over increased customs duties. British India generated revenue and export flows under colonial institutions rather than sovereign developmental policy.

The significance is that 1907 economic policy in many large economies was not simply "market policy". It was state-building, empire management, and fiscal extraction. The forward linkage is political instability: the Young Turk Revolution in 1908, China's 1911 revolution, Russia's continuing social strain, and the Habsburg and Ottoman empires' vulnerability before World War I.

**Theme 6: Technology, shipping, electricity, and synthetic materials.** Despite crisis, 1907 was a year of technological momentum. Electrification spread in leading cities and factories. Marine engineering advanced with ships such as RMS Mauretania, which entered transatlantic service in 1907. Royal Dutch Shell's formation created a global oil competitor. Baekeland's Bakelite work introduced the first fully synthetic plastic path, with later importance for electrical equipment, telephones, radios, and industrial components. The U.S. Patent Office granted 36,620 patents and reissues, 14.56% more than in 1906.

The leading countries were the United States, United Kingdom, Germany, Netherlands, Japan, and France. The forward linkage was the twentieth-century shift toward oil, electrical equipment, chemical engineering, telecommunications, and mass production.

**Theme 7: An international crisis with local antecedents.** Several European disturbances had domestic roots. In Italy, the Societa Bancaria Italiana combined short liabilities with assets tied to industrial and stock-market finance; its deposits fell from 36 million to 32 million lire between 31 August and 30 September, and a consortium agreed a 50 million lire rescue framework on 11 October. Denmark entered a construction and banking crisis lasting from 1907 to 1909 after the Copenhagen building boom and reduced international financing. Sweden lost foreign credit, and the Riksbank supplied liquidity while tightening its discount terms. The forward linkage was a stronger role for central banks as lenders of last resort in smaller European systems.

**Theme 8: Social regulation and labor conflict.** Social and labor institutions changed unevenly. In Britain, the Workmen's Compensation Act of 1906 commenced on 1 July 1907, while the school medical-inspection duty enacted in August 1907 took effect only on 1 January 1908. In Australia, the Harvester judgment of 8 November set a benchmark of 7 shillings a day for unskilled work in an excise-exemption proceeding, not a universal statutory minimum wage. In Mexico, the Rio Blanco textile conflict of 7 January preceded the October panic. These were separate institutional changes rather than an international welfare settlement.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

World GDP: Data not available as a contemporary official aggregate. Maddison Project Database and Our World in Data provide long-run historical GDP estimates in 2011 international dollars, but the 2023 release has only benchmark world observations and 38 country records for 1907, with no GDP value for China or for the Russian, Ottoman and Habsburg empires as historical units. The sum of the available records is therefore not world output, and any single world figure should be treated as an external dataset value rather than a statistic reproduced here.

World growth rate: Data not available as a consistent global annual growth statistic for 1907. The United States entered recession in May 1907 according to NBER chronology, while Germany and parts of continental Europe continued industrial expansion before financial tightening. The global growth pattern was therefore mixed: industrial momentum early in the year, financial contraction later in the year. Most observed annual series still grew: Maddison Project Database 2023 real GDP rose 1.53% in the United States, 1.81% in the United Kingdom, 4.43% in Germany, 4.33% in France and 2.25% in Japan, but fell 1.74% in the Netherlands.

World population: The Federico-Tena world population reconstruction (January 2026 revision) rises from 1,685.1 million in 1906 to 1,699.8 million in 1907 (+0.87%), in both its 1991-border and historical-border versions. These are reconstructions that include interpolation and extrapolation, not census observations.

Global trade volume: In the Federico-Tena World Trade Historical Database, the world merchandise export-volume index (1913=100) rose from 78.2663 to 79.2529 (+1.26%), while current-border exports at current prices rose from USD 13,359.72 million to USD 14,094.15 million (+5.50%). Volume and current-dollar value should not be conflated; services, capital flows and domestic commerce are outside the series. Qualitatively, the world remained within the first wave of globalization, with high trade integration, large capital flows, and migration. Trade was dominated by manufactured exports from Britain, Germany, France, and the United States and by commodity exports from Russia, India, Canada, Argentina, Australia, Brazil, Indonesia, and other colonial or settler economies.

Reserve currency dynamics: Sterling was the dominant international reserve, settlement, and trade-finance currency. London was the primary global money market. The U.S. dollar was important domestically and increasingly relevant internationally, but the United States lacked a central bank and did not yet play the reserve-currency role it would later acquire. Gold itself was the anchor of the system. Reserve-currency shares: Data not available.

Global inflation trends: Data not available as a unified global CPI estimate. Under the gold standard, price-level movements were constrained by gold supply, productivity, and cross-border payments. Retrospective annual consumer-price reconstructions rose 4.63% in the United States, 1.10% in France, 4.72% in Italy, 10.46% in Japan, 4.21% in Spain, 2.20% in the Netherlands and 5.19% in Sweden, and fell 0.75% in Germany; the Bank of England series gives 1.59% for the United Kingdom. Their baskets and coverage differ, and many economies lacked national CPI statistics.

Key commodity prices: Gold was fixed by U.S. parity at $20.67 per fine troy ounce under the gold standard. In the Federico-Tena commodity price dataset, Newcastle steam coal rose from 10.50 to 14.50 shillings per ton (+38.10%), English Gazette wheat from 28.25 to 30.58 shillings per quarter (+8.26%), while Chilean copper bars eased from 88 to 87 pounds sterling per ton (-1.14%) and silver from 41.08 to 40.90 pounds per 10 kg (-0.44%). Copper prices weakened sharply late in 1907 amid financial stress and collapsing speculative demand, but the annual-average quotation fell only slightly. A verified 1907 crude-oil price in dollars per barrel: Data not available; the dataset's petroleum quotation rose from GBP 0.00905 to GBP 0.01258 per gallon, but its quotation convention is uncertain and it is not a global benchmark. Cotton, sugar, coffee, and wool conditions were region-specific.

International capital flow direction: Capital moved mainly from Britain and other European financial centers to the Americas, Australasia, Russia, colonial Asia, and infrastructure projects. The 1907 panic interrupted this pattern by raising liquidity preference, tightening London and New York credit, and exposing dependence on short-term finance. Retrospective current-account ratios show Britain's surplus widening to 7.37% of GDP and France at 4.22%, while Canada (-9.54%), Denmark (-5.92%), the Netherlands (-3.46%) and Sweden (-2.99%) were net absorbers of resources. These are net balances, not gross foreign lending; worldwide capital-flow totals are Data not available.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The applicable framework is the classical gold standard, not Bretton Woods. No euro area, Federal Reserve policy rate, OPEC, EEC or ASEAN existed in 1907.

United States: The U.S. had no central bank. Seasonal currency demand, agricultural payments, and call-money markets made New York vulnerable each autumn. In October 1907, call money became extremely scarce and private rescue pools were required to keep the stock exchange operating; convertibility restrictions separated cash from deposit claims. The Macrohistory Database annual short-term market rate rose from 6.54% to 7.01%. The NBER business-cycle chronology places the U.S. peak in May 1907 and the trough in June 1908.

United Kingdom: Britain remained the center of the gold-standard system. Bank of England policy was focused on gold reserves and sterling convertibility. Bank Rate ended 1907 at 7%, up from 6% at the end of 1906, but its calendar-year average was 4.93%, so the 7% level applied only to the crisis months. London tightening transmitted stress abroad.

Germany and France: Both were gold-standard economies with major banking systems. Germany's universal banks financed heavy industry and exports, while France's high savings and gold holdings supported its role as a capital exporter. Official Reichsbank and Banque de France discount rates: Data not available. Market short rates averaged 5.12% in Germany (1906: 4.04%) and 3.35% in France (1906: 2.68%); long government yields rose from 3.63% to 3.75% in Germany and were nearly unchanged at 3.148% in France.

Japan: Japan was on the gold standard after its late nineteenth-century reforms and was industrializing rapidly after the Russo-Japanese War. In 1907, the financial system experienced stress linked to postwar expansion, tight money, and global liquidity strains. The Bank of Japan Head Office rate on commercial bills other than collection bills rose from 6.57% to 7.30% effective 4 December.

Other central banks: The Riksbank responded to cancelled foreign credits with November discount tightening and access to a foreign loan taken over from the National Debt Office. De Javasche Bank in the Netherlands Indies cut its bill-discount rate by half a point on 1 March and again on 5 April, to 5.5%. The Swiss National Bank began business on 20 June; its sole note-issue privilege began only in 1910.

Exchange-rate regime: The main advanced economies operated under the classical gold standard. Sterling-dollar parity was fixed at approximately $4.8665 per pound before World War I; the Bank of England annual average rate was $4.8580. Gold-standard exchange rates could move within gold points, but not freely float in the modern sense.

Stock markets: The New York market was the epicenter of panic. U.S. equities had already weakened before October, and the panic caused forced selling, collateral pressure, and rescue operations; a reconstructed U.S. share sample lost 33.23% in price terms. London, Berlin, and other exchanges were exposed through credit and investor sentiment. Reconstructed returns for other markets are given in section 6A.

Bond markets and yield curves: Government bond markets existed in Britain, France, Germany, the United States, and other major countries. Reconstructed long government yields are shown in section 6B; full yield curves and maturity-matched credit spreads for 1907: Data not available.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

1907 operated in a world of empires, tariff systems, and strategic alliances. There was no WTO-style multilateral trade regime. Tariffs, imperial preferences, colonial monopolies, and bilateral concessions shaped trade. The EEC and ASEAN did not exist. The British Empire, French Empire, German Empire, Russian Empire, Ottoman Empire, Qing Empire, Japanese Empire, and U.S. overseas possessions structured economic geography.

Major geopolitical-economic context included the Anglo-Russian Convention, signed on 31 August with ratifications exchanged on 23 September, which helped define spheres of influence in Persia, Afghanistan, and Tibet. Its Persian clauses concerned concession zones and existing loan-security arrangements, including customs revenues; it was not a customs union or a general opening of trade. Japan consolidated influence in Korea and Manchuria after the Russo-Japanese War. The United States continued antitrust and railroad regulation debates after the Hepburn Act of 1906. In China, railway finance and foreign concessions remained central economic-political issues. The Ottoman Empire negotiated with the powers over increased customs duties in 1907; the date of collection and the revenue effect: Data not available.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil: Oil was still a small share of world energy compared with coal, but its strategic importance was rising. Standard Oil remained dominant in the United States, while Royal Dutch Shell was created in 1907 to compete internationally. Major producing regions included the United States, Russia's Baku fields, Romania, the Dutch East Indies, and Mexico's emerging oil regions. British India's petroleum output was 152,045,677 gallons, of which 148,888,002 gallons came from Burma. A verified 1907 world oil-production total: Data not available.

Coal: Coal was the dominant industrial fuel. Britain, Germany, the United States, France, Belgium, and Japan relied on coal for factories, railways, steel, and shipping. Coalfields shaped regional industrial geography, including the Ruhr, South Wales, Pennsylvania, northern France, Belgium, and Japanese mining districts. In an official comparative table (coal excluding lignite, thousand metric tonnes), U.S. output rose from 375,700 to 435,758 (+15.99%), British from 255,085 to 272,116 (+6.68%) and German from 137,118 to 143,186 (+4.43%).

Metals: Steel, copper, tin, gold, and silver were central. Crude-steel output rose from 10.700 to 11.620 million metric tonnes in Germany (+8.60%) and by 12.89% in France, but was almost flat in the United States (-0.15%). Copper price weakness was part of the 1907 financial story because copper securities and mining speculation were linked to market stress. Tin and petroleum were important in the Dutch East Indies; gold remained crucial in South Africa, Australia, Russia, and monetary reserves.

Agriculture: Wheat, cotton, sugar, jute, coffee, wool, rice, and tea moved through global trade networks. Weather, monsoons, rail freight, and shipping costs mattered. National agricultural data were uneven, and global annual crop-price comparisons are not consistently available. ### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Global labor markets were dominated by agriculture. Industrial employment was concentrated in the United States, Britain, Germany, France, Belgium, northern Italy, Japan's growing factory sector, and parts of Austria-Hungary. Migration remained a major economic force, especially European migration to the United States, Canada, Argentina, Australia, and other settler economies. U.S. immigrant arrivals reached 1,285,349 in the fiscal year ended 30 June 1907, against 1,100,735 a year earlier (+16.77%); by last permanent residence, 338,452 came from Austria-Hungary, 285,731 from Italy and 258,943 from the Russian Empire. These are gross arrivals, not net migration.

Unemployment data are not comparable across countries. Modern labor-force surveys did not exist. Retrospective estimates give 2.80% for the United States, 4.39% for the United Kingdom (Bank of England series), 1.60% for Germany, 7.00% for France, 3.20% for the Netherlands and 5.20% for Australia, with source-specific coverage. Urban wage laborers, miners, dockworkers, railway workers, and textile workers experienced cyclical layoffs and wage pressure differently from rural households. At U.S. Steel, average employment rose 3.81% to 210,180 and payroll 8.84% to $160.83 million, partly because coverage expanded with Tennessee. Labor movements were active in many industrial countries, but institutional coverage varied widely.

Demographically, large agrarian societies such as China, India, Russia, and the Dutch East Indies had huge populations but low measured GDP per capita. Settler economies such as Canada, Australia, and Argentina had smaller populations and higher GDP per capita, supported by land abundance, commodity exports, and foreign capital.

**Economic narrative:**

The U.S. economic story in 1907 was the transition from industrial boom to financial panic. Railways, steel, copper, trusts, and urban real estate had expanded on credit. The financial system was fragmented between national banks, state banks, trust companies, private bankers, and clearinghouses. Because the United States had no central bank, liquidity crises were managed through private coordination. When runs hit trust companies and collateral values fell, the crisis moved into call-money markets and the New York Stock Exchange.

Policy decisions were reactive. Treasury actions and private rescues mattered, but the decisive institutional result came later: monetary reform. In 1907 itself, the federal government did not yet have a lender-of-last-resort institution. The crisis demonstrated that the U.S. had become too large and financially central to rely on ad hoc private rescue.

Annual aggregates still stood above 1906. Real GDP rose 1.53% in the Maddison Project Database 2023, coal output rose 15.99%, and consumer prices rose 4.63%, while a reconstructed share-price sample fell 33.23% (total return -28.76%). The Comptroller of the Currency identified failures to remit funds and the hoarding of reserves as impediments to commercial collections and crop movement; this describes the transmission mechanism, not insolvency of all banks or contraction of every industry throughout the year.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Railroads, steel, copper, and trust-company finance were the most exposed sectors. ### 5C. Innovation & technology

Bakelite was developed by Leo Baekeland from his June 1907 laboratory work, with the first public announcement on 8 February 1909, marking the beginning of fully synthetic plastics and later affecting electrical insulation, telephones, radios, consumer goods, and industrial components; no 1907 commercial turnover is implied. Electrification continued to move from novelty to industrial infrastructure in the United States, Germany, Britain, and Japan. German chemical and electrical firms were among the world's leading innovation systems.

Transport technology also mattered. RMS Mauretania entered transatlantic service in 1907, demonstrating scale, speed, and turbine marine engineering. Railways remained the main infrastructure technology for continental integration, settlement, and commodity export.

Patent activity: the U.S. Patent Office granted 36,620 patents and reissues in 1907 against 31,965 in 1906 (+14.56%), comprising 35,880 ordinary patents, 589 designs and 151 reissues; applications rose from 56,482 to 57,679. A global patent aggregate: Data not available. R&D spending trends: Data not available. Infrastructure megaprojects: Data not available as a global list, but railways in Canada, Russia, India, Argentina, China, and Ottoman territories were among the most economically significant infrastructure channels, and U.S. Steel's Gary works absorbed $19.34 million of property additions in 1907.

### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Sectoral employment was still heavily agricultural worldwide. Industrial employment was concentrated in the United States, Britain, Germany, France, Belgium, northern Italy, Japan, and selected colonial cities. Urban industrial workers faced cyclical layoffs and wage pressure after the credit shock. Rural households faced price, weather, tax, and debt risks. U.S. Steel reported that January wage advances (March for coke companies) covered about 65% of its employees.

Wage dynamics across skill levels: Data not available as a comparable global series. Education and workforce trends favored high-income industrial countries with expanding literacy, technical education, and engineering institutions. Japan and Germany stand out for state and institutional support of technical modernization. Britain's Education (Administrative Provisions) Act of August 1907 required school medical inspection from January 1908.

Union and labor movements were active in Britain, Germany, France, Italy, the United States, Australia, and parts of Latin America, but membership, bargaining rights, and strike data are not compiled here. Australia's Harvester judgment set a 7-shilling daily benchmark for unskilled work in a specific proceeding, and Mexico's Rio Blanco textile conflict of 7 January was a major labor confrontation. Immigration affected labor supply in the United States, Canada, Argentina, and Australia, while emigration affected Italy, Sweden, and parts of Eastern Europe; U.S. arrivals reached 1,285,349 in the fiscal year to June 1907.

### 6C. Currencies

The classical gold standard anchored major currencies. Sterling-dollar parity was approximately $4.8665 per pound, and the Bank of England annual average was $4.8580. Gold was fixed in U.S. law at $20.67 per fine troy ounce. Currencies of gold-standard countries moved within gold points, not floating freely: annual averages per USD were 5.1849 French francs, 5.17 lire, 5.17 Belgian francs, 2.4875 guilders and 3.7785 kronor, while Japan's December demand-draft quotation, 49.375 dollars per 100 yen, was unchanged from December 1906. The Spanish peseta, not on gold, averaged 5.77 per USD, and the Mexican peso 2.01 per USD.

China's silver-linked and fragmented monetary environment differed from gold-standard systems. India operated within a colonial rupee-sterling framework; Council bills and De Javasche Bank's sterling bills are not spot USD rates. Brazil's conversion office, created by the decree of 6 December 1906, could issue notes against gold at 15 pence sterling per milreis; that is a statutory rule, not a 1907 market observation. Exchange rates versus USD for China, the Russian, Ottoman and Habsburg empires, Germany, Australia and Argentina: Data not available.

Capital controls: Modern capital-control regimes were not the central feature of 1907. The relevant constraints were gold convertibility, central-bank reserve defense, colonial exchange systems, and political risk.

Reserve currency composition: Data not available in modern percentage terms. Sterling and gold dominated international reserves and settlement; the French franc, German mark, and U.S. dollar were important but secondary in different contexts.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

Active crises during 1907: The Panic of 1907 was the primary global financial crisis. It included trust-company runs, stock-market stress, call-money scarcity, emergency lending pools, and a private rescue of key institutions. The crisis was amplified by the absence of a U.S. central bank and by gold-standard liquidity constraints. Contemporary evidence from the Comptroller of the Currency documents impaired remittance and collection channels, demand for cash and difficult industrial refinancing. Other crises had local roots: the Societa Bancaria Italiana's liquidity problem before October, credit withdrawal in Sweden, the start of Denmark's 1907-1909 construction and banking crisis, and food-price pressure in India.

Near-misses: The threatened closure of the New York Stock Exchange and the potential failure of major brokerage and trust institutions were systemic near-misses. U.S. Steel's purchase of Tennessee Coal, Iron and Railroad helped prevent forced liquidation of collateral tied to Moore & Schley and related institutions. A rescue commitment is not the same as cash disbursed, and support for selected institutions did not establish system-wide solvency.

Systemic vulnerabilities building, hindsight-labeled: First, the gold standard provided stability but could transmit shocks quickly. Second, international capital markets relied heavily on London and a narrow group of private banks. Third, U.S. trust companies had become large enough to create systemic risk while remaining less regulated than national banks. Fourth, empire-based trade and debt systems created political-economic fragility in China, the Ottoman Empire, Russia, and Austria-Hungary. Fifth, reliance on short foreign credit, industrial securities used in bank finance, and urban construction booms such as Copenhagen's could amplify shocks. Historians weigh prior tightening, information problems, international gold movements and domestic institutional design differently.

Policy mistakes visible in retrospect: The lack of a U.S. lender of last resort was the central institutional failure. Gold-defense tightening may have been rational within the rules of the gold standard, but it worsened liquidity stress. Regulatory gaps around trusts and securities collateral made runs more likely.

Tail risks that materialized: A local market manipulation failure became a broader banking panic. A financial shock became a recession amplifier. Tail risks that did not fully materialize: a complete collapse of the New York Stock Exchange, a general suspension of gold convertibility among leading economies, a global sovereign-debt crisis, a worldwide annual GDP contraction, and a universal collapse of industrial output.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1907 vs. 1906)

The largest year-over-year change was the shift from expansionary confidence to financial distress. In 1906, the San Francisco earthquake created huge insurance claims and gold flows, but the global financial system still appeared functional. In 1907, the aftershocks of those gold flows, tighter Bank of England policy, and U.S. financial fragility culminated in panic.

Credit conditions changed sharply. In 1906, capital markets financed railways, urban construction, industrial firms, and commodity exporters. In 1907, liquidity preference rose, call money tightened, and banks became more cautious. This change affected the United States most directly but also influenced Canada, Britain, Japan, Sweden, Italy, Denmark and commodity exporters. Monetary policy did not move uniformly: Britain, Japan and Sweden tightened, while De Javasche Bank eased.

Policy expectations changed. Before 1907, U.S. central banking reform was a specialized topic. After 1907, it became politically unavoidable. The crisis did not create the Federal Reserve immediately, but it changed the intellectual and legislative path. Institutional changes during the year included the opening of the Swiss National Bank and the commencement of Britain's workmen's compensation law.

Corporate structure also shifted. Royal Dutch Shell's formation strengthened global oil competition. U.S. Steel's emergency acquisition of Tennessee Coal, Iron and Railroad showed how crisis could accelerate concentration. Industrial capitalism became more global, more consolidated, and more financially connected.

Geopolitically, 1907 saw continued great-power alignment and imperial competition. The Anglo-Russian Convention reduced certain diplomatic tensions but also formalized imperial spheres. China, the Ottoman Empire, Russia, and Austria-Hungary entered 1908 with unresolved economic and political strains. Two statistical breaks should not be read as economic contractions: Canada's 1907 fiscal period covered only nine months, and India's cotton-mill statistics changed territorial coverage in fiscal 1906-07.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

Reliable statistics: The best statistical confidence is for broad historical GDP and population estimates in Maddison Project Database, especially for leading Western economies. The United States, United Kingdom, Germany, France, Canada, Australia, Sweden, and the Netherlands have relatively stronger historical statistical bases, although not modern national accounts in the 1907 sense. Dated official rate schedules of the Bank of England, Bank of Japan and De Javasche Bank, statutes, U.S. patent and immigration counts, official Indian and colonial tables, comparative industrial output tables and the U.S. Steel annual report are well documented, although colonial reporting populations, selected companies and fiscal periods limit their representativeness.

Medium-confidence statistics: Japan, Italy, Spain, Belgium, Argentina, and Mexico have useful historical series, but country-specific macro indicators such as CPI, unemployment, fiscal balance, and current account are not consistently available in comparable modern form. Retrospective CPI, unemployment, debt, current-account and equity-return series from the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database and its rate-of-return extension, the Bank of England millennium dataset, the Ma-de Jong China reconstruction and the Mexico City price index are reconstructions of moderate quality; every ratio uses a same-source nominal denominator, not PPP output.

Low-confidence statistics: China, British India, the Dutch East Indies, the Ottoman Empire, Austria-Hungary, and the Russian Empire have larger uncertainty because of imperial boundaries, colonial statistical systems, non-market production, fragmented fiscal systems, and retrospective PPP estimation. Error bands of plus or minus 20-30 percent are plausible for some non-Western or imperial estimates.

Historical data conflicts: GDP values differ depending on whether one uses Maddison 1990 international dollars, Maddison Project 2018 or 2023, OWID's 2011 international dollar processing, modern borders, or historical empires. Country ranking also changes depending on whether colonial territories are counted separately or inside imperial metropoles. Austria-Hungary is especially difficult because some datasets split later successor territories, while historical economic analysis treats it as one empire. The rounded levels in the ranking table are retained for continuity, but several differ markedly from the Maddison Project Database 2023 values for 1907: the database gives the United States 869.071 billion 2011 international dollars (87.339 million people, 9,950 per person), the United Kingdom 343.554 billion, undivided India 290.665 billion, Japan 112.466 billion, Indonesia 57.011 billion, Spain 54.930 billion, Belgium 45.995 billion, Argentina 33.674 billion, the Netherlands 31.930 billion and Australia 30.366 billion, against the table's 660, 270, 245, 105, 42, 79, 34, 28, 25 and 25 billion. In the database the United Kingdom ranks above Germany, Belgium above Canada, and Spain below Indonesia. Some levels are also inconsistent with the 1906 report: the 1906 table gives China 410 billion, British India 350 billion, the United Kingdom 295 billion and the Netherlands Indies 55 billion, all higher than the 1907 figures, and Spain 60 billion, lower. The levels should not be used to compute growth; the growth column comes directly from the database. Growth figures of 0.2% for China, 4.9% for Germany, 2.6% for Spain and 5.5% for Canada from a processed annual interpolation of the 2018 release are shown only in the Notes.

Conflicting series: U.S. unemployment is 2.80% in the Mitchell-based Macrohistory series and 1.8% in Lebergott's estimate; UK share-price reconstructions from Grossman and from Campbell, Grossman and Turner differ, though they share underlying material; Japan's official Bank of Japan bill rate (7.30% at year-end) differs from the 8.69% annual short rate in the Macrohistory series; the positive Milan equity return is hard to reconcile with the Italian banking crisis; the Netherlands Indies postal-savings balance was revised from f7,227,554 to f7,227,608; and printed Java sugar components and Javasche loan subtotals contain arithmetic discrepancies. These are not averaged or silently corrected. Market short-term rates for Germany, France, Italy, Spain, Belgium, the Netherlands and Sweden are shown only in Notes because they are not official discount rates. The Belgian current-account field in the Macrohistory Database is a merchandise balance and is not shown; the Dutch current account omits separately identified secondary transfers. German and Australian exchange rates are not shown because the database stores them in later currency units, and French values are restated from new francs. No verified 1907 crude-oil benchmark in dollars per barrel is available, so no barrel price is given.

Weakest claims: Bottom-rank ordering from ranks 12-20 is the weakest part of the document. The inclusion and ordering of Dutch East Indies, Ottoman Empire, Belgium, Mexico, Argentina, Australia, Netherlands, Sweden, Brazil, Switzerland, Denmark and Portugal depends heavily on boundary and data-source choices. Growth for China, British India, the Dutch East Indies, Austria-Hungary, the Russian Empire and the Ottoman Empire is unavailable on a matching territory.

Important data that simply does not exist in comparable form for 1907: global CPI, global unemployment, harmonized national unemployment rates, current-account balances as percent of GDP for all top-20 economies, government debt-to-GDP for all top-20 economies, budget balances as percent of GDP for all top-20 economies, global equity index returns, global credit spreads, global corporate revenue rankings, comprehensive city-level real-estate price indices, telecommunications and defense output, and R&D spending. Data not available means that no reconciled observation with the stated definition and territory was found in the sources used, not that no historical record exists; for some fiscal rows raw receipts and outlays exist but have not been reconciled to a GDP denominator.

Use recommendation: The ranking table is a research-grade narrative scaffold, not a final statistical database. A reproducible ranking would download MPD 2023 directly, filter year 1907, multiply GDP per capita by population when necessary, and record whether modern boundaries or historical polities are used.
