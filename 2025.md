# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 2025

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The world economy in 2025 was defined by resilience under pressure. Global output continued to expand even after several years of inflation, high interest rates, geopolitical fragmentation and tariff uncertainty. The IMF's April 2026 World Economic Outlook estimated 2025 world output growth at 3.4 percent, while the OECD's late-2025 assessment, made before the year had ended, put global growth at 3.2 percent. The difference is not a contradiction so much as a reminder that 2025 data were still being revised in early 2026. The October 2026 IMF World Economic Outlook database puts world real growth at 3.505 percent using PPP weights and 2.931 percent using market-exchange-rate weights; these are distinct aggregations, not conflicting estimates of the same object. The same vintage estimates world current-dollar GDP at US$118.485 trillion. All of these 2025 figures are estimates that can still be revised, and they were not known during 2025 itself. The broad conclusion is consistent across sources: the global economy avoided recession, but growth was uneven and increasingly dependent on a few engines.

The most important engines were the United States, India, parts of emerging Asia, services, and the AI-linked investment cycle. The global semiconductor industry rebounded sharply, AI-related venture investment surged, and data-center construction became a macroeconomic issue because it affected power demand, capital expenditure, equity valuations and industrial policy. Revenues, announced spending envelopes, actual capital expenditure and domestic value added must nevertheless be kept separate: Microsoft's fiscal year ended in June 2025, NVIDIA's fiscal 2026 ended in January 2026, and TSMC's global investment was not all located in Taiwan. China met its growth target, but its growth mix depended heavily on exports, manufacturing policy and stimulus while private domestic demand and property remained weak. Europe lagged, with Spain outperforming while Germany's old export-manufacturing model looked strained.

Inflation continued to fall from the extreme levels of 2021 to 2023, but it did not disappear as a policy problem. The IMF April 2026 estimate put world consumer-price inflation at 4.1 percent for 2025 (4.131 percent in the October 2026 vintage), with advanced economies near 2.5 percent and emerging and developing economies above 5 percent. Central banks therefore eased only carefully, and monetary policy diverged. The Federal Reserve, Bank of England and European Central Bank ended the year less tight than at the peak of the cycle, but policy remained restrictive compared with the 2010s. Japan was the exception in regime terms, raising its overnight call-rate target to about 0.75 percent, and Brazil raised its Selic target to 15 percent during the year.

Trade produced one of the year's surprises. Early in 2025, tariff uncertainty and trade-policy conflict made a trade slowdown plausible. Instead, world goods and services trade expanded strongly in value terms: WTO data show the dollar value of goods and commercial services rising 8 percent to US$34.89 trillion, a nominal balance-of-payments measure rather than a volume measure, and UNCTAD data point in the same direction. Front-loading before tariff changes, services expansion and AI-related goods all contributed. The same pattern also created fragility: trade growth was not proof that globalization had returned to the pre-2016 model. It was partly an inventory and timing effect, and partly a new technology-cycle effect.

The year also exposed a public-finance and market-structure problem. Long-term bond yields stayed high or rose in many countries even as short-term policy rates stabilized or fell. Public debt, defense spending, energy-security investment, aging populations and infrastructure needs competed for fiscal space. Germany's infrastructure fund illustrates the gap between authorization and spending: EUR 500 billion of twelve-year borrowing authority was not EUR 500 billion spent in 2025, and preliminary 2025 disbursements were EUR 24 billion. Equity markets, especially in the United States and Asia, rewarded AI-linked firms, while gold reached a record annual average. In hindsight, 2025 was not a crisis year; it was a balance-sheet and allocation year in which vulnerabilities accumulated beneath apparently solid headline growth.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 2025

**1. Resilient growth with weaker safety margins.** The significance of 2025 in economic history is that the global economy absorbed high rates, tariff uncertainty and geopolitical stress without a synchronized recession. IMF and OECD assessments agree on continued global expansion. Yet resilience was not evenly distributed. The United States, India and emerging Asia carried more of the growth load, while Germany, Italy, Mexico and parts of Europe were weak. The forward linkage is that 2026 began with less room for policy mistakes: public debt was high, private credit had grown, and long-term yields were less forgiving.

**2. AI moved from a technology story to a macro driver.** In earlier years, AI was mostly discussed as a productivity possibility. In 2025, it became measurable in semiconductor sales, venture capital, data centers, electricity demand, equity-market concentration and capital expenditure. The Semiconductor Industry Association reported calendar-2025 sales of US$791.7 billion, up 25.6 percent, and OECD research reported that AI firms accounted for more than half of global venture-capital investment. Chips, servers, buildings and power became complements to software. The United States, South Korea, Taiwan, the Netherlands, Japan and China were most affected. The forward linkage is double-edged: faster productivity is possible, but disappointment could destabilize investment plans and equity valuations, and concentration and capacity-utilization risks were already identifiable.

**3. Trade fragmentation coexisted with trade growth.** The year showed that higher trade barriers do not automatically mean a collapse in trade volumes. The US reciprocal-tariff order of 2 April, the 9 April pause, the 12 May China arrangement and the revised schedule effective 7 August show that announcement, effective date, product exemption and all-in duty are separate facts. Firms front-loaded shipments, rerouted supply chains and invested in alternative suppliers. WTO and UNCTAD data show strong 2025 trade values, but the causes were partly defensive. China, Mexico, the United States, Germany, South Korea, Japan and Southeast Asia were most exposed. The forward linkage is that inventory cycles and tariff timing can flatter trade data temporarily while reducing efficiency over time.

**4. Disinflation did not restore the 2010s policy regime.** Inflation fell, but the interest-rate environment did not return to near zero. The Federal Reserve cut to a 3.50 to 3.75 percent target range by year end, the Bank of England ended at 3.75 percent, and the ECB held its deposit rate at 2.00 percent in December after earlier easing. Japan's move away from ultra-easy policy was historically significant, and Brazil's Selic target rose from 12.25 to 15 percent, so easing was not universal. The countries most affected were those with high household debt, high public debt or interest-sensitive housing markets, including Canada, Australia, the United Kingdom and parts of Europe.

**5. China became a global demand and supply imbalance story.** China reached roughly 5 percent real growth, but the IMF flagged weak private demand, muted inflation and export reliance. Preliminary national accounts assign 1.6 percentage points of growth to net exports and 2.6 points to final consumption, while real-estate development investment fell 17.2 percent, so exports were important without being the sole growth engine. This mattered globally because China's industrial scale affects prices, margins and investment in autos, batteries, solar, steel, shipbuilding and electronics. The countries most affected included Germany, South Korea, Japan, the United States, India, Mexico and emerging manufacturing economies. The forward linkage is rising trade conflict if domestic demand does not absorb more Chinese output.

**6. Energy shifted from shortage fears to oversupply in oil, while electrification demand rose.** Oil prices fell in 2025 because supply growth outpaced demand, with IEA and EIA data describing large inventory builds and an average Brent price well below the 2022-2024 energy-shock period. At the same time, electricity demand from AI data centers and electrification became more important. Oil exporters such as Saudi Arabia and Russia faced a different macro constraint than in the price-spike years. The forward link is that energy security, grid investment and commodity volatility will remain central to inflation and fiscal outcomes.

**7. Fiscal space became the binding constraint.** Public debt and rising sovereign borrowing needs were central to the OECD and BIS assessments. Germany's 2025 fiscal-framework reform was one of the most important policy shifts in Europe because it opened room for infrastructure and defense spending, but fiscal authorization was not expenditure: the macro effect depends on outturns and disbursements, as the German infrastructure fund and Italian recovery-plan payment milestones show. In many countries, the problem was not only the debt stock, but the cost and term structure of financing. The forward linkage is a steeper trade-off among defense, welfare, climate investment, aging and tax capacity.

**8. Data revisions changed apparent comparisons.** India and Nigeria rebased their national accounts, Taiwan's annual growth was revised in 2026, and the US federal shutdown interrupted consumer-price collection. Rankings and real-growth comparisons for 2025 therefore depend on the data vintage and source definitions as well as arithmetic.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

World GDP in current US dollars for 2025 is estimated at US$118,485.328 billion in the IMF World Economic Outlook database of October 2026, with current-price PPP GDP of 210,789.697 billion international dollars. These are IMF world composites, not a sum of the twenty economies in section 4, and the IMF gives no universal actual or estimate marker for them; they should be read as estimates. World Bank WDI current-dollar GDP, in the WDI release used for the level comparison, which runs through 2024, serves only as a continuity cross-check. For real activity, the IMF April 2026 estimate gives 2025 world output growth of 3.4 percent; the October 2026 vintage gives 3.505 percent on the PPP-weighted measure and 2.931 percent on the market-exchange-rate-weighted measure; the OECD late-2025 report gives 3.2 percent. All show steady expansion rather than recession.

World trade volume grew faster than output. The IMF April 2026 estimate for world trade volume in goods and services was 5.1 percent in 2025, and the October 2026 vintage gives 4.969 percent. WTO data put the nominal value of goods and commercial services on a balance-of-payments basis at US$34.89 trillion, up 8 percent, with services representing 27.5 percent of the total; UNCTAD trade-value releases describe a similar dollar-value expansion. AI-related goods, services trade and front-loaded imports played important roles. Value and volume measures differ, and differences from customs merchandise data or other organizations' estimates are not resolved by rounding. This means trade data for 2025 should not be read as a simple return to frictionless globalization.

Reserve-currency dynamics changed slowly. The IMF COFER note for the second quarter of 2025 reported that the US dollar's reserve share was little changed in exchange-rate-adjusted terms. The dollar remained the core reserve and funding currency, but diversification into gold and selected non-dollar assets continued. Gold's 2025 performance is consistent with reserve diversification, geopolitical hedging and investor demand; a measured matrix of reserve flows and international capital flows is not available.

Global inflation cooled but remained above the pre-pandemic norm. IMF staff estimated world consumer-price inflation at 4.1 percent in 2025 in April 2026 (4.131 percent in October 2026, a composite using logarithmic aggregation rather than a simple average of national rates), advanced-economy inflation at 2.5 percent and emerging-market and developing-economy inflation at 5.2 percent. The key global commodity price movements were oil down 14.4 percent in the IMF April 2026 commodity-price table, nonfuel commodities up 9.6 percent, and gold at a record annual average according to World Gold Council data. International capital flows favored the United States and AI-linked equity markets, while high yields kept pressure on weaker sovereign and corporate borrowers.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The 2025 interest-rate environment was one of cautious easing, not a full return to easy money. The Federal Reserve's target range moved from 4.25-4.50 percent entering 2025 to 4.00-4.25 percent on 18 September, 3.75-4.00 percent on 30 October and 3.50-3.75 percent on 11 December. Its December decision to purchase short-term Treasury securities was framed as maintaining ample reserves, not as a new stimulus program. The ECB deposit facility rate moved from 3.00 percent to 2.75 percent effective 5 February, 2.50 percent on 12 March, 2.25 percent on 23 April and 2.00 percent on 11 June, and was then held, including in December; the main refinancing and marginal lending rates were different instruments.

The Bank of England cut Bank Rate to 3.75 percent by a 5-4 vote at the meeting ending 17 December, with publication on 18 December. The Bank of Japan remained the main regime-change story: its call-rate target of about 0.50 percent took effect on 27 January, followed by about 0.75 percent after the 19 December decision, continuing the move away from the ultra-low-rate framework that had defined Japanese policy for years. In Brazil, the Selic target reached 15 percent from 12.25 percent at the start of the year. The country policy rates in section 4 are dated year-end or latest-decision endpoints, not annual averages or comparable commercial borrowing rates.

Exchange rates remained important because nominal GDP rankings are expressed in current US dollars. The 2025 IMF ranking put the United Kingdom above India in nominal current-dollar GDP, while India remained far larger on a PPP basis and much faster-growing in real terms. This is a reminder that nominal rankings reflect exchange rates, inflation, market prices and timing, not only real output.

Credit conditions were mixed. Large firms, especially in technology and AI infrastructure, had strong access to capital. Smaller firms, commercial real estate borrowers and lower-rated issuers faced tighter conditions. OECD debt work and BIS analysis both emphasized that long-term yields and sovereign issuance needs were becoming more important than short-term policy rates alone. An internationally comparable panel of loan rates and credit spreads is not available.

Equity markets were strong, led by AI and semiconductor exposure. US indices posted double-digit returns, with the S&P 500 price return around 16 percent and the Nasdaq above 20 percent in market data. Hong Kong, Japan, Germany and parts of China also performed strongly. Bond markets were more uncomfortable: long-term yields stayed elevated as investors priced fiscal supply, inflation risk and less central-bank buying.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

Trade policy uncertainty was one of the defining features of 2025. The US executive order of 2 April provided for a 10 percent additional reciprocal duty effective 5 April and differentiated country schedules from 9 April; the 9 April modification suspended most differentiated rates temporarily, excluding China; the 12 May arrangement with China applied a 10 percent reciprocal component from 14 May, including Hong Kong and Macau, without removing other applicable tariff layers; and a revised schedule ordered on 31 July generally took effect on 7 August. Exceptions, transit rules, the treatment of Canada and Mexico, and separate Section 232 or other duties all mattered. Tariff changes and threats affected shipment timing, inventory management and investment decisions. WTO releases initially warned about trade downside risk, but later data showed stronger-than-expected goods trade because firms front-loaded shipments and AI-related products grew quickly. The United States, China, Mexico, Germany, Japan, South Korea and Southeast Asian exporters were most exposed.

Geopolitics shaped energy, defense spending, sanctions and supply chains. Russia remained under extensive sanctions, with its economy shaped by war-related state spending and import restrictions, which also constrained commercial and statistical transparency. China's export model generated greater pushback from trading partners. Europe increased attention to defense capacity and energy security. ASEAN and India benefited from supply-chain diversification, but not all nearshoring expectations turned into immediate investment. Red Sea disruption affected shipping and Egypt's external receipts (Egypt is outside the nominal top 20); the US$6 billion Suez revenue loss cited in the IMF's 2025 Egypt consultation refers to 2024 compared with 2023, not to 2025. Announced investment or energy-purchase commitments in trade agreements are not verified 2025 disbursements.

Regional integration was mixed. The European Union remained a deep market, but its internal growth divergence widened. ASEAN benefited from manufacturing and trade rerouting. North America remained highly integrated, yet US-Mexico-Canada trade was more politically uncertain than in earlier years. The Middle East's economic story centered on oil, sovereign investment and diversification, with Saudi Arabia still highly influential.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil was the clearest commodity reversal. EIA data describe crude oil prices falling in 2025 as oversupply developed, with Brent monthly averages of about US$79 per barrel in January and about US$63 in December and an annual average of about US$69 per barrel; these are monthly and annual averages, not single-day quotations. EIA also identified temporary support from attacks on energy infrastructure. IEA data describe supply growth near 3 million barrels per day versus demand growth below 1 million barrels per day, producing inventory builds. The IMF's average oil-price measure was US$67.74 per barrel for 2025 in the April 2026 commodity table.

Gold was the opposite story. The World Gold Council reported a record annual average LBMA PM gold price of about US$3,431 per troy ounce, 44 percent above the previous annual average, 53 new all-time highs during the year and total demand including OTC above 5,000 tonnes. The 44 percent change compares annual averages and is not a year-end investment return. Gold's strength reflected central-bank demand, investment demand, geopolitical hedging and concern about fiscal and currency regimes.

Metals and agricultural commodities were mixed. Copper and critical minerals benefited from electrification, grid investment and AI-infrastructure narratives, but demand was sensitive to China construction and global manufacturing. Agricultural staples were affected by weather, fertilizer costs, trade restrictions and currency movements. ### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Global labor markets were stable but less tight than in 2022-2023. ILO and OECD material pointed to unemployment rates near historically low levels in many advanced economies, but with signs of weakening and persistent youth, gender and informality challenges. OECD unemployment was around 4.9 percent in mid-2025, but country definitions are not directly comparable. The ILO's January 2026 report puts global youth unemployment at 12.4 percent in 2025; its 4.9 percent global unemployment headline is a projection for 2026, not an observed 2025 value.

Demographics were a major hidden driver. India and Indonesia benefited from young populations and labor-force expansion. Japan, Germany, South Korea, Italy and China faced aging and labor scarcity. Migration mattered for Canada, Australia, Spain, the United Kingdom and the United States because it affected labor supply, housing demand and public-service capacity. Population series for 2025 are estimates or statistical compilations, not census counts.

Wages and productivity did not move uniformly. Japan's wage gains were historically important because they supported the shift away from deflation psychology. In the United States, real-income gains were uneven and asset-price gains favored wealthier households. In Europe, wage recovery supported consumption but also kept services inflation sticky. The Bank of England cited unemployment of 5.1 percent for the three months to October and slower private-sector wage growth. The structural labor-market theme was clear: firms responded to labor scarcity with automation, AI, outsourcing, migration and higher wages, but productivity gains were still uncertain.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States remained the largest economy by nominal GDP, with growth of about 2 to 2.3 percent in IMF estimates despite tight credit, fiscal pressure and changing trade policy. The dominant macro story was resilience: household spending, corporate services and AI-related investment supported activity even as housing, small firms and interest-sensitive sectors felt the lagged effect of high rates. The Federal Reserve ended the year with a 3.50 to 3.75 percent target range after three cuts from September, but policy was still restrictive by post-2010 standards.

At the micro level, the United States was the main center of the AI capital-expenditure cycle. Data centers, semiconductors, cloud platforms and electricity demand became visible macro variables, not only technology-sector stories. Consumer balance sheets remained uneven: higher-income households benefited from asset gains, while lower-income households faced rent, insurance, food and credit-card pressure. Commercial real estate and smaller banks stayed watch-list items, but no broad systemic banking crisis materialized.

Measurement was interrupted during the year: the federal shutdown suspended consumer-price collection from 1 October through 12 November, so affected annual averages are built from eleven monthly indices rather than a normal twelve-month mean. Dated reciprocal-tariff instruments materially changed import costs, with exemptions and later amendments.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** AI infrastructure, cloud services, semiconductors, defense spending, health care and consumer services led. Housing, office real estate and rate-sensitive durables were weaker. Major listed-company value creation concentrated in platform technology and semiconductor firms. Microsoft's cash additions to property and equipment were US$64.551 billion in the fiscal year ended 30 June 2025, against US$44.477 billion a year earlier; this is company-wide fiscal-year investment, not calendar-2025 or AI-only spending. Nippon Steel completed its acquisition of U.S. Steel on 18 June, and Spirit Airlines filed Chapter 11 petitions on 29 August while continuing to fly. The FHFA national house-price index rose 1.8 percent from 2024 Q4 to 2025 Q4, which is a price measure, not an affordability or disposable-income measure.

**Economic narrative:**

China grew near its official target, but the composition of growth exposed a structural tension. Exports, manufacturing upgrading and policy stimulus kept real GDP near 5 percent, while private domestic demand remained weak and headline inflation was roughly flat. The property-sector adjustment continued to weigh on household confidence, local-government finance and construction-linked supply chains.

The National Bureau of Statistics' preliminary communique attributes 2.6 percentage points of growth to final consumption, 0.8 points to capital formation and 1.6 points to net exports, so exports were important but not the sole growth engine. Real-estate development investment fell 17.2 percent on a comparable, revised prior-year basis to CNY 8,278.8 billion; this is an investment flow, not a house-price decline.

China's micro story was industrial strength paired with balance-sheet stress. Electric vehicles, batteries, solar equipment, shipbuilding and electronics exports remained powerful, but overcapacity debates intensified across trade partners. The policy challenge was to shift from investment and exports toward household income and consumption without destabilizing local debt, property developers or banks. This imbalance became one of the largest spillover issues in the world economy.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** EVs, batteries, solar, machinery and export manufacturing were strong. Residential property, local-government financing vehicles and some consumer-facing services remained weak; real-estate development investment fell 17.2 percent. ### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Labor markets were stable but not uniform. Advanced-economy unemployment remained low by historical standards, but many countries showed softer hiring. Youth unemployment, informality and gender gaps remained persistent in developing and emerging economies; the ILO puts global youth unemployment at 12.4 percent in 2025. In India, Indonesia, Brazil and Mexico, the quality and formality of jobs mattered as much as headline employment, and national unemployment definitions in IMF data (such as India's current-weekly-status measure) are not interchangeable with ILO-modelled series.

Sectoral employment shifted toward services, technology infrastructure, health care, logistics and construction linked to public investment or data centers. Manufacturing employment was more complex: high-value chip and equipment jobs expanded, while traditional export manufacturing faced automation and China-driven competition. Aging economies used immigration, automation and higher wages to offset labor scarcity.

Union and wage dynamics mattered most in Japan, Europe and parts of North America. Japan's wage gains supported a historic monetary-policy transition. ### 6B. Fixed income

Fixed income was less comfortable than equities. OECD debt analysis reported that long-term government bond yields continued to rise in 2025 while short- and medium-term yields stabilized, producing yield-curve steepening. The drivers were larger sovereign borrowing needs, reduced central-bank bond buying, inflation risk and changing investor demand. The US 10-year Treasury annual average was about 4.29 percent in market data, an approximate figure; a complete official annual table for all major 10-year yields and credit spreads is Data not available. Central-bank policy targets are not ten-year government yields, and Germany's federal cash borrowing of EUR 66.9 billion is a budget flow, not a bond-market yield.

Sovereign debt events were mostly slow-moving rather than sudden defaults among the top 20. The more important story was fiscal pressure: defense, aging, infrastructure, health care and interest costs competed for budget space. IMF October 2026 data show gross general government debt above 100 percent of GDP in the United States, Japan (an IMF staff estimate), Italy, France, Canada, the United Kingdom and Spain. Government debt stocks, annual borrowing flows and interest costs are distinct measures. Emerging-market borrowers with weaker credibility remained more exposed to dollar funding and rollover risk.

### 6C. Currencies

The US dollar remained the central reserve, invoicing and funding currency. IMF COFER material reported little change in the dollar's reserve share on an exchange-rate-adjusted basis in the second quarter of 2025. Nevertheless, gold demand and diversification interest showed that reserve managers and investors were hedging geopolitical, fiscal and inflation risks.

Annual-average exchange rates from the World Bank WDI show the euro at 0.8850 per dollar (a 4.21 percent fall in euros per dollar, meaning euro appreciation), sterling at 0.7595 (-2.93 percent), the yen at 149.6579 (-1.13 percent) and the ruble at 83.6525 (-9.62 percent), while the Turkish lira (+20.27 percent), Mexican peso (+5.10 percent), Korean won (+4.33 percent), Indian rupee (+4.17 percent), Indonesian rupiah (+3.93 percent) and Brazilian real (+3.68 percent) depreciated. These are changes in annual-average quotations, not year-end investor returns, and verified end-year pair movements are Data not available. The qualitative currency context was that current-dollar GDP rankings were exchange-rate sensitive. This matters for comparing the United Kingdom, India, Japan, Germany, Russia and Brazil in nominal GDP terms.

Capital controls and sanctions mattered most for Russia and, in a different institutional form, China. Russia's financial system operated under wartime sanctions and restrictions, and immobilized reserve assets complicate interpretation of its reserves. China retained managed capital-account features that shaped exchange-rate and financial-market transmission. Saudi Arabia's riyal remained pegged at 3.75 per dollar.

### 6D. Alternative assets

Gold was the standout alternative asset. The World Gold Council reported a record annual average LBMA PM price of about US$3,431 per ounce, 44 percent above the previous annual average, 53 new highs and annual demand including OTC above 5,000 tonnes. The average-price change is not a buy-and-hold return. Gold's rise linked financial markets, central-bank reserves and geopolitical risk.

Real estate was divided. Residential affordability was a major issue in Canada, Australia, the United Kingdom, the Netherlands, Spain and parts of the United States. The FHFA national house-price index rose 1.8 percent from 2024 Q4 to 2025 Q4, 0.8 percent quarter on quarter and 0.1 percent in the seasonally adjusted December monthly index; these cover different periods and adjustments. ## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The most important active risks in 2025 were trade fragmentation, public debt, China's property and demand imbalance, oil-market volatility, AI-valuation concentration, commercial real estate, and geopolitical conflict affecting sanctions, energy and defense spending. None produced a synchronized global recession in 2025, but several increased fragility.

Trade-policy risk materialized through front-loading and rerouting rather than immediate collapse. Tariff uncertainty was an implemented policy risk rather than simply a forecast: dated instruments changed duties, suspensions and exceptions. This means the data can be misleading: strong 2025 trade growth may partly represent shipments pulled forward from future periods, and the annual trade total alone cannot separate genuine demand from inventory timing. The risk that did not fully materialize in 2025 was a broad trade-volume contraction. The risk that did materialize was reduced predictability for investment and supply chains.

China's property-sector adjustment remained a slow-moving crisis. It did not create a sudden global financial crash, but it weakened domestic demand, local-government finance and household confidence. The global spillover was excess manufacturing supply and greater dependence on exports. This became a source of trade conflict.

Public debt and long-term yields were a systemic vulnerability. The problem was not a single sovereign default among the top economies. The problem was that many governments needed to finance defense, aging, infrastructure, green investment and interest costs at the same time. German fund transfers and Italian payment assessments show why loosely defined fiscal arithmetic can overstate realized demand. Hindsight label: 2025 looks like a year in which bond investors began enforcing a stricter fiscal constraint even as central banks cut short-term rates.

AI was both upside and black-swan risk. The upside was faster productivity and new investment. The downside was an investment bubble, power bottlenecks, equity concentration or a sudden repricing if revenue failed to match infrastructure spending. Company revenues and spending are observable anchors; assumptions about future margins, utilization, electricity availability and productivity are not. In 2025, optimism dominated. The risk was building rather than resolved. Spirit Airlines' bankruptcy illustrated sector-specific fragility rather than a broad corporate-credit event, and the absence of a named systemic failure does not amount to a completed banking stress test.

Oil was a surprise in the opposite direction from 2022. Instead of an oil-price spike, 2025 delivered lower prices because supply growth exceeded demand growth. This helped inflation and oil-importing consumers, but pressured exporters and contributed to OPEC+ tensions. The unresolved risk was that geopolitical disruption could reverse the oversupply story quickly.

Measurement itself was a risk. Nigeria's and India's national-account rebasing, the interruption of US price collection and Russia's restricted disclosure of gross trade are analytically relevant, not mere footnotes. The October 2026 IMF data and 2026 company reports are used to assess 2025 retrospectively; they do not show what contemporaneous decision makers knew, and later 2026 events or forecasts are not treated as events of 2025.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (2025 vs. 2024)

The biggest 2025 versus 2024 change was the shift from inflation shock management to post-shock allocation. In 2024, many economies were still focused on whether disinflation could proceed without recession. In 2025, that question was largely answered in the affirmative, but new questions appeared: who pays for higher debt, who benefits from AI, and who absorbs China's manufacturing surplus?

Nominal GDP rankings shifted but did not transform. The top-20 membership was unchanged in the IMF April 2026 estimates. The United Kingdom moved above India in nominal current-dollar terms, even though India remained much faster-growing and much larger on a PPP basis. Russia moved up in nominal ranking versus 2024, partly reflecting exchange-rate, inflation and wartime-economy measurement effects, including a 9.62 percent fall in the annual-average ruble-per-dollar rate. Poland was close to the cutoff but remained rank 21.

Real growth changes were uneven in the IMF October 2026 data: growth slowed sharply in Russia (-3.921 pp), Brazil (-1.133 pp), Spain (-1.080 pp), South Korea (-1.054 pp) and Mexico (-0.963 pp), and picked up in Saudi Arabia (+1.946 pp) and Japan (+1.094 pp). Inflation fell steeply in Turkey (-23.622 pp) and India (-2.590 pp), while it rose in the United Kingdom (+0.843 pp). Gross government debt ratios rose in most of the economies covered, most strongly in China (+8.104 pp) and Brazil (+6.301 pp).

Trade accelerated versus the pessimistic early-2025 forecasts. Goods and services trade grew strongly by value, and the IMF estimate for trade volume exceeded world output growth. The delta is important: 2025 trade strength was not simply a healthy globalization signal. It partly reflected tariff front-loading and AI-related hardware demand.

Commodity conditions changed materially. Oil fell from earlier elevated levels, easing inflation pressure for importers and limiting fiscal upside for exporters. Gold surged to records, with the annual average 44 percent above that of 2024, signaling demand for hedges against geopolitical and monetary uncertainty. This combination, weak oil and strong gold, captures the unusual mix of lower near-term energy inflation and higher structural uncertainty.

Policy regimes changed at the margin. The Fed's target bounds fell 0.75 percentage point, the ECB deposit rate fell 1.00 point and Brazil's Selic target rose 2.75 points, while Japan moved further out of ultra-easy policy. Germany's fiscal-framework reform was a structural break with implications beyond 2025, although the preliminary annual flow was much smaller than the new borrowing authority. AI investment also changed the industrial-policy map: semiconductor sales rose 25.6 percent, power grids, chips, data centers and advanced manufacturing became central macro issues. Brazilian agriculture grew 11.7 percent while manufacturing contracted in the same national release, showing why a broad expansion headline can conceal opposite micro outcomes. Statistical deltas also include rebasing and revised estimates, which are not newly produced goods.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

Highest-confidence data in this report are the IMF WEO aggregate growth, inflation, trade-volume and commodity-price estimates; OECD global outlook and labor-market assessments; WTO and UNCTAD trade releases; IEA and EIA oil-market summaries; dated central-bank policy decisions from the Federal Reserve, ECB, Bank of England, Bank of Japan and the other central banks named in section 4; and World Gold Council gold-demand and price summaries. Even these are, for 2025, estimates that remain subject to revision.

The top-20 country ranking is medium-high confidence as an IMF WEO April 2026 estimate tabulated by Worldometer and checked against IMF WEO tables where possible. It is not the same as final national statistical office data. World Bank WDI current-dollar GDP at the time of that ranking only extended to 2024, so the World Bank could not provide a final 2025 current-dollar ranking cross-check. The ranking should therefore be labeled an IMF estimate, not final history.

**Vintages, units and status.** GDP levels and GDP per capita in section 4 are current-dollar IMF WEO April 2026 estimates and are not rebased; populations are derived from them and rounded. Real GDP growth, CPI inflation, unemployment, gross government debt, the current account and the government balance for seventeen economies come from the IMF WEO database of October 2026 (published 13 October 2026), a later retrospective vintage; policy rates come from the central banks named and exchange rates from the World Bank WDI. Where the IMF marks a 2025 value as within its latest-actual range, this report says "IMF-marked actual"; such values are preliminary and not certified final. India's unemployment, debt and fiscal balance and the debt and fiscal balance of Japan and South Korea are IMF staff estimates beyond their latest-actual markers. Current-price PPP levels in international dollars, based on ICP benchmarks for 2017-2021, carry no IMF actual/estimate marker and appear only as supplementary comparisons; PPP and US$ levels are not spliced, and current-PPP level changes include price and conversion effects, so they are not real growth. World aggregates have no universal status marker. India's 2025 values cover FY2025/26 (April 2025-March 2026), which extends beyond the calendar year; India's national accounts were rebased in 2026. For Australia, the Netherlands and Switzerland only the April 2026 estimates are available, so their indicator rows other than the shared euro-area values for the Netherlands are Data not available. Exchange rates are World Bank WDI annual averages in local currency per US dollar. The OECD 3.2 percent world growth figure was an estimate published before the year ended, and the ILO's 4.9 percent global unemployment figure is a 2026 projection.

**Conflicting data.** Real growth differs by IMF vintage: the United States 2.344 percent (October 2026) against 2.12 percent (April 2026), Spain 2.592 against 2.77, the United Kingdom 1.213 against 1.32, India 7.819 against 7.62, Canada 1.853 against 1.74, South Korea 1.139 against 1.01, Turkey 3.738 against 3.60, Mexico 0.497 against 0.56 and Saudi Arabia 4.595 against 4.50; Brazil's IBGE figure is 2.3 percent. World growth is 3.4 percent in the April 2026 estimate against 3.505 percent (PPP weights) or 2.931 percent (market weights) in October 2026, world CPI inflation 4.1 against 4.131 percent and world trade volume 5.1 against 4.969 percent. The October 2026 population estimates differ slightly from the derived April 2026 populations (for example Spain 49.452 million against about 49.7 million, Indonesia 284.670 million against about 284.5 million). WTO's 8 percent nominal trade-value growth and UNCTAD's estimate near 7 percent use different coverage, and IMF commodity-price figures from April 2026 (oil -14.4 percent, average US$67.74 per barrel) differ in definition from the EIA Brent annual average near US$69. These differences are flagged rather than reconciled. The IMF and World Bank current-PPP compilations share national-account and ICP inputs, so their agreement is not independent confirmation, and World Bank PPP coverage omits Taiwan.

Country table gaps remain. Several indicators require country-by-country final 2025 official releases, and where a figure was not available, it is marked Data not available rather than estimated. This is especially important for unemployment because definitions vary across countries and are not directly comparable. Fiscal coverage also differs: China's IMF debt measure includes part of LGFV issuance, and US general government data are calendar-year values compiled from federal fiscal-year inputs. The US CPI annual average reflects eleven monthly observations because of the 2025 federal shutdown.

Data confidence is lower for Russia because sanctions, capital controls, wartime state spending and restricted data make GDP, inflation, fiscal and corporate statistics harder to interpret; gross exports and imports in Russia's national accounts are IMF staff estimates. The same caution applies, in different form, to countries where inflation, exchange-rate management or large informal sectors complicate measurement.

Financial-market data are mixed confidence. US index returns are high confidence because they are widely reported by index providers and major financial data firms. Non-US annual index returns in this report are medium confidence because they rely on market reporting rather than a single official global index table. Index levels are marked Data not available because matched start and end levels were not available.

**Measurement caveats.** Announced borrowing authority (Germany's EUR 500 billion fund), payment approvals (Italy's EUR 12.8 billion request) and trade-agreement commitments differ from executed spending and disbursements. Company figures for Microsoft, NVIDIA, Walmart and the Fortune Global 500 are fiscal-year measures, not calendar 2025. Policy rates are dated endpoints, not annual averages. The UK unemployment rate of 5.1 percent covers the three months to October, the COFER reserve share refers to the second quarter of 2025, and the FHFA house-price change compares 2025 Q4 with 2024 Q4.

Important data not available here include complete 2025 country indicators for Australia, the Netherlands and Switzerland; exchange-rate observations for every economy as end-year quotations; complete country-level policy-rate paths; complete 10-year government-bond yield tables; verified annual FX-pair changes; comparable real-estate price data across major cities; complete global IPO proceeds and M&A totals; R&D and patent counts; labor skill, union and migration panels; reserve-currency and capital-flow matrices; and country-level sector GDP shares for 2025.
