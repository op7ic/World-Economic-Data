# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1991

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1991 was a hinge year in the economic history of the late twentieth century. Global output fell slightly in the IMF's 1992 accounting, with world output shown at -0.3 percent for 1991 after 2.2 percent growth in 1990. The result was not a synchronized depression, but a highly uneven shock: the United States, Canada, and the United Kingdom were in or near recession; Japan was still expanding but its asset-price bubble had already broken; western continental Europe was constrained by high German interest rates and the European Monetary System; and Eastern Europe plus the former Soviet Union suffered a historic contraction as command economies disintegrated.

Whether world GDP actually fell depends on the source and its weighting. The BIS contemporary table puts world real growth at -0.4% (2.1% in 1990), or 1.4% excluding its eastern European group, using 1989 GDP weights for the major industrial groups and non-commercial exchange rates for eastern output. Current composites are positive: the IMF World Economic Outlook (October 2026) world series shows 2.48% at purchasing-power weights and 2.02% at market weights, and current World Development Indicators show 1.225%. These estimates differ in weighting, coverage and vintage, and the newest database does not automatically settle the question.

The dominant structural forces were transition, disinflation, and financial adjustment. The Cold War economic order ended in practical terms as the Soviet Union dissolved in December 1991. Germany was still absorbing unification costs. Central and Eastern Europe were moving from administrative allocation to market prices, private ownership, and hard-budget constraints. The macro cost was severe: output in Eastern Europe and the former USSR fell at double-digit rates (the BIS records -17% on a row whose footnote assigns the 1991 observation to the CIS and earlier years to the former USSR, and -15% for Yugoslavia including Croatia, Slovenia and Bosnia-Hercegovina), while inflation accelerated sharply as price controls, fiscal institutions, and monetary controls broke down.

In the advanced economies, 1991 was a year of policy divergence. The Federal Reserve eased aggressively as the U.S. recession and credit stress persisted. The Bank of Japan began lowering rates after earlier tightening had punctured equity and land markets. Germany, by contrast, kept policy tight because unification raised fiscal demand, wages, and inflation pressure. That stance transmitted high rates across Europe through the EMS and set up strains that became much more visible in 1992.

Commodity conditions were shaped by the aftermath of the 1990 Gulf crisis. Oil prices averaged about USD 18.30 per barrel in IMF reporting, down from the wartime 1990 average of about USD 22.04, but still central to Middle Eastern, energy-importing, and inflation dynamics. Non-fuel commodity prices declined, copper softened, and many developing exporters faced weak terms of trade. At the same time, several emerging regions began to attract renewed capital inflows, especially Latin America after stabilization and debt restructuring efforts.

At the microeconomic level, 1991 was the beginning of several long-run shifts that were not fully obvious at the time: the spread of World Wide Web software beyond CERN, the first official GSM call (Helsinki, July 1991), early Linux kernel releases, the reopening and creation of post-socialist capital markets, and the intensification of Japanese banking and real-estate balance-sheet stress. The year is therefore best understood not only as a recession and transition year, but also as a platform year for the globalization, digitization, and financial liberalization of the 1990s.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1991

**1. Collapse of the Soviet economic system and transition shock.** The USSR dissolved in December 1991, but the economic rupture was already visible throughout the year. State orders, inter-republican trade, administered prices, and fiscal-monetary controls weakened simultaneously. The IMF estimated a 17 percent fall in output for the former USSR in 1991 and noted large price increases; the BIS shows the same -17% but assigns its 1991 observation to the CIS, and its accompanying -8% is industrial production, not an alternative GDP estimate. The breakdown of CMEA payments and trade, lost intermediate inputs and uncertain ownership could cut production before private markets became effective. This was significant because it ended the main non-market alternative to the OECD-led global economic model and created a vast new transition region. The forward linkage was direct: 1992 and 1993 brought deeper price liberalization, hyperinflation in several successor states, debt and payments problems, and major privatization programs.

**2. Advanced-economy recession with divergent policy reactions.** The United States, United Kingdom, and Canada entered recessionary conditions around 1990 to 1991. Financial adjustment after earlier credit booms, falling property values, weak consumer confidence, and bank balance-sheet repair restrained demand. The Federal Reserve eased; the Bank of England reduced rates only gradually because sterling was in the ERM; Japan began easing after the bubble broke; Germany stayed tight. The forward linkage was the 1992 European currency crisis, the long Japanese slowdown, and the U.S. recovery that began to strengthen in 1992.

**3. German unification as a European macro shock.** Germany moved from political unification in 1990 into the fiscal, monetary, labor-market, and industrial integration problems of 1991. Eastern German industry faced sudden exposure to market prices, hard currency wages, and western competition. Fiscal transfers supported demand but increased borrowing needs. The Bundesbank raised official rates in three stages during 1991, and wage settlements of nearly 7% worsened the inflation outlook; this anti-inflation stance contributed to high European interest rates. The forward linkage was stronger pressure on EMS parities, especially for high-debt or low-growth members.

**4. End of the Gulf crisis, oil normalization, and Middle East divergence.** Oil prices fell from the 1990 crisis peak after the U.S.-led coalition war ended and supply conditions normalized. Saudi Arabia expanded output sharply, while Kuwait and Iraq faced war damage, sanctions, and production disruption. Energy-importing countries received some relief, but exporters with fiscal dependence on oil faced price volatility. The forward linkage was the 1990s pattern of lower average oil prices and fiscal consolidation pressure in several exporting economies.

**5. Emerging-market stabilization and renewed capital inflows.** Latin America moved from the late-1980s debt-inflation crisis toward stabilization in several countries. Argentina introduced the Convertibility Plan in 1991; Brazil was still unstable, but regional inflation rates were falling from extremely high levels. Mexico continued liberalization ahead of NAFTA negotiations. Inflows, privatization receipts, new borrowing and debt forgiveness were distinct sources of financing, and imports could grow faster than exports. The significance was a shift from bank-debt crisis management toward market-based inflows, privatization receipts, Brady restructurings, and local-market development. The forward linkage was the 1994 Mexican crisis and a broader debate over capital account sequencing.

**6. Japan's bubble burst becomes a balance-sheet problem.** Japan's equity market had peaked at the end of 1989, and land-price stress was spreading through 1991. Output still grew by advanced-economy standards, but investment slowed and banks became increasingly exposed to collateral decline. The significance was not a one-year recession, but the start of a long balance-sheet adjustment that reshaped Japanese banking, corporate governance, household saving, and monetary policy for more than a decade.

**7. Early digital infrastructure and telecommunications transition.** The World Wide Web was announced outside CERN in August 1991 (CERN's formal public-domain declaration followed on 30 April 1993), the first official GSM call was made in Helsinki in July 1991, and Linux appeared as an experimental kernel in 1991. These were not yet macro-sized industries in 1991, but they created the technological substrate for the 1990s internet, mobile, open-source, semiconductor, and software cycles. The forward linkage was the acceleration of ICT investment, productivity debates, and the late-1990s technology equity boom.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The United States eased monetary policy through 1991 as recession, weak credit creation, and financial-sector caution weighed on activity. The effective federal funds rate moved lower over the year, and U.S. bond yields declined as inflation moderated. The policy stance was countercyclical, but banks and households were still repairing balance sheets after late-1980s credit excesses.

The United Kingdom was constrained by ERM membership. Sterling's exchange-rate commitment limited the speed of monetary easing even as the real economy contracted and property weakness hit banks and households. Canada also experienced recession and disinflation. Japan entered the post-bubble easing phase: after prior tightening to restrain speculation, the Bank of Japan lowered rates as equity and land-price declines began affecting investment and credit quality.

Germany was the outlier. Unification raised fiscal transfers, wage pressures, and domestic demand. The Bundesbank defended anti-inflation credibility with a firm stance, raising official rates in three stages during the year. Because many European currencies were tied to the Deutsche mark through the EMS, German tightness transmitted to countries with weaker growth. This policy mix became a major source of stress in 1992.

Instruments differed across central banks. The Federal Reserve's basic discount rate stood at 3.5% after the cut effective 20 December 1991. The Bank of Japan cut its official discount rate from 6% to 5.5% on 1 July, to 5% on 14 November and to 4.5% on 30 December; its June decision to abolish window guidance was a separate measure, and money-market and bank lending rates had become less mechanically tied to the discount rate. The Bank of England's contemporary view was that ERM membership permitted lower rates than would otherwise have preserved sterling's firmness. The BIS notes that long-term yields sometimes moved together internationally despite differing short rates. World banking aggregates contracted for the first time in the BIS series, but the BIS warned against reading this as a general international credit crunch: Japanese interbank contraction and altered bank strategies mattered, although this did not rule out tight credit for particular domestic borrowers.

Equity markets diverged. U.S. equities rallied strongly in anticipation of recovery and lower rates, with the S&P 500 ending 1991 at about 417 and producing a large positive annual return. The FTSE 100 rose about 16 percent on index-price measures. Japan's Nikkei remained far below its 1989 peak and continued the broad post-bubble adjustment. Several emerging and post-socialist markets were either newly reopened, thin, or not yet comparable with mature exchanges.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

GATT estimates 1991 merchandise export and import volume growth of 3% and 3% for the world, 6.5% and 0% for North America, 2% and 10.5% for Latin America, 2% and 3% for Western Europe, and 8.5% and 8.5% for Asia. Hong Kong, Korea, Malaysia, Singapore, Taiwan and Thailand together recorded 13% and 17%. GATT left the eastern European and former-USSR cells blank, while tentatively estimating that regional imports fell about 20%, with former-USSR imports perhaps more than 40% lower; import compression there reflected lost financing and missing inputs rather than improved competitiveness. The U.S. dollar appreciated about 2.5% against the ECU over the year, while the U.S. trade-weighted effective rate was virtually unchanged between year endpoints.

The Uruguay Round of GATT negotiations remained unresolved. Agriculture, subsidies, services, and intellectual-property issues were central sticking points, especially between the United States and the European Community. The stalled talks mattered because they delayed the multilateral trade settlement that would eventually produce the WTO in 1995.

The European Community continued the move toward the Single Market, scheduled for completion at the end of 1992. In 1991, the Maastricht negotiations moved toward monetary union, fiscal criteria, and a broader political-economic settlement, with political agreement reached in December 1991; the treaty and monetary institutions came later. The forward-looking tension was that European integration was advancing institutionally while macro conditions were diverging sharply.

The Cold War ended as an economic organizing framework. Trade, aid, and capital flows were redirected toward transition economies, but institutional capacity was weak. The former Soviet Union and Eastern Europe needed stabilization finance, trade credits, food and energy arrangements, and new property-rights systems at the same time that output was contracting.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil normalized after the Gulf crisis, but the regional effects were uneven. Saudi output rose materially to offset supply disruption and support coalition logistics, while Iraq and Kuwait faced production and export constraints. The lower 1991 oil price helped importers but reduced fiscal space for exporters relative to 1990 crisis prices. GATT reports world spot crude prices down 17% after a 28% rise in 1990, a percentage change that is not the price level of any particular grade.

Non-fuel commodities were generally weak. Slower industrial production in OECD economies and the Eastern European/Soviet contraction reduced demand for metals. LME copper stocks rose in historical tables, consistent with softer industrial conditions. The BIS non-fuel commodity index fell 3.5%, and GATT's food, beverage and agricultural raw material composite about 2%. Agricultural markets were region-specific: China's grain output fell 2.5% to 435.24 million tonnes while cotton rose 25.6% to 5.663 million tonnes, and no consistent global staple price table is shown.

Energy policy was also affected by geopolitics. Iraq was under sanctions. The former Soviet energy system began to fragment across new national borders. Western Europe remained dependent on imported energy, but gas and pipeline politics were not yet the dominant issue they became in later decades.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Unemployment rose in recession-hit advanced economies. IMF reporting indicated industrial-country unemployment rose from about 6.25 percent in 1990 to about 7 percent in 1991. This aggregate hides large cross-country differences and definitional problems. U.S. labor-market slack increased through the recession; the United Kingdom and Canada also saw job losses; Germany's western labor market was supported by fiscal demand while eastern Germany faced massive industrial restructuring.

Demographically, 1991 continued long-run urbanization and workforce expansion in Asia and Latin America. China and India had much lower GDP per capita than OECD countries but much larger labor forces. Migration had important economic consequences in Europe after the opening of Eastern borders, in the former USSR through return migration and displacement, and in the Gulf after the war, but no harmonized global migration table for 1991 is shown.

Wages and productivity diverged sharply by region. In transition economies, administered wage systems and subsidies broke down, and the BIS describes employment adjusting more slowly than output, lowering measured productivity and raising unit labor costs even with wage restraint; a job retained with reduced hours or unpaid wages was not secure employment. In Australia, the Reserve Bank reported unemployment averaging 9.5% in the June 1991 quarter. In Japan, high employment stability masked growing capital misallocation. In the United States, productivity concerns were still prominent, but ICT foundations were being laid.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States was in recession in 1991, with weak household confidence, bank caution, commercial real-estate stress, and inventory adjustment restraining demand. The Gulf War ended early in the year, removing an energy-price shock but not immediately restoring private demand. The policy mix was monetary easing and recession-related fiscal deterioration.

The micro story was balance-sheet repair. Savings and loan losses, commercial-property weakness, and tighter lending standards reduced credit availability. Corporate restructuring continued in manufacturing, airlines, and retail. U.S. equities, however, rallied strongly as markets anticipated lower rates and recovery.

Property stress was concentrated rather than uniform. The BIS records an 18% fall in North-East commercial property prices, fourth quarter to fourth quarter; this is a regional commercial measure, not a national house-price index or a total property return. Over the first three quarters of 1991, residential prices fell 2% in New York and were unchanged in Los Angeles.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Technology hardware and software were early growth areas but not yet dominant in macro data. Automobiles and cyclical manufacturing were weak. Defense procurement faced a post-Cold-War adjustment. Pan Am ceased operations in 1991, showing the stress in legacy airlines. Banking remained cautious after the savings and loan crisis.

**Economic narrative:**

Japan still looked strong in aggregate output data in 1991, but the quality of growth had changed. Equity values were far below their 1989 peak, land prices were turning down, and fixed investment growth slowed. The banking system's reliance on real-estate collateral converted asset deflation into a credit and solvency issue.

The main policy issue was whether the post-bubble slowdown was cyclical or structural. In real time, Japan still had high savings, strong exporters, and world-class manufacturing. In hindsight, 1991 was the opening phase of a balance-sheet recession, with non-performing loans, bank forbearance, and weak domestic demand becoming persistent.

Monetary transmission had also changed. A Bank of Japan research study notes that money-market and bank lending rates had become less mechanically tied to discount-rate changes, and that the contemporaneous policy objective was a soft landing, distinct from later critiques of insufficient easing.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Autos, electronics, precision machinery, and semiconductors remained globally competitive. Construction and real estate were vulnerable. In October 1991 the resolution of Toho Sogo Bank, with assistance to the receiving bank Iyo, was the first application of deposit insurance in Japan. Over the twelve months to January 1992 the National Land Bureau recorded land-price falls of 8.4% in Tokyo, 21.3% in Osaka and 5.1% in Nagoya.

**Economic narrative:**

Argentina's 1991 Convertibility Plan was one of the year's defining stabilization experiments. By legally linking the currency to the U.S. dollar and restricting monetary finance, the plan broke hyperinflation expectations and caused a strong rebound in confidence and activity.

The micro effect was immediate: contracts, prices, and balance sheets became dollar-stabilized. Imports, privatization, and consumption expanded. The forward linkage was also clear in hindsight: the hard peg created competitiveness and debt vulnerabilities that culminated in the 2001 crisis.

The reserve-backing condition in Article 4 of the law does not by itself make every later operation an orthodox currency-board transaction.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Privatization, utilities, banking, consumer durables, agriculture, and energy were central. Households shifted from inflation survival behavior toward credit and imported consumption. Domestic industry faced tougher import competition under stabilization and liberalization.

### 5B. Corporate landscape

A verified top-10-to-top-20 global company table by revenue or market capitalization for 1991: Data not available. Revenue, market capitalization and state-enterprise gross output are different ranking measures. Qualitatively, the world's largest corporate groups included U.S. oil and auto firms, Japanese trading houses and manufacturers, European oil and industrial groups, and large state-linked enterprises. General Motors, Exxon, Ford, Royal Dutch/Shell, IBM, Toyota, and major Japanese trading companies were central to the global revenue landscape, but exact ranks are not asserted here.

Major corporate and financial events included AT&T's USD 7.4 billion acquisition of NCR, Pan Am's collapse, the closure of BCCI by regulators, privatization and restructuring across Central/Eastern Europe, and Mexico's privatization-led corporate reshaping. In Japan, the more important corporate story was not a single bankruptcy but the gradual recognition that banks, property developers, and leveraged firms were exposed to declining asset prices.

Banking cases illustrate the mechanisms. The Bank of England records the BCCI closure in July 1991 and subsequent nervousness among wholesale-funded banks, and describes covenant waivers and coordinated lender negotiations that let firms avoid immediate liquidation, which does not establish that their debts were repaid. In Japan, the October 1991 resolution of Toho Sogo Bank through assistance to the receiving bank Iyo was the first application of deposit insurance; it separated the failed institution, the receiving institution and the deposit insurer, and did not mean that Japanese nonperforming assets were disclosed or resolved that year.

### 5C. Innovation & technology

1991 was disproportionately important for technologies whose macro effects came later. CERN's World Wide Web software was announced to internet newsgroups in August 1991; CERN's formal declaration placing the software in the public domain is dated 30 April 1993. GSM digital mobile service began in Europe in July 1991, when the first official GSM call (as distinct from engineering test calls) was made in Helsinki, creating the platform for mass mobile telephony. Linux appeared as an experimental open-source Unix-like kernel in 1991. These technologies were small in GDP terms at the time but foundational for later ICT productivity, platform business models, and global communications.

R&D spending remained concentrated in the United States, Japan, and Western Europe, with defense and electronics important channels. Semiconductor, computing, telecom, and software sectors were positioned for faster growth as networks became commercialized in the 1990s.

### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Labor markets split into three regimes. First, recession economies saw rising unemployment and weak wage bargaining. Second, East Asian industrializers continued absorbing labor into manufacturing and urban sectors. Third, transition economies experienced a collapse of old employment guarantees, although official unemployment statistics badly understate the disruption because enterprises hoarded labor, wages went unpaid, and informal activity grew. The BIS notes that retained workers and lower productivity could coexist with rapidly rising registered unemployment.

Human-capital trends favored Korea, Japan, Western Europe, and the United States in advanced manufacturing and technology. India's 1991 reforms began to create stronger incentives for private-sector services and software. China's coastal manufacturing zones expanded demand for migrant labor and basic industrial skills.

### 6C. Currencies

The dollar remained the main reserve currency, but IMF reporting showed its share of official foreign-exchange reserves had declined over the prior decade. Major currencies operated under managed floating or regional arrangements, not Bretton Woods. The EMS tied many European currencies to the Deutsche mark, while sterling's 1990 ERM entry constrained UK policy. The yen's level supported Japan's high nominal-dollar GDP ranking. Many developing and transition economies used pegs, crawling arrangements, capital controls, or multiple exchange rates.

GATT notes a 2.5% dollar appreciation against the ECU in 1991, while the U.S. trade-weighted effective rate was virtually unchanged between year endpoints. Argentina's Convertibility Plan was the most important new currency regime among the top 20: Law 23,928, enacted 27 March 1991, set conversion from 1 April at 10,000 australes per U.S. dollar, created a hard link to the dollar and rapidly changed inflation expectations. India's 1991 devaluation was a key crisis adjustment. Soviet and Russian currency arrangements were unstable and not comparable with market exchange-rate systems.

### 6D. Alternative assets

Gold averaged roughly USD 360 per troy ounce and did not behave like a crisis boom despite geopolitical uncertainty, partly because disinflation and lower U.S. rates changed opportunity-cost dynamics. Real estate was the more systemically important alternative asset: Japan's land-price reversal, UK property weakness, Swedish real-estate stress, Australian recession-linked property problems, and U.S. commercial real-estate losses all affected bank balance sheets.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The largest active crisis was the disintegration of the Soviet economic system. It combined output collapse, price instability, fiscal fragmentation, payments breakdown, food and goods shortages, and institutional uncertainty. This was visible in 1991, although the depth of the later transition depression was clearer in hindsight.

The second major crisis cluster was recession plus financial stress in advanced economies. The United States had bank and commercial-property problems after the savings and loan crisis. The United Kingdom and Australia were dealing with property and household-credit adjustment. Sweden was moving toward a full banking crisis. Japan's asset-price decline had begun creating the non-performing-loan problem that later defined the lost decade.

A third risk was European exchange-rate fragility. In 1991 the ERM still looked politically strong, especially with Maastricht moving forward. In hindsight, the incompatibility between German unification-driven tight money and weak conditions elsewhere was already a systemic vulnerability.

A fourth risk was emerging-market capital-flow reversal. In 1991 the return of capital to Mexico and parts of Latin America looked like a success of reform and stabilization. In hindsight, maturity, currency, and current-account vulnerabilities were building.

The BIS itself warned of possible feedback between real-estate declines and recovery while acknowledging uncertainty over how far such spirals would develop; a warning is not proof that a later crisis was inevitable. Several episodes are easily misdated: the Bank of England's report covers the year to 29 February 1992, a Canadian property firm's difficulties described by the BIS emerged in spring 1992, and Japanese deposit-insurance cases after Toho Sogo belong to 1992.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1991 vs. 1990)

The biggest year-over-year change was the move from slowdown to outright global output decline in IMF accounting. In 1990 the world economy was hit by the Gulf crisis and late-cycle tightening; in 1991 the oil shock faded, but recession and transition collapse dominated.

Oil moved from crisis spike to normalization. The IMF average oil price fell from about USD 22.04 per barrel in 1990 to about USD 18.30 in 1991. That reduced headline inflation pressure in importers but also reduced revenue expectations for exporters. GATT's spot crude measure swung from +28% in 1990 to -17% in 1991.

Trade slowed. GATT merchandise volume growth eased from 5% to 3%, while the six dynamic Asian exporters accelerated from 7.5% to 13%; commercial services export value grew 5% against 17% in 1990. In the Maddison Project Database, U.S. growth fell 2.03 points to -0.28% and Canadian growth 2.13 points to -1.88%, while unemployment rose 1.23 points in the United States, 1.75 in the United Kingdom, 2.17 in Canada and 2.68 in Australia (IMF WEO). Brazilian CPI inflation fell by about 2,515 points to 432.79%, which is disinflation at an extreme rate rather than falling prices.

The Soviet bloc moved from severe stress to institutional rupture. Eastern Europe had already started transition, but 1991 brought a much deeper collapse across the former USSR. This was not just a cyclical decline; it was a regime break in ownership, prices, money, trade, and state capacity.

Japan moved from late-bubble resilience to post-bubble vulnerability. In 1990 the equity market had already fallen, but in 1991 the investment and banking implications became clearer. The long-run damage was still underestimated by many observers.

Latin America moved from hyperinflation and debt-crisis legacy toward stabilization and capital inflows in several countries. Argentina's Convertibility Plan was the clearest break. Mexico's reform path strengthened. Brazil remained unstable.

Europe moved closer institutionally to monetary union while becoming more macroeconomically divergent. Maastricht momentum and Single Market preparation advanced, but Germany's unification shock and tight money created tensions that were not resolved inside the ERM.

**Medium-confidence data:** Country narratives for policy stance, recession timing, and structural themes in OECD economies, based on IMF, OECD, BIS, central-bank, and national-source reporting. These are generally robust, but exact indicator values differ across vintage, national-account revision, and definitional conventions. The IMF Annual Report 1992 table (world output -0.3%, industrial 0.8%, developing 3.3%, eastern Europe and former USSR -16.9%, trade volume 3.3%, oil USD 18.30, non-fuel commodities -4.7%, and the unemployment range) is a contemporary estimate that has not been checked against the original publication; the oil and gold levels are approximations. Maddison Project Database 2023 real growth is a long-run reconstruction, not a 1991 national-accounts release, and IMF WEO fiscal series mix perimeters (India reports on an April-March fiscal year; Mexico's perimeter includes public corporations).

**Low-confidence data:** Unemployment rates outside the OECD, government debt and budget balances for several emerging economies, policy rates other than the U.S. and Japanese discount rates, and stock-index tables for every major exchange. Where a sourced 1991 value is not established, this document states "Data not available" rather than filling from memory or unverified tables. Brazilian and Mexican WDI exchange rates are expressed in later redenominated currency units and are not used.

**Soviet/Russian data caveat:** The Russian Federation WDI series is a retroactive country series and should not be read as the GDP of the USSR in 1991. Maddison Project and Penn World Table estimates are useful for historical scale and PPP comparisons, but Soviet and early post-Soviet output figures can have error bands in the 20 to 30 percent range or more because prices, exchange rates, plan indicators, shortages, and statistical institutions were changing simultaneously. The widely quoted -17% Soviet contraction appears in the BIS on a row labelled Soviet Union whose footnote assigns the 1991 observation to the CIS; Yugoslavia's -15% includes Croatia, Slovenia and Bosnia-Hercegovina. Year-end dissolution does not establish comparable full-year coverage for successor states.

**Ranking caveat:** Nominal current-U.S.-dollar rankings reflect exchange rates as much as real output. Japan, Switzerland, Sweden, and Germany appear very large in per-capita or dollar terms partly because of currency values. China and India appear smaller than their real-economy scale because market exchange rates and low price levels suppress nominal dollar GDP. Taiwan is not included in the World Bank WDI country ranking used here, so a ranking of "economies" rather than World Bank countries may differ. A Maddison PPP ranking would place the former USSR fourth and include Indonesia, Turkey, Thailand and Iran.

**Vintage differences:** The GDP, population and per-capita levels in the ranking table come from an earlier WDI vintage. World nominal GDP is USD 23.92 trillion in that vintage, while the current WDI vintage gives USD 23.998 trillion and the IMF WEO USD 24.36 trillion. Population levels are WDI figures and differ from Maddison reconstructions, most notably for India (888.1 million against 857.5 million in Maddison) and China (1,150.8 million against 1,150.0 million).

**Material conflicts:** World growth for 1991 is -0.3% in the IMF Annual Report 1992, -0.4% in the BIS contemporary table, 2.48% (PPP weights) and 2.02% (market weights) in the current IMF WEO and 1.225% in current WDI; the differences reflect weighting and estimates, not a resolved historical contradiction. Country growth differs between the contemporary IMF estimates (United States about -0.7%, Japan about 4.5%) and Maddison (-0.28% and 3.37%). China's growth is 3.66% in Maddison, about 9.38% in current WDI and 7% preliminary GNP in the official 1992 communiqué. German growth is 6.24% in Maddison and 5.01% in the IMF WEO, and WEO German unemployment and current-account series switch from West Germany to united Germany in 1991. Brazilian CPI inflation is 432.79% in WDI and 428% in the BIS. The Macrohistory Database equity returns (United States capital gain 18.18%, United Kingdom 15.05%) differ from the S&P 500 and FTSE 100 index-price returns because they are separate reconstructions. GATT's rounded merchandise export values imply growth of about 1.1%, against its stated 1.5%. Agreement among WDI, IMF, BIS and GATT is not necessarily independent corroboration, since all draw on national sources.

**Dating notes:** The Argentine Convertibility Law of March 1991 fixed convertibility at 10,000 australes per U.S. dollar from 1 April; the one-peso-per-dollar unit belongs to the later currency reform. The CERN public-domain declaration is dated 1993, so the 1991 Web milestone is dissemination, not a legal release.
