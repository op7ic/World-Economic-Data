# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1922

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1922 was a hinge year between the immediate post-World War I dislocation and the more expansionary middle 1920s. The world economy was recovering from the 1920-1921 slump, but recovery was highly uneven. The United States, the United Kingdom, France, Germany, Canada, Italy and Belgium grew strongly in real terms from depressed bases, and the Soviet republics recovered from civil war and famine under the New Economic Policy. Japan grew only slowly, Czechoslovakia contracted, and large agrarian economies such as China and India remained difficult to measure with modern national-account precision. Summed across the 50 economies with annual GDP estimates in the Maddison Project Database 2023, recorded GDP was 3,715.8 billion 2011 international dollars, 7.05% above the same records in 1921; this is a mechanical change in source-defined records that include the Former USSR, not a measured world growth rate. World export volume rose 15.29% while remaining below its 1913 level.

The central macroeconomic divide was deflation versus currency collapse. Many advanced economies experienced falling consumer prices as wartime inflation unwound and central banks moved toward monetary stabilization: U.S. consumer prices fell 6.1% on an annual-average basis, British prices 14.0% and Swedish prices 16.7%. Germany was the most important exception: its real economy was recovering, but the papiermark was collapsing into hyperinflation. The Reichsbank doubled its discount rate from 5% to 10%, yet the December-average dollar value of the paper mark fell 97.41% from a year earlier. Austria was also close to financial collapse before League of Nations stabilization. This split shaped capital flows, savings behavior, trade competitiveness, and political stability.

1922 was also a year of monetary reconstruction. The Genoa Economic and Financial Conference tried to build a postwar monetary order and gave official legitimacy to the gold-exchange standard idea; its resolutions proposed an international convention and a meeting of central banks rather than an implemented system. It did not solve reparations, German fiscal weakness, or Soviet reintegration. The Treaty of Rapallo between Germany and Soviet Russia showed how excluded or isolated economies could build bilateral alternatives when multilateral reconstruction failed, and the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics was constituted only on 30 December.

At the micro level, the mechanisms of recovery were sectoral and uneven. Automobiles, electricity, radio, chemicals, oil, construction, and reconstruction-heavy industries advanced, while coal, shipbuilding, old textiles, and agriculture remained weak in several economies. Ford's acquisition of Lincoln from receivership, the formation of the British Broadcasting Company, and the first clinical use of insulin illustrate how corporate organization, communications, and healthcare innovation were beginning to reshape future productivity, consumption, and human capital. Mexican crude output fell 5.75% despite aggregate growth, and Brazil enacted coffee-defense legislation that tied crop inventories to credit and note issue.

Data quality is a defining issue for 1922. The GDP ranking below uses Maddison Project Database 2023 GDP per capita and population, expressed in constant 2011 international dollars. Direct 1922 observations are used where available; China and Poland are included by bracketed Maddison interpolation because excluding them would create a misleading ranking, but no growth rate is attributed to them. Soviet-bloc and non-market economy figures should be treated as rough historical reconstructions, not official contemporary accounts.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1922

**1. Postwar recovery after the 1920-1921 recession.** The most visible theme was rebound. The constructed MPD-based world GDP estimate, which includes interpolated values for economies without annual observations, rises from about $4,232.9bn to $4,488.8bn in 2011 international dollars, or about 6.0 percent; the summed records of the 50 economies with annual MPD observations were 7.05% higher, a mechanical comparison rather than certified world growth. France, the United Kingdom, Germany, Canada, Italy and Belgium grew rapidly from low bases, and world export volume rose 15.29%. This mattered historically because it made the 1920s look like a recoverable decade rather than a permanently broken postwar depression. The forward linkage was the mid-1920s boom in several economies, but also the buildup of financial fragility.

**2. Deflation in many advanced economies, hyperinflation in Central Europe.** The United States, the United Kingdom, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Spain, Canada, Japan, and Sweden recorded deflation or near-deflation in the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor (JST) Macrohistory series, while Germany recorded hyperinflation and Austria required international stabilization. Polish wholesale prices rose about sixfold between December 1921 and December 1922. The theme is significant because it shows that "postwar inflation" did not move uniformly. Some economies prioritized currency credibility and debt stabilization; others were overwhelmed by fiscal and political constraints. The forward linkage was Germany's 1923 hyperinflation climax and the broader interwar struggle over gold-standard restoration.

**3. The failed attempt to rebuild a cooperative monetary order.** The Genoa conference sought European reconstruction, Soviet reintegration, and principles for a gold-exchange standard. It offered a conceptual bridge between the pre-1914 classical gold standard and later managed international monetary systems, but it failed to settle reparations or restore trust. The affected countries were mainly European, but the implications were global because sterling, the dollar, and gold formed the core of international payments. The forward linkage was the fragile gold-exchange order of the mid-1920s and its collapse in the Great Depression.

**4. Reparations, war debts, and public balance sheets.** France and Italy carried extremely high public debt ratios in the JST data (235.9% and 143.4% of GDP); Britain also carried a very high debt burden, while the United States had a lower but still elevated wartime ratio. In Britain the debt ratio rose 25 points partly because nominal GDP fell 9.15% during deflation. German fiscal weakness and reparations uncertainty destabilized the mark. The historical significance is that postwar debt politics became a macroeconomic regime problem, not just a fiscal accounting problem. The forward linkage was the Ruhr crisis of 1923, Dawes Plan of 1924, and the international debt chain connecting Germany, France, Britain, and the United States.

**5. The Soviet New Economic Policy and partial market restoration.** The Soviet republics recovered in 1922 after civil war, famine, and war communism. The New Economic Policy reintroduced private trade and small-scale markets while the state kept control of heavy industry and strategic sectors. This was significant because it proved that the early Soviet economy could recover through a mixed regime, not only through centralized command. The Union Treaty of 30 December assigned currency, external trade, transport and budget powers to Union institutions. The forward linkage was the NEP recovery of the 1920s and the later turn toward forced collectivization and Five-Year Plans.

**6. Commodity normalization after wartime boom.** The U.S. domestic crude oil first-purchase price averaged $1.61 per barrel in 1922, down from $1.73 in 1921. Gold remained fixed at $20.67 per troy ounce under the U.S. gold standard framework. London quotations show coal down 15.09%, copper down 8.65% and wheat down 34.25%, while cotton and coffee rose. Agricultural exporters such as Canada, Argentina, Australia, India, Brazil, and the Dutch East Indies were recovering but remained exposed to price swings. The forward linkage was persistent farm distress in several economies despite industrial recovery.

**7. Industrial modernization beneath macro instability.** Even where macro data were weak, microeconomic modernization continued. Automobiles, electrification, petroleum distribution, radio broadcasting, chemicals, and medical innovation were expanding. These sectors mattered because they changed household consumption, urban infrastructure, advertising, transport costs, and human capital. The forward linkage was the consumer-durable and communications economy of the later 1920s.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The Genoa conference was the year's most important economic-diplomatic meeting. It attempted to solve problems of reconstruction, reparations, Soviet trade relations, currency stabilization, capital flight, and central-bank cooperation. Its financial resolutions recommended a gold-exchange convention and further central-bank consultation, conditional on stabilization. Its failure to settle reparations or integrate Germany and Soviet Russia into a stable framework was one of the clearest limits of postwar multilateralism.

The Treaty of Rapallo, signed by Germany and Soviet Russia on 16 April 1922, renounced mutual claims and restored diplomatic and economic relations; diplomatic relations resumed immediately, while Articles 1(b) and 4 took effect only on ratification. Economically, it showed that excluded states could bypass the Allied-centered order. Politically, it alarmed France and Britain and deepened the sense that reparations and reconstruction were unresolved.

Tariff and trade barriers remained important but fragmented. The U.S. Tariff Act was enacted on 21 September 1922 (chapter 356, 42 Stat. 858); its weighted effective tariff increase is Data not available. The European economy had new borders, new currencies, and new state institutions after the collapse of empires. In South China, the Lappa customs district recorded trade of 26.3 million Haikwan taels against 30.9 million in 1921 (-14.71%), with movements affected by tariff diversion, strikes and piracy. Colonial trade channels remained crucial for Britain, France, the Netherlands, and Japan. Regional integration bodies such as the later EEC did not yet exist, and ASEAN did not exist. The Washington naval treaty, signed on 6 February 1922, limited capital ships; ratifications were deposited only on 17 August 1923.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil was becoming more central to transport, military logistics, shipping, and industry. The U.S. domestic crude first-purchase price of $1.61 per barrel, down 6.94% from $1.73, reflected retreat from the 1920 spike as production expanded. Mexico's crude output fell from 193.4 million to 182.3 million barrels (-5.75%). The United States, Mexico, the Dutch East Indies, and the Baku region were important in the wider oil story, though production data differ by source and jurisdiction.

Gold was not a freely floating modern asset. The U.S. official gold price remained $20.67 per troy ounce, making gold a monetary anchor rather than an ordinary alternative asset. Copper, lead, zinc, and other base metals were recovering from the postwar collapse, but evidence points to weak early-1922 conditions rather than a synchronized global boom.

Agricultural conditions were central to welfare. Wheat, cotton, coffee, sugar, rubber, tea, rice, jute, wool, and beef linked farmers and colonial exporters to global demand. Many farm economies had not recovered from the price collapses after World War I, even where national GDP was rising. Indian retail rice prices fell 8.17% and wheat prices 12.50%, while Brazil's Decree 4548 of 19 June authorized credit secured by stored coffee.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor markets were not comparable across countries. The United Kingdom's unemployment rate was high (9.8% in the JST series), the United States still had elevated unemployment (7.6% in the Lebergott reconstruction, after 11.9% in 1921), and several small European economies had severe labor slack. Germany's measured unemployment looked low in some series, but hyperinflation makes the statistic misleading because workers could be employed while real wages and savings collapsed.

Demographically, the world remained young and agrarian outside the advanced industrial economies. World population reached about 1,867.5 million in the Federico-Tena reconstruction, up 0.89%. China and India dominated population scale. Urbanization continued in the United States, Western Europe, Japan, and Latin American cities such as Buenos Aires and São Paulo. Migration was economically important, but U.S. immigration restriction after the 1921 Emergency Quota Act changed transatlantic labor flows.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States was the largest economy by a wide margin and was moving out of the sharp 1920-1921 downturn. Output growth resumed, CPI prices were still falling, and the Federal Reserve eased money-market conditions. The New York Federal Reserve discount rate moved down during the year, while fiscal balances improved as wartime spending receded. The United States also remained the central creditor economy for postwar finance, even though it did not join the Genoa monetary program. The Tariff Act was enacted on 21 September 1922; its effect on average tariff rates is Data not available.

Easier money reached bank portfolios before credit expanded broadly. Reporting member banks' loans rose 5.49% between 26 July and 27 December, and their time deposits from $3,515 million to $3,708 million over the same dates. Federal Reserve discounts fell while the System's holdings of government securities rose, so the liquidation of discounts did not amount to monetary tightening. Over the calendar year, total bank loans in the Macrohistory Database still fell 3.77%.

The main domestic tension was the split between industrial recovery and rural weakness. Manufacturing, automobiles, electric machinery, construction, and consumer durables were improving, while farm prices and farm debt remained difficult after the collapse of the wartime agricultural boom. The economy was becoming more consumption-led and credit-intensive, a pattern that would define much of the later 1920s.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Automobiles, electrical goods, petroleum, chemicals, and residential construction were the clearest growth drivers. Ford acquired Lincoln on 4 February 1922 for $8 million from receivership, and mass-production carmaking continued to reshape steel, rubber, glass, petroleum distribution, roads, retail credit, and household mobility. Railroads were recovering but faced competition from motor transport. Banking conditions were healthier than in 1920-1921, but agricultural banks remained exposed to weak land and crop prices. Real consumption per person in the Barro-Ursúa reconstruction rose 8.40%, and the nominal equity total return in the rate-of-return reconstruction was 27.09%. The Macrohistory nominal house-price index rose 2.78%; newspaper asking-price indices rose 3.80% in New York City and 14.01% in Boston, while asking rents fell 12.31% in New York and rose 2.35% in Boston, so no single national property trend can be stated. ### 5C. Innovation & technology

The most important technologies with economic implications were automobiles, electrification, radio, chemicals, petroleum engineering, aviation, and medical science. ### 6C. Currencies

The U.S. dollar was the key gold-linked creditor currency. Sterling remained a reserve and trade currency but was not yet restored to prewar gold parity. Policymakers in London treated credibility and eventual restoration as a central objective, even while easing Bank Rate in 1922. Between December 1921 and December 1922, the New York monthly mean dollar values of sterling (+10.92%), the lira (+13.47%), the guilder (+9.71%), the krona (+9.87%), the yen (+1.94%), the rupee (+11.66%) and the Argentine gold peso (+14.39%) rose, while the French franc (-7.84%), the Brazilian paper milreis (-5.71%) and the German paper mark (-97.41%) fell.

Germany's papiermark was the most important currency crisis. It depreciated rapidly and set the stage for the 1923 hyperinflation climax. Austria also faced severe currency instability before the League stabilization program; its crown averaged 0.0014 U.S. cents in December. France and Italy had weaker fiscal and currency positions than the United States or United Kingdom, although they did not experience German-style hyperinflation in 1922.

### 6D. Alternative assets

Gold was a monetary anchor, not a free-floating hedge in the modern sense. The official U.S. price remained $20.67 per troy ounce. In countries with unstable currencies, foreign exchange, gold, real estate, inventories, and equities could function as stores of value, but legal access and market prices varied.

Real estate data are limited. JST housing total returns for 1922 show positive returns in the United States, United Kingdom, France, Belgium, Switzerland, and Finland, and negative or weak returns in the Netherlands, Denmark, and Sweden. Nominal house-price indices in the Macrohistory Database rose 2.78% in the United States, 4.62% in the United Kingdom, 10.36% in France, 34.00% in Belgium, 2.31% in Australia and 63.81% in Germany, and fell 13.01% in the Netherlands and 7.79% in Sweden; the rate-of-return housing capital gain was 60.87% for Germany and -4.61% for Spain, where no house-price index is available. In the U.S. city indices built from newspaper advertisements, asking prices rose 3.80% in New York City and 14.01% in Boston, while asking rents fell 12.31% in New York and rose 2.35% in Boston; these are hedonic asking-price estimates sampled mostly in late May, not transaction prices. These estimates are useful but not fully comparable across national housing markets.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The largest active crisis was German hyperinflation. In 1922 it was already a systemic currency failure, not merely high inflation. The real economy still expanded, but savings, contracts, tax collection, wage bargaining, and political legitimacy were being destroyed. With hindsight, 1922 was the penultimate year before the full 1923 collapse and Rentenmark stabilization.

Austria was another active crisis. The Austrian state was fiscally and monetarily fragile after the Habsburg collapse. The League of Nations stabilization framework, agreed in Geneva on 4 October 1922, was one of the first major international financial rescue programs of the interwar era; the statutes of the new central bank followed on 14 November, but it began operating only on 1 January 1923, and the schilling came in 1925. The program helped prevent a deeper immediate collapse but came with fiscal discipline and external oversight.

The Soviet economy was recovering but fragile. NEP created a practical recovery mechanism, but famine aftereffects, inflation, weak industry, and urban-rural price imbalances remained serious. With hindsight, the later "scissors crisis" between industrial and agricultural prices showed that recovery itself could create new distributional tensions.

Agricultural debt was a hidden vulnerability in several economies. The United States, Canada, Australia, Argentina, Brazil, and India all had regions where farmers were exposed to postwar price declines and debt burdens. This risk did not produce a single 1922 global crash, but it weakened rural banks and political coalitions. Deflation raised the real burden of fixed nominal debt, and Brazil's coffee scheme tied crop prices to credit and possible note issue.

Japan's financial system remained fragile after the 1920 panic. The Bank of Japan's year-end expansion of banknotes and lending to the private sector, which reversed in January 1923, points to a short liquidity shock in late 1922. With hindsight, the 1923 Great Kanto earthquake would soon become a major shock to banking, insurance, construction, and fiscal policy. That was not knowable in 1922, but the fragility was already visible.

A broader tail risk was failure of monetary reconstruction. Genoa did not solve reparations, debt, or Soviet reintegration. With hindsight, the interwar gold-exchange system would remain structurally fragile, dependent on confidence, fiscal discipline, and cooperation that were often absent.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1922 vs. 1921)

1922 was materially better than 1921 in aggregate output. The constructed MPD-based world estimate rose about 6.0 percent, and the summed records of the 50 economies with annual MPD observations were 7.05% higher, a mechanical comparison that includes the Former USSR record. The largest top-20 rebounds were France at about +17.9 percent, Canada at about +14.6 percent, Belgium at about +9.8 percent, the United Kingdom at about +9.6 percent, Germany at about +8.8 percent, and Italy at about +8.4 percent. The Soviet economy also recovered, but no comparable-territory growth rate is available. The main negative case in the top 20 was Czechoslovakia, down about -2.6 percent. World export volume rose 15.29%, after a fall in 1921.

The monetary delta was more complex. Deflation continued in many advanced economies, but interest rates were lower than in 1921 in key money centers: end-December official rates fell in London (5% to 3%), New York (4.5% to 4%) and most of Western Europe. This helped recovery but also reinforced the focus on restoring currency credibility. In Germany, the change from 1921 to 1922 was a worsening currency crisis despite real output growth: CPI inflation rose from 31.08% to 1,023.71% and the Reichsbank rate doubled. Czechoslovakia moved the other way, raising its discount rate to 7% as output fell. Austria moved from near-collapse toward League-supervised stabilization.

The geopolitical delta was significant. In 1921, the postwar order still looked unsettled but potentially negotiable. In 1922, Genoa exposed the limits of cooperation, while Rapallo showed that Germany and Soviet Russia could create a separate channel. The Soviet republics ended the year as a constituted Union. These shifts altered expectations for 1923, when the Ruhr crisis would move from risk to reality.

The sectoral delta favored industry and construction over agriculture. Reconstruction, autos, electricity, and urban activity improved. Farm sectors remained strained by lower prices, debt, and uneven demand; the London wheat quotation fell by about a third. Mexican oil output fell 5.75%, while Brazil's Treasury and rediscount note circulation rose 7.69%. Consumer durables and radio began to look more important as future growth engines.

The power-balance delta favored the United States. Its economy was the largest, its currency was credible, its financial markets were recovering, and its creditor position was strong. Britain remained central to trade and finance, but sterling's role was more contested, even as its dollar value rose. Germany remained industrially powerful but financially unstable. China and India were huge by population and total GDP, but low income per capita and weak centralized statistical systems limited their policy influence.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

The most reliable statistics are for the United States, the United Kingdom, France, Canada, Australia, Sweden, the Netherlands, Belgium, Italy, Japan, Spain, and other advanced economies covered by the JST Macrohistory Database. Even there, unemployment, housing, and financial indicators are not always directly comparable. The best-documented figures are dated official discount rates, New York monthly exchange quotations, official Indian banking, fiscal, price and industrial tables, Mexican oil and price tables, and statutory and treaty texts; reconstructed GDP, price, fiscal, consumption and asset-return series are moderate in quality.

GDP rankings are estimates, not contemporary official national accounts. The main ranking uses MPD 2023 GDP per capita and population. GDP is expressed in constant 2011 international dollars, not nominal 1922 U.S. dollars. China and Poland are included by bracketed interpolation between Maddison benchmarks; this is more historically plausible than excluding them, but it lowers confidence, and neither economy has an annual 1922 observation in MPD 2023 or in the Maddison 2010 vintage, so no growth rate is attributed to them. The 1921 report ranks only economies with annual MPD observations and therefore lists Austria and Sweden in place of China and Poland; the difference between the two years' top 20 reflects method, not economic entry or exit. MPD direct observations are stronger for the United States, India, Germany, United Kingdom, France, Japan, Italy, Former USSR, Indonesia, Spain, Canada, Argentina, Belgium, Netherlands, Australia, Czechoslovakia, Mexico, and Brazil.

The MPD records are source-defined territories. India is undivided India dated to 1 October, Germany uses 1936 frontiers for 1919-1945, Czechoslovakia and other Eastern European records use later frontiers, and Indonesia covers modern Indonesian territory. The Former USSR figure is especially uncertain. It is a reconstructed aggregate for a non-market, post-civil-war economy, and the national-income reconstruction behind it has not been matched to the territory of its population series, so the implied +15.90% change is not presented as growth. A plausible uncertainty band of 20-30 percent should be assumed. For the United Kingdom, the 1922 rate compares two records on the same territory, but the 1921 rate does not, so the acceleration between the two years cannot be stated. The same caution applies, though in different ways, to colonial economies such as India and the Dutch East Indies and to fragmented China. The Maddison 2010 vintage, which shares the MPD lineage and is not an independent check, gives Japan -0.27% growth against +1.26% in MPD 2023, and the United States 5.53% against 4.90%.

World GDP is a constructed estimate from available country data and interpolation. It is not an official world national account. The 50-economy sum avoids interpolation but omits China, Poland and other economies without annual observations. Global capital-flow totals, global unemployment, global wage growth, global R&D spending, global corporate rankings, and most global sector output values are Data not available in a single reliable 1922 series. World trade volume and world population are published reconstructions that incorporate the authors' own interpolation.

Current-account, budget, public-debt, interest-rate, and exchange-rate data are strongest for JST countries. They are weak or unavailable for China, India, Indonesia, Poland, Argentina, Czechoslovakia, Mexico, Brazil, and the Former USSR in this report. Exchange rates are shown as New York December monthly means in U.S. cents per unit, which are contemporary quotations, except for Mexico, where an annual-average reconstruction in pesos per USD is used; the 1921 report showed JST annual levels for the United Kingdom, Italy, the Netherlands, Japan, Spain, Canada and Belgium, so the exchange-rate basis differs between the two years. JST annual levels are given here only as context, because some series use restated currency units (the French level is in new francs and the Australian level in retrospectively converted Australian dollars) and because hyperinflation destroys annual-average interpretability. The Belgian current-account field in the JST database is a merchandise trade balance and is not shown as a current account. JST fiscal balances are fiscal-year central-government revenue minus expenditure over calendar-year GDP; the U.S. ratio uses the federal year ending 30 June and the Japanese ratio covers the General Account only.

Several indicators rest on conflicting sources. The New York discount-rate cut to 4% is dated 22 June in the Federal Reserve annual report. The December 1921 yen comparator is the Federal Reserve New York mean of 47.9219 U.S. cents, while the Bank of Japan demand-draft series gives 48.125 for the same month; the instruments differ. The Mexican peso rates for 1921 (2.04 per USD) and 1922 (2.05) both come from the Gómez-Galvarriato-Musacchio annual reconstruction. U.S. unemployment is 7.6% in the Lebergott reconstruction but 6.7% in the JST series; the Lebergott figure is shown. For the United Kingdom, the JST series and the Bank of England millennium dataset differ on unemployment (9.8% against 9.68%), debt (183.48% against 178.60% of GDP), the current account (4.41% against 4.36%) and the budget balance (+1.03% for the fiscal-year central government against +0.05% for calendar-year public-sector net lending); the JST values are shown for consistency with the other JST countries. U.S. CPI inflation is -6.1% in the BLS table and -6.15% in JST, a rounding difference.

The weakest evidence in this document concerns corporate rankings, country-level micro indicators outside advanced economies, and real-estate conditions. Data not available means that no observation with the stated definition and territory was found for 1922, not that no historical record exists. National CPI and unemployment remain unavailable for China, India, Indonesia, Poland, Argentina, Czechoslovakia, Mexico and Brazil; wholesale and city price indices are shown in notes but not substituted. Exchange-level index data for Toronto, Bombay, Shanghai, Rio de Janeiro and São Paulo are Data not available. The strongest evidence concerns MPD-derived GDP/population estimates, JST macro-financial indicators for advanced economies, official or quasi-official interest-rate histories, and well-documented historical events such as Genoa, Rapallo, Austrian stabilization, and the first clinical use of insulin.
