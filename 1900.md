# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1900

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The world economy in 1900 was defined by the late classical gold standard, the high point of the first era of modern globalization, and an increasingly visible shift in industrial leadership from Britain toward the United States and Germany. International capital moved with relatively few formal controls among the major financial centers, sterling was the central trade and reserve currency, and gold parities anchored exchange rates for core economies. The United States was already the largest measured economy in the Maddison territorial series, with Germany rapidly closing the industrial gap with Britain.

The year was not economically calm. China was shaken by the Boxer Rebellion and foreign intervention, British India faced the severe 1899 to 1900 famine, Britain was financing and fighting the Boer War, and the United States experienced a mild recession that ran through much of 1900. These shocks did not overturn the global system, but they revealed its hierarchy: colonial and semi-colonial economies absorbed severe welfare losses, while the financial centers managed instability through gold-standard discipline, bank-rate adjustments, and imperial fiscal capacity.

The microeconomic mechanisms of the year were industrial concentration, railways, export agriculture, mineral extraction, and early mass consumer markets. Steel, coal, railroads, chemicals, textiles, shipping, and finance were the decisive sectors. Oil was still much smaller than coal in the global energy system, but Baku, the United States, the Dutch East Indies, and Romania showed that petroleum would soon matter more.

Data confidence is uneven. Britain, the United States, Germany, France, and several Western European economies have relatively strong historical series. China, India, colonial territories, partitioned Poland, Czechoslovak lands, Romania, and the Russian Empire core require stronger caveats. Unemployment, current accounts, government debt to GDP, budget balances and consumer-price inflation exist only as retrospective reconstructions with differing definitions, mostly for Western Europe, the settler economies, the United States and Japan. Where no series matches an economy's definition and territory, the indicator is shown as Data not available rather than estimated.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1900

**1. The gold standard as operating system.** The monetary order of 1900 rested on gold convertibility and fixed par exchange rates among the core economies. The regime lowered exchange-rate uncertainty and encouraged long-distance capital flows, but it also constrained discretionary monetary responses. Central banks and treasury systems defended gold reserves through discount-rate policy, fiscal credibility, and sometimes painful domestic adjustment. The forward link was direct: this system survived until the First World War, then fractured under wartime finance.

**2. Industrial leadership shifted toward the United States and Germany.** Britain remained financially dominant, but the United States had the largest aggregate GDP estimate and Germany had become Europe's most dynamic industrial challenger. Steel, electrical equipment, chemicals, heavy engineering, and railway-related production were the key sectors. This mattered beyond economics because industrial capacity fed naval procurement, army logistics, and imperial competition.

**3. Colonial economies were integrated but vulnerable.** India, the Dutch East Indies, and other colonial territories were connected to world markets through railways, plantations, ports, and imperial finance. Integration did not guarantee welfare resilience. The Indian famine showed that market connections and railways could move grain without ensuring purchasing power for vulnerable households.

**4. Political violence and fiscal extraction shaped Asia.** China's Boxer crisis disrupted trade and administration in North China and led to the 1901 indemnity regime. Japan, by contrast, showed the gains of Meiji state-building and export-oriented industrialization. The contrast between Qing fiscal weakness and Meiji institutional strength was one of the defining Asian economic patterns of the year.

**5. Resource frontiers expanded.** Wheat in Canada and Russia, wool in Australia, oil in Baku and the Dutch East Indies, tin and plantation crops in Southeast Asia, and minerals in Mexico and Belgium-linked Congo networks tied commodity frontiers to global finance. These sectors attracted foreign capital but often concentrated risk and benefits.

**6. Corporate organization was changing.** Large trusts, cartels, universal banks, zaibatsu, and colonial concession companies were becoming central to microeconomic structure. Modern global rankings by revenue or market capitalization do not exist for 1900, but the period clearly saw larger multi-plant firms, financial syndicates, and cross-border investment networks.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

Maddison's 1900 world GDP estimate in the 2010 workbook is **1,971,881 million 1990 international dollars**, with world population estimated at **1,563.5 million** and world GDP per capita near **1,261 1990 international dollars**. The estimate is a reconstruction, not a contemporary national-accounts publication. The Federico-Tena world population reconstruction (1991 borders) gives a higher 1,601.7 million for 1900, up 0.84% from 1,588.4 million in 1899.

Global trade was in the first globalization era. The Federico-Tena world trade database puts world exports at USD 9,860.5 million in current prices in 1900, with a real export index of 61.08 (1913=100). Reconstructions of trade shares from 1870 to 1949 that combine Maddison population and GDP data with trade records show that openness before 1914 was high by historical standards. Capital flows generally ran from Britain, France, Germany, the Netherlands, and Belgium toward settler economies, Latin America, Russia, colonial infrastructure, and sovereign debt markets.

Reserve-currency dynamics were sterling-centered. The U.S. dollar gained credibility through the Gold Standard Act of 1900, but London remained the central bill market. Global inflation trends were low in the long run under gold, though short-run price volatility remained substantial. No harmonized world consumer-price index exists for 1900; national reconstructions ranged from about 0.5% in Italy and in the territory of modern Austria to more than 12% in Japan and Belgium, and consumer, wholesale and commodity prices must be kept separate. Gold was fixed by monetary parities. U.S. petroleum output rose 11.48% to 63,620,529 barrels, world copper production rose 2.66% to 484,799 long tons, and the London standard copper price was almost unchanged at GBP 73.625 per long ton (1899: 73.6875). Agricultural staples require local series, and no single world price index is available.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The United States had no central bank. The dollar was legally anchored to gold, but liquidity still depended on national banks, trust companies, the Treasury, correspondent banking, and New York money markets. The Gold Standard Act of 14 March defined the dollar as 25.8 grains of gold nine-tenths fine, required gold redemption of United States notes and created a USD 150 million redemption reserve, while the Treasury exchanged specified 3%, 4% and 5% bonds into 2% gold bonds dated 1 April. Britain used Bank Rate to manage gold flows and market liquidity: the Bank of England cut it from 6% to 4% in three steps in January and to 3% on 14 June, then raised it to 4% on 19 July, for an annual mean of 3.96%. The Reichsbank, whose official discount-rate series begins in 1876, cut its rate from 7% to 5.5% in January and to 5% on 13 July, the Banque de France cut from 4.5% to 3% by 25 May, and the Nederlandsche Bank and the National Bank of Belgium cut to 4.5% in January. The Bank of Japan moved the other way, raising its Head Office bill rate from 6.94% to 8.76% in three steps between March and July, and the Banca d'Italia held its official rate at 5% all year.

Exchange-rate context was gold-standard fixed parity for core gold currencies. The U.S. gold price was $20.67 per ounce, and the sterling-dollar par exchange rate was about $4.867 per pound; the annual mean market rate was USD 4.86525 per pound. Silver and gold-exchange economies, including China, India, Mexico, and some colonial territories, had more complicated exchange systems. Credit conditions were generally credible for core sovereign borrowers but far less stable for peripheral borrowers, colonial producers, and economies dependent on commodity prices.

Stock-market data are incomplete. The Dow Jones Industrial Average rose 7.01% between year-end closes, from 66.08 to 70.71, although the NBER chronology dates a U.S. contraction from the June 1899 peak to a December 1900 trough. London, Paris, Berlin, Amsterdam, Brussels, New York, and Tokyo all had important securities markets, but comparable index levels and annual returns for each exchange are Data not available.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

Trade policy combined globalization with protection. Britain was still the great free-trade power, while the United States and Germany used tariffs to protect domestic producers. Colonial preference, imperial procurement, concessions, and treaty-port arrangements were crucial in Asia and Africa. The Boer War affected British fiscal and military spending, while the Boxer crisis shaped China's customs revenue, foreign claims, and treaty obligations. The Powers' joint note of 22 December demanded compensation and financial guarantees from China; the indemnity of 450 million Haikwan taels was fixed only by the protocol of September 1901. Canada raised its British preference from one-quarter to one-third of the ordinary duty from 1 July, and a U.S. proclamation of 13 July reduced duties on specified German goods under the Dingley Act.

Regional integration as known later did not exist. There was no EEC, ASEAN, WTO, IMF, or World Bank. Integration came through empire, the gold standard, shipping, telegraph cables, railways, private finance, and bilateral treaty systems. This made the economy more connected than before, but not institutionally protected against war, famine, or financial panic.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Coal was still the dominant industrial fuel. Oil was emerging from a smaller base, led by the United States and the Russian Empire's Baku region, with additional activity in Romania, the Dutch East Indies, and other early fields. OPEC did not exist. Petroleum was important for kerosene, lubricants, shipping and industrial uses, but it had not yet displaced coal in electricity, rail, steel, or heavy industry. U.S. crude output rose from 57,070,850 to 63,620,529 barrels, Russian Empire crude output from 8.96 to 10.38 million tonnes in Suhara's reconstruction, Sumatran refined output from 24,187,530 to 38,324,960 U.S. gallons, and shipments from Ploiești from 19,830 to 22,650 tank carloads. British coal output rose from 220,094,781 to 225,181,300 long tons.

Metals mattered for industrial rivalry. Steel capacity shaped railways, construction, shipbuilding, and armaments. Copper was increasingly important for electrification and telecommunications; world mine output rose from 472,244 to 484,799 long tons, with the United States at 268,787 long tons. Pig iron output rose in the United States, Germany and France but fell in Great Britain, from 9,421,435 to 8,959,691 long tons. Agricultural commodities were central to household welfare and political stability. The Indian famine showed how crop failure and purchasing-power collapse could overwhelm market integration.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

World population was about 1.56 billion in the Maddison 2010 estimate and 1.60 billion in the Federico-Tena reconstruction. Labor markets were not measured by modern unemployment definitions. Migration was economically significant: Europeans moved to the United States, Canada, Argentina, Australia, and other settler economies (recorded Italian emigrant departures rose from 308,339 to 352,782, and Canada recorded 44,697 declared settlers); Japanese and Chinese migration fed Pacific labor markets; Indian indenture and internal migration connected plantations, ports, and railways. Urbanization accelerated in industrial economies, while agrarian labor still dominated China, India, Russia, the Balkans, and much of Latin America.

Wages and productivity diverged sharply. Industrial workers in Britain, the United States, Germany, and Belgium had higher productivity and more monetized labor markets than agricultural workers in India, China, Romania, Mexico, and the Dutch East Indies. Labor unions, socialist parties, and employer cartels were becoming more important in Europe, while colonial labor regimes often limited bargaining power.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States was the largest measured territorial economy in the Maddison 1900 series. The dominant story was scale: a continental rail network, large internal markets, high tariffs, abundant coal and iron, and expanding urban demand made the United States the clearest industrial growth leader. The Gold Standard Act of 1900 made the gold basis of the dollar explicit. That did not create a central bank; money markets still depended heavily on New York banks, Treasury operations, and seasonal crop-finance needs.

At the micro level, railroads, steel, oil refining, meatpacking, electrical equipment, and farm machinery were the strategic sectors. The Great Merger Movement was still reshaping corporate organization, although the most famous U.S. Steel transaction came in 1901, just outside this year. Household consumption was increasingly connected to national brands, department stores, and mail-order retail, but income distribution, labor conflict, and regional farm vulnerability remained major constraints.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Pig iron output edged up from 13,620,703 to 13,789,242 long tons, while crude steel including castings fell from 10,639,857 to 10,188,329 long tons, consistent with the 1899-1900 contraction; petroleum output rose 11.48% to 63,620,529 barrels and mine copper from 262,206 to 268,787 long tons. Bradstreet recorded 9,913 commercial failures with liabilities of USD 127.18 million, against 9,634 and USD 119.73 million in 1899. Under the Gold Standard Act's new entry rules, 348 national-bank organizations were completed between 14 March and 31 October (62 conversions, 123 reorganizations and 163 primary organizations), which is not the number of net new banks. General Electric established its Schenectady research laboratory under Willis R. Whitney, and American Bridge consolidated bridge and structural firms ahead of U.S. Steel's formation in 1901. Nonfarm residential mortgage debt was about USD 2.9 billion at the end of 1900, and Goldsmith's estimates put personal saving at USD 1.27 billion including consumer durables, against USD 2.19 billion in 1899. Standard Oil, Carnegie Steel and the major railroads dominated the corporate landscape; urban mass consumption grew, but wages were uneven and there was no federal safety net. Some rail securities and cyclical manufacturing were under stress during the recession.

**Economic narrative:**

China in 1900 was still enormous in aggregate output because of population scale, but its Maddison GDP per capita estimate was very low. The year was dominated by the Boxer Rebellion, foreign military intervention in North China, and the collapse of confidence in Qing state capacity. The national economy remained primarily agrarian, with handicrafts, regional markets, treaty-port commerce, salt and customs revenue, and silver-based monetary conditions shaping economic life.

The main microeconomic effect was uncertainty. Northern trade routes, urban provisioning, missionary and treaty-port networks, and merchant finance were disrupted by violence and occupation. The Boxer Protocol was signed in 1901, but the fiscal burden became visible from the 1900 crisis itself: China would soon face an indemnity regime that shifted customs and fiscal capacity toward debt service and foreign claims.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** A translated decree of the Empress Dowager dated 25 June granted 100,000 taels each to three named corps, including the Boxers, and 60,000 taels each to two other forces. In the Yangtze region the Nanking and Hankow-Wuchang viceroys exchanged assurances with the Shanghai consular body, protecting foreign lives, property and treaty rights in return for assurances against intervention, which kept central treaty-port commerce functioning while North China logistics were disrupted. Later banking chronologies record a June liquidity compact among Shanghai officials, Chinese bankers and foreign banks during capital flight, although the underlying agreement has not been independently documented. In Shanghai's International Settlement four district committees assessed land in 1900, and the Central District commanded higher prices than peripheral industrial districts. Silver-based merchant credit, native banks and rural subsistence under tax burdens dominated household economic life.

**Economic narrative:**

The United Kingdom remained one of the world economy's central nodes despite losing relative industrial leadership to the United States and Germany. London was the leading international capital market, sterling was the key reserve and trade currency, and Britain operated within the classical gold standard. Bank Rate started the year at 6%, was cut to 3% by 14 June, and was raised to 4% on 19 July, ending the year at 4%; policy was oriented toward gold flows and sterling credibility.

The Boer War increased fiscal pressure and exposed military and administrative limits, but Britain still benefited from shipping, marine insurance, overseas investment income, coal exports, and imperial trade networks. Micro performance was mixed: finance, shipping, and services remained globally strong, while some older manufacturing branches, especially textiles, faced sharper competition from newer industrial economies.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Great Britain's pig iron output fell from 9,421,435 to 8,959,691 long tons, while coal rose from 220,094,781 to 225,181,300 long tons and steel excluding castings from 4,955,325 to 5,001,054 long tons. The Finance Act 1900 set income tax at one shilling in the pound, raised the tea duty to sixpence per pound, added duties on tobacco, beer and spirits, and suspended the New Sinking Fund; on 30 July the Chancellor reported a GBP 30 million War Loan issued at 98.5, raising GBP 29.55 million in cash, alongside GBP 8 million of Treasury bills. Post Office savings depositors rose from 8,046,680 to 8,439,983. Total mortgage lending reached GBP 131 million at the end of 1900 against GBP 128 million, while building-society mortgages alone fell from GBP 47.7 million to GBP 45.6 million, and the London house-price index eased 1.26%. Marconi filed British patent 7777 for tuned wireless telegraphy on 26 April, and Associated Portland Cement Manufacturers was incorporated on 10 July. The London bill market and Bank of England discount policy set global credit signals. Real incomes had risen since 1870, but poverty and urban crowding remained large.

**Economic narrative:**

British India was the fourth-ranked Maddison territorial economy, but its GDP per capita was among the lowest in the top 20. The 1899 to 1900 famine was the central economic shock. Drought, crop failure, plague and epidemic disease, weak purchasing power among landless laborers, and colonial relief rules produced very high social costs. Railways helped move grain but did not remove entitlement failure for poor households.

The economy was dominated by agriculture, land revenue systems, export crops, jute, cotton, tea, opium, and railway-linked commerce. Micro conditions varied sharply by region: Bombay Presidency, Central Provinces, Rajputana, and other drought-hit areas faced acute distress, while port cities, railway workshops, plantations, and jute mills remained tied into imperial markets.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** On 23 March the Secretary of State for India reported about 61 million people in the drought-affected area, about 5 million receiving relief and 4.2 million on public relief works, with GBP 2.055 million spent on direct famine relief in 1899-1900 and GBP 3.335 million estimated for 1900-1901; applications were invited for a GBP 3 million loan under existing borrowing powers, and the Government of India estimated that a further GBP 1.2 million would be needed for relief and land-revenue remission. Calendar-year coal output rose from 5,093,260 to 6,118,692 tons and petroleum from 32,934,007 to 37,729,211 gallons. Private deposits at the Presidency Banks rose from Rs 114,107,601 to Rs 128,826,655, and postal savings depositors numbered 785,729 in 1899-1900 against 755,871, with balances of Rs 96.46 million. In 1899-1900 the cotton mills numbered 187 with 163,296 workers and produced 513,923 thousand pounds of yarn. The government purchased the Great Indian Peninsula Railway but leased it back to private management, and Act VIII of 1900 allowed note issue against gold held by the Secretary of State. Livestock and rural credit suffered in the drought districts, and famine and plague made consumption collapse local and class-specific.

The Trans-Siberian Railway was not fully complete, but its construction was central to Russian imperial strategy and Eurasian market integration.

### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Sectoral employment shifted toward manufacturing and services in the United States, Britain, Germany, Belgium, France, Japan, and settler economies, while agriculture still dominated China, India, Russia, Romania, Mexico, and the Dutch East Indies. Modern unemployment rates are not comparable. Historical reconstructions give 5.0% for the United States, 3.68% for the United Kingdom on a labour-force basis, 4.39% for the Netherlands, 6.8% for France and 2.0% for German trade-union members, but definitions differ and global labor-force accounting does not exist.

Wage dynamics varied sharply by skill and region. Skilled industrial labor in the Atlantic economies had bargaining power and rising real incomes over the late nineteenth century, while colonial agricultural laborers and landless workers were highly exposed to crop failure and price shocks. Education and human capital mattered increasingly in Germany, Japan, and the United States, where technical training and engineering capacity supported industrial sectors. Labour law advanced in 1900: Spain introduced employer liability for work accidents on 30 January, France limited covered working days to 11 hours from 30 March, Canada's Conciliation Act created a Department of Labour, New South Wales required wages to be paid in money, and the Murray Hill Agreement in the U.S. machinery trades set a 57-hour week. In Japanese cotton spinning, average daily operatives fell from 73,985 to 56,022 while daily wages rose from 26.6 to 31.0 sen for men and from 16.4 to 19.3 sen for women.

### 6B. Fixed income

Government bond markets were central to the global capital system. British consols, French rentes, German and Russian government obligations, U.S. federal and railroad debt, and colonial securities were major instruments. The French 3% perpetual rente rose from 99.05 to 101.10 between December 1899 and December 1900, lowering its current yield from 3.03% to 2.97%. In the United States, corporate railroad bonds averaged 4.05% against 3.12% for municipal bonds, a 93 basis-point spread between annual-average baskets that are not maturity-matched. ### 6C. Currencies

Gold-standard currencies had fixed parities. The dollar's gold price was $20.67 per ounce of pure gold, and the sterling-dollar par was about $4.867 per pound. Germany, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, and other core currencies operated within gold or gold-linked systems, with annual rates of 4.2012 marks, 5.1694 francs and 2.4857 guilders per USD. Off gold, the peseta depreciated 3.63% to 6.6943 per USD and the Italian lira stood at 5.51; the yen, on gold since 1897, averaged 2.0253 per USD in December, and the Mexican silver peso 2.06 for the year. China remained silver-based, India used a sterling-linked rupee regime, and several economies had hybrid or colonial currency arrangements. Capital controls in the modern sense were limited among core economies, but colonial and silver-standard monetary systems constrained local autonomy.

Gold was money rather than an alternative asset in the modern portfolio sense. ## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The active crises of 1900 were the Boxer Rebellion and foreign intervention in China, the Indian famine, the Boer War, the U.S. recession, and the beginning of industrial weakness in Russia. In India about 5 million people were receiving relief and 4.2 million worked on relief works by March; Britain borrowed through a GBP 30 million War Loan and Treasury bills; and the NBER chronology places the U.S. trough in December 1900. Each had different transmission channels: military occupation and indemnity expectations in China; crop failure, disease, and purchasing-power collapse in India; fiscal and bond-market pressure in Britain; credit and production adjustment in the United States; and foreign-capital-sensitive industrial slowdown in Russia.

Systemic vulnerabilities were building. Hindsight shows that the gold standard, imperial rivalries, naval arms spending, colonial fiscal extraction, and concentrated banking systems created fragilities that would become much more visible between 1905 and 1914. At the time, however, many observers saw globalization, gold convertibility, and industrial expansion as durable sources of order. The key policy mistake visible in retrospect was treating fixed exchange credibility and imperial fiscal extraction as substitutes for domestic welfare resilience.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1900 vs. 1899)

The most concrete year-over-year changes were crisis-specific. China moved from late-Qing strain into open anti-foreign rebellion and military intervention. India moved from monsoon failure into famine mortality and relief controversy. Britain moved deeper into Boer War financing and military mobilization. The United States remained in the 1899 to 1900 recession, even though its long-run industrial lead was intact. Germany and Japan continued industrial expansion.

The Maddison annual dataset does not permit a clean top-20 entry/exit comparison because several major 1900 rows lack 1899 values. Where annual GDP is available, Germany, Japan, Italy, Canada, Australia, India, Indonesia, Belgium, Spain, Austria, the Netherlands and the United States show positive real GDP growth, while Britain and France show small negative movements. In the Maddison Project Database 2023 the Netherlands also turns negative (-0.99%), and Italy and Japan grow much more slowly (+3.21% and +1.99%). These figures should be treated as historical reconstruction, not official national accounts. Monetary conditions eased in Europe as the Bank of England, the Reichsbank and the Banque de France cut rates from their late-1899 peaks, while the Bank of Japan tightened. Britain's public-sector deficit widened from 0.54% to 3.61% of GDP with Boer War borrowing.

Structurally, 1900 reinforced three trends already visible in 1899: the rise of U.S. and German industrial scale, the vulnerability of colonial agrarian populations, and the dependence of global finance on gold-standard credibility. The new risks were more geopolitical than purely economic: China, South Africa, and the naval-industrial rivalry in Europe.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

Reliable statistics were strongest for Britain, the United States, Germany, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Canada, and Australia, which carry Medium confidence in the ranking table. Even there, modern GDP, unemployment, and CPI categories are retrospective reconstructions. Official national accounts did not exist in the modern post-World War II form. Italy, Japan, Spain and Austria have reconstructed macro series that are less complete or more contested and carry Medium-low confidence.

Confidence is weaker for China, India, the Russian Empire core, Indonesia/Dutch East Indies, Mexico, Romania, Poland lands, and Czechoslovak lands, which carry Low confidence. Maddison-style estimates for colonial, semi-colonial, and pre-statistical economies can easily carry error bands of 20 to 30 percent or more. For 1900 the problem is the absence of modern national accounts and the use of modern territorial units for places that were not sovereign countries.

GDP, population and GDP per capita come from the Maddison 2010 horizontal workbook, in millions of 1990 international Geary-Khamis dollars, with population converted from thousands. The 2010 workbook is used for the annual 1900 levels and growth rates because it provides a consistent annual top-20 set; the Maddison Project Database 2023 (2011 international dollars) documents the current methodology and is shown in the indicator notes as a second vintage. The two vintages agree within about 0.1 percentage point for the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, France, Belgium, Austria, Canada, Australia and India, but differ for Spain and Indonesia and sharply for Italy (+5.57% against +3.21%), Japan (+4.31% against +1.99%) and the Netherlands (+0.22% against -0.99%). Mexico's growth is available only from the 2023 vintage. The 1901 report of this series uses rounded 1990-dollar levels together with Maddison Project Database 2023 growth; compared with these 1900 levels, its figures imply increases of about 27% for Spain and 11% for Japan, and its populations for British India (275.0 million) and the Dutch East Indies (39.5 million) are below the Maddison India and Indonesia territorial figures of 284.5 and 45.1 million. These differences reflect unit definitions and vintages, not economic change, and growth should not be computed across the two reports' levels.

The United Kingdom indicators come from the Bank of England's Millennium of Macroeconomic Data. Its CPI, Labour Force Survey-consistent unemployment, public-sector debt and current-account series differ from the Macrohistory Database vintage that supplies the 1900 comparators in the 1901 report, so British changes between the two reports mix definitions; the year-end Bank Rate of 4.00% matches the December average used there. The U.S. CPI of 1.01% is the precise Lindert-Sutch reconstruction, while a rounded Minneapolis Fed table implies zero. Mexico's 1900 peso rate of 2.06 per dollar is common to INEGI, COLMEX (2.062) and Gómez-Galvarriato-Musacchio, but their 1899 values differ (2.08, 2.075 and 2.10), so the annual change depends on the source; the Census copper compilation also prints two different Mexican 1900 outputs. The Belgian franc quote of 5.47 per dollar is not reconciled with the French rate, and the Macrohistory Japanese short-term rate of 11.50% is a market rate, so neither is used as an official series. For Spain, Canada, Australia, India and the Dutch East Indies only market, deposit or bank-specific rates are available; for Belgium and the Netherlands the official discount rates are known only as dated changes or as a series of uncertain timing. All of these appear in the notes rather than as policy-rate values.

Fiscal, debt, current-account and exchange-rate figures for historical states are not attached to modern-territory units whose borders differ: Russian Empire debt and budget ratios, the Cisleithanian budget and the Kingdom of Romania's ratios appear only in notes. The Austro-Hungarian Bank rate and the krone cross-rate are shown for Austria and the Czechoslovak lands because both lie within that monetary area, whereas the ruble area differs from the former-USSR territory. Debt and budget ratios use same-source nominal output, never the Maddison purchasing-power levels, and fiscal-year accounts divided by calendar-year output (United States, Japan, Canada) are labelled as such. Unemployment is available for only five of the twenty economies, under different definitions.

Historical data conflicts include nominal versus PPP GDP, modern-border versus historical-state units, and annual estimates versus benchmark-year interpolation. The weakest figures are the country-level macro indicators other than GDP, population, and GDP per capita. Data not available means that no verified observation with the stated definition and territory was found, not that no historical record exists. Important data that simply do not exist in reliable global form for 1900 include: global sector-level GDP by country, household debt, consumer confidence, comprehensive property prices, full company rankings by market capitalization or revenue, comparable unemployment rates, and standardized current-account balances for every top-20 economy. Values derived from the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database are used under the CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 license; Federico-Tena data are used under CC BY 4.0.
