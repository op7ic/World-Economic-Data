# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1924

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1924 was a stabilization year in the world economy. The dominant story was not a single boom or crash, but a shift from postwar disorder toward managed reconstruction, currency stabilization, and renewed cross-border lending. The Dawes Plan was the central European event: it stabilized Germany's reparations schedule, supported the new German currency regime, helped end the Ruhr crisis, and reopened Germany to large-scale foreign lending. This mattered far beyond Germany because reparations, inter-Allied debts, and U.S. capital exports formed a circular flow that shaped the entire late-1920s international financial system. Summed across the 51 economies with annual GDP estimates for both years in the Maddison Project Database 2023, recorded GDP was 4,143.9 billion 2011 international dollars, 6.20% above the same records in 1923; this is a mechanical change in source-defined records that include the Former USSR, not a measured world growth rate. World export volume rose 9.48% and moved above its 1913 level.

The global ranking of economies still looked very different from modern rankings. The United States was clearly first by reconstructed real GDP. China, using an explicitly interpolated Maddison-style estimate because the 2023 database lacks a direct 1924 GDP-per-capita value, was probably second. The United Kingdom and India were nearly tied, followed by Germany, France, the former USSR, Japan, Italy, and the Dutch East Indies, now Indonesia. These rankings are real-GDP purchasing-power estimates, not current-dollar national accounts. They should not be read as official 1924 national statistical releases.

The monetary background was the incomplete interwar gold standard. The United States was on gold and held a central reserve role. Britain had not yet returned to gold at the old parity, but policy was already oriented toward that 1925 decision. Many European currencies were still recovering from wartime inflation, fiscal stress, and exchange-rate instability. Germany had just ended hyperinflation through the Rentenmark and fiscal measures, while France, Belgium, Austria, and several other states still faced tensions between budget repair, currency confidence, and social demands. U.S. consumer prices were unchanged in the Bureau of Labor Statistics index after a 1.8% rise in 1923, British consumer prices fell 0.70%, and prices rose 14.04% in France and 17.06% in Belgium in the Macrohistory Database.

Microeconomically, 1924 was also a year of industrial consolidation and infrastructure formation. Ford-style mass production, radios, electrical equipment, tabulating machines, telephony, oil, aviation, cinema, and international shipping were important mechanisms through which macro recovery became visible. IBM adopted its modern name in 1924, Compagnie Française des Pétroles was created in France, Telefónica was established in Spain, Imperial Airways was created in Britain, and MGM was formed in the United States. These were not yet the largest global firms by modern standards, but they show the transition from wartime and postwar reconstruction toward communications, energy, data processing, aviation, and mass entertainment.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1924

**1. European financial stabilization after postwar disruption.** The Dawes Plan defined the year. It did not solve the reparations problem permanently, but it reduced immediate payments pressure, coordinated external supervision, encouraged evacuation of the Ruhr, and opened Germany to a large foreign loan. The German External Loan of 1924 targeted 800 million gold marks net; its U.S. tranche had a face value of $110 million, raised $95.625 million net and carried a 7% coupon. Its significance is that it converted a reparations crisis into a credit-dependent recovery. The countries most affected were Germany, France, Britain, Belgium, and the United States. The forward linkage was direct: late-1920s prosperity became increasingly dependent on U.S. lending and confidence in interwar debt and reparations flows.

**2. The United States as the financial center of gravity.** The U.S. economy had the largest reconstructed GDP, a deep capital market, and rising consumer-industrial strength. U.S. lending to Europe was decisive in the Dawes framework and broader capital flows. The U.S. stock market strengthened sharply, with the Dow Jones Industrial Average ending 1924 at 120.51, up about 26.16 percent for the year in common historical index series. Industrial activity nevertheless dipped: the Federal Reserve Board's manufacturing index (1919=100) averaged 114 against 124 in 1923 (-8.06%), while the Federal Reserve Bank of New York cut its discount rate from 4.5% to 3% between May and August. The forward linkage was the build-up of a U.S.-centered credit cycle that became a major vulnerability by 1928-1929.

**3. Incomplete return to the gold standard.** 1924 was between the wartime suspension of old gold rules and the attempted restoration of prewar monetary order. The United States was anchored to gold. Britain was preparing to return to gold, which it did in 1925. Germany had stabilized after hyperinflation, and its currency legislation of 30 August 1924 converted one trillion paper marks into one Reichsmark. Sweden restored the gold convertibility of the krona in 1924. The interwar gold standard was not simply the pre-1914 gold standard restored; it included more gold-exchange arrangements, reserve concentration, central bank coordination problems, and political constraints. Countries most affected included the United Kingdom, Germany, France, Austria, Belgium, Japan, and much of Latin America.

**4. Mass production and consumer credit in the United States.** U.S. growth was increasingly driven by automobiles, electrical goods, construction, advertising, radios, and installment credit. This was a microeconomic mechanism behind macro leadership. The Ford Motor Company remained central to autos, while General Motors was expanding a multi-brand, finance-supported competitive model. Household durable consumption and securities-market participation linked industrial output, consumer confidence, and finance. The Federal Reserve Board's index of building-contract values rose from 136 to 155 (+13.97%), and member-bank loans rose 5.93% in the year to December.

**5. Colonial and export-economy dependence.** India, Indonesia, Argentina, Brazil, Canada, Australia, Mexico, and many smaller economies were still tied to commodity exports, imperial finance, shipping, and sterling or dollar markets. Their growth was sensitive to agricultural prices, harvests, freight rates, and capital access. London quotations for Rio coffee rose 55.15% and English wheat 16.80%, while Newcastle coal fell 21.09%. The forward linkage was vulnerability to the commodity-price and trade collapse of 1929-1933.

**6. Soviet NEP recovery and statistical uncertainty.** The former USSR was recovering under the New Economic Policy after civil war and war communism. Agriculture, small trade, and light industry revived, but the statistical basis is weaker than for Western Europe or North America. The GDP figures should be read as approximate reconstructed values, not contemporary market-based national accounts. The 1924 currency reform exchanged 50,000 rubles of the 1923 issue for one gold ruble. The forward linkage was the policy turn toward forced industrialization later in the decade.

**7. Communications, aviation, oil, and cinema as emerging network industries.** 1924 saw important corporate formation in data processing, telecommunications, oil, civil aviation, and entertainment. IBM, Telefónica, CFP, Imperial Airways, and MGM indicate a broader shift toward network-scale firms. The effect was not immediate macro dominance in 1924, but these sectors became strategic in later decades.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The international monetary system was in a transition phase. The United States operated with gold convertibility and deep capital markets. Britain was trying to restore sterling credibility ahead of the 1925 return to gold. Germany had stabilized the currency after the 1923 hyperinflation, supported by domestic fiscal measures and the Dawes framework. France remained under fiscal and exchange-rate strain, with the franc still vulnerable until later stabilization.

Precise comparable policy rates are difficult because central-bank instruments differed. Bank Rate in Britain, Federal Reserve discount rates in the United States, Reichsbank operations in Germany, and Bank of Japan instruments are not directly equivalent. The safe conclusion is that credit was easing compared with crisis conditions, but international lending remained highly confidence-sensitive.

**United States.** The Federal Reserve Bank of New York discount rate, 4.5% at the end of 1923, was cut to 4% on 1 May, 3.5% on 12 June and 3% on 8 August. The Macrohistory short-term rate averaged 3.08% against 4.86% in 1923. Loans of all member banks rose from $19,052 million in December 1923 to $20,181 million in December 1924 (+5.93%) and their investments from $7,686 million to $8,845 million (+15.08%). Banks reported suspended during 1924 numbered 759, of which 595 were nonmember banks; suspensions are banks reported closed, not certified permanent failures.

**United Kingdom.** Bank Rate was 4% throughout 1924. The Macrohistory short-term rate averaged 3.39%, up from 2.62% in 1923.

**Germany.** The currency legislation of 30 August 1924 converted one trillion paper marks into one Reichsmark and set a legal minimum note cover of 40%, at least three-quarters of it in gold. The Reichsbank was reconstituted on 11 October and new notes appeared from mid-November. A Reichsbank discount rate with a documented definition is Data not available for 1924; the Macrohistory short-term rate was 11.11%.

**Japan.** Reconstruction after the 1923 earthquake kept credit demand and bank exposure high. The Macrohistory short-term series averaged 9.42% against 9.56% in 1923; it is not reconciled with the 8.03% Bank of Japan head-office commercial-bill rate, so no official rate is shown.

**Other economies.** The Oesterreichische Nationalbank raised its discount rate from 9%, the rate in force since 1 January 1923, to 12% on 5 June and 15% on 13 August, and cut it to 13% on 6 November. Official discount rates with a documented definition are Data not available for 1924 for France, Belgium, Italy, the Netherlands, Spain and Czechoslovakia. Macrohistory short-term rates rose in France (4.39% to 5.21%), the Netherlands (2.62% to 4.19%) and Belgium (5.44% to 5.50%), were little changed in Italy (5.38% to 5.35%) and unchanged in Spain (5.0%); these are market or reconstructed series, not policy rates. In British India, the Imperial Bank's rate on demand loans against government paper ranged from 4% to 9% during the year and ended December at 6%, against 7% a year earlier.

**Credit conditions and equities.** Major stock-market data are uneven. The Dow Jones Industrial Average ended 1924 at 120.51, a 26.16 percent annual rise in common historical index series; the U.S. equity portfolio in the rate-of-return reconstruction shows an 18.83% capital gain and a 25.26% total return. Unofficial Nikkei-style retrospective calculations put Japan's market up about 7.10 percent for 1924, but the modern Nikkei 225 was not officially calculated until after World War II. For London, Paris, Berlin, and other exchanges, comprehensive annual index data are not consistently available; the available reconstructions are shown in section 6A.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

Trade policy remained shaped by postwar debts, reparations, exchange controls, shipping networks, and imperial preference debates. The Dawes Plan was the most important economic-diplomatic event because it changed Germany's relationship with creditors and occupied territories. The Agent General for Reparation Payments disbursed 620.7 million gold marks between September 1924 and April 1925, a period that is not calendar 1924. The United States also passed the Immigration Act of 1924, which reduced immigration through national-origins quotas and had long-run implications for labor supply and trans-Pacific relations, especially with Japan.

Regional integration in the modern sense was limited. The European Economic Community did not yet exist. ASEAN did not exist. Trade networks were instead organized through empire, bilateral finance, shipping, commodity exchanges, and gold-standard constraints. The British Empire, French Empire, Dutch East Indies, and U.S.-Latin American relationships mattered more than formal regional trade blocs. World export volume rose 9.48% in the Federico-Tena index. In the Americas, the Pan American Sanitary Code, a regional public-health and trade instrument, was signed on 14 November 1924 by 18 signatories; U.S. ratification was deposited only in April 1925.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil was already strategic, but OPEC did not exist. Production and concessions were shaped by U.S. oil fields, British and Anglo-Persian interests, Dutch-Shell networks, and new French efforts to secure independent oil access. France's creation of Compagnie Française des Pétroles on 28 March 1924 reflects the postwar lesson that oil was a national strategic asset. U.S. crude production fell from 732.4 million to 713.9 million barrels (-2.52%), while its value rose from $978.4 million to $1,022.7 million (+4.52%). Mexican crude production was 139.7 million barrels; the 1923 comparison is unresolved between two sources.

Agricultural commodities were central for Canada, Argentina, Australia, India, Brazil, Indonesia, and much of Eastern Europe. Wheat, beef, wool, coffee, sugar, rubber, jute, rice, and cotton transmitted global price changes into rural incomes, fiscal receipts, rail freight, and bank credit. Metals and coal remained central to Britain, Germany, Belgium, France, Czechoslovakia, Mexico, and the United States.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor data are not comparable across countries in 1924. Definitions of unemployment differed sharply, and many agrarian economies had seasonal underemployment rather than recorded unemployment. Britain and Germany had visible industrial unemployment, while the United States had a more dynamic urban-industrial labor market. U.S. unemployment nevertheless rose from 3.2% to 5.5% in the Lebergott reconstruction, and the Federal Reserve Board's factory-employment index fell from 104 to 95. British unemployment eased from 8.1% to 7.2% in the Macrohistory Database. German trade-union unemployment reported to the International Labour Office fell from 26.5% in January to 8.1% in December, two endpoints rather than an annual rate. Immigration restrictions in the United States altered labor-market expectations and reduced the previous model of mass European migration. Urbanization continued across the United States, Japan, parts of Europe, Latin America, and colonial port economies.

World population rose to about 1,906.5 million (+1.12%) in the Federico-Tena reconstruction. In British India, average daily employment in cotton mills rose from 314,103 in fiscal 1923-24 to 337,790 in fiscal 1924-25 (+7.54%).

**Economic narrative:**

The United States was the largest economy in 1924 by a wide margin. Growth was moderate in the Maddison reconstruction, but the composition mattered: autos, electrical equipment, construction, advertising, consumer finance, petroleum, and mass retailing were changing the structure of demand. The economy had recovered from the 1920-1921 downturn, and equity markets were strong. The federal government also changed the labor-supply outlook through the Immigration Act of 1924, which reduced immigration through national-origins quotas and excluded most Asian immigration.

Industrial activity dipped within the year even as total output grew. The Federal Reserve Board's annual-average indices (1919=100) show manufacturing down from 124 to 114 (-8.06%), mining from 132 to 122 (-7.58%), factory employment from 104 to 95 and factory payrolls from 113 to 104, while the value of building contracts rose from 136 to 155 (+13.97%) and department-store sales, a nominal measure, edged up from 124 to 125. Monetary conditions eased: the New York Reserve Bank cut its discount rate in three steps from 4.5% to 3%, and member-bank demand deposits rose from $16,086 million to $17,766 million (+10.44%) and time deposits from $8,651 million to $9,805 million (+13.34%) between December 1923 and December 1924.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Automobiles remained the leading durable-goods industry, with Ford still central but General Motors building a finance- and brand-led competitive model. Radio, appliances, movie distribution, oil refining, and chain retailing were growing. IBM adopted its modern name in 1924, replacing the Computing-Tabulating-Recording Company name and signaling the commercial importance of tabulating and business-machine technologies. MGM was formed in the film industry, reinforcing Hollywood integration between production, stars, distribution, and theaters. Household installment credit and securities-market confidence were expanding, which was a source of strength in 1924 and a later vulnerability. Of the 759 banks reported suspended in 1924, 595 were nonmember banks, 129 national banks and 35 state member banks, and 39 previously closed banks reopened. Real consumption per person in the Barro-Ursúa reconstruction rose 0.31%, total bank loans in the Macrohistory Database rose 5.94%, and the Macrohistory nominal house-price index rose 1.58%. Newspaper asking-price indices rose 0.22% in New York City and 5.90% in Boston, while asking rents fell 2.12% in New York and 5.43% in Boston; these are hedonic asking-price estimates, not transaction prices.

### 6C. Currencies

Sterling was still below the old prewar gold parity in 1924, with historical dollar-pound data placing the annual dollar value of the pound below the later 1925 parity. Britain returned to gold at the old parity in 1925, not in 1924. Germany stabilized after hyperinflation through the Rentenmark and then the Reichsmark framework; the legislation of 30 August 1924 converted one trillion paper marks into one Reichsmark. Austria legislated the schilling on 20 December 1924, at 10,000 crowns per schilling from 1 January 1925, and the Soviet reform exchanged 50,000 rubles of the 1923 issue for one gold ruble. Sweden restored the gold convertibility of the krona in 1924. The French franc remained under pressure. Many colonial or commodity economies were linked to sterling, gold, silver, or managed local arrangements; in the Dutch East Indies, a decision of 3 May 1924 continued the minimum metallic cover of one-fifth of notes and other immediately payable claims. China lacked a unified national currency regime, and silver remained important.

In the Macrohistory Database annual series, sterling (0.2264 pounds per USD, from 0.2186, about $4.42 per pound), the yen (+17.96% more yen per USD), the lira (+5.59%), the peseta (+7.90%), the Belgian franc (+12.39%) and the guilder (+2.33%) weakened against the dollar, while the Canadian dollar strengthened (-2.07%); the restated French series shows about 16% more francs per dollar. ## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The active crisis of 1924 was the European stabilization problem. The Dawes Plan reduced immediate danger but also created a dependence on U.S. lending. This was not necessarily visible as a crisis in 1924 because the arrangement improved confidence, but hindsight shows it as a structural vulnerability. A sudden stop in U.S. capital flows would later become catastrophic for Germany and the wider reparations-debt system. The U.S. tranche of the Dawes loan alone had a face value of $110 million, with the loan service a first charge on German payments.

Currency risk remained high. Germany had stabilized, but the memory of hyperinflation shaped behavior. France and Belgium still had currency pressures, with consumer prices up 14.04% and 17.06%. Austria's central bank raised its discount rate to 15% in August before easing to 13%. Britain's attempt to restore gold at the old parity was already a looming policy risk, visible to contemporary critics and later widely judged as costly for employment and export competitiveness.

Commodity risk was another systemic vulnerability. Many top-20 economies depended on wheat, coffee, wool, beef, rubber, sugar, oil, coal, or metals. A broad fall in commodity prices would hit rural incomes, fiscal receipts, rail freight, banks, and external balances. This was not a black swan; it was a built-in exposure. Australia's external deficit widened to 9.01% of GDP, and London coal and sugar quotations fell while coffee rose 55.15%.

Banking fragility persisted at the margins of the stabilization. In the United States, 759 banks were reported suspended in 1924, mostly small nonmember banks, and in Argentina the Banco Español had to cut its capital by three-quarters.

Financial-market optimism in the United States was a near-term strength and a hindsight vulnerability. The 1924 equity rise and expanding consumer credit did not make a crash inevitable, but they formed part of the financial culture that later intensified leverage, speculation, and asset-price sensitivity.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1924 vs. 1923)

The most important change from 1923 to 1924 was Germany's move from hyperinflation and Ruhr disruption toward stabilization. The Maddison reconstructed growth rate for Germany was about 17.06 percent, reflecting a rebound from the 1923 breakdown, after a fall of 16.91% in 1923. France, Austria, Czechoslovakia, and the Netherlands also show strong reconstructed growth, and the former USSR records show a large rise in levels that is not presented as comparable-territory growth; these gains partly reflect recovery from earlier disruption rather than a stable long-run trend.

The U.S. economy moved from recovery into a stronger financial-market phase. The Dow's positive 1924 performance and the role of U.S. lending in the Dawes framework show the country's increasing importance as the marginal supplier of international capital. Compared with 1923, the United States became even more central to European stabilization. U.S. growth slowed from 12.51% to 2.44% in the MPD estimate, consumer prices stopped rising, unemployment rose from 3.2% to 5.5%, and the New York discount rate fell from 4.5% to 3%.

The policy regime also shifted. In 1923, the international problem was acute disorder: German hyperinflation, Ruhr occupation, reparations conflict, and postwar currency instability. In 1924, the problem became managed fragility: stabilization through international loans, central-bank credibility, fiscal discipline, and gold-standard expectations. That was an improvement, but it also created a more interconnected financial system.

The ranking changed in two places: the former USSR moved above Japan, and Czechoslovakia above Australia. Austria's appearance at rank 20 reflects the absence of an interpolated Poland, which the 1923 report ranked 11th, not a measured economic change.

Microeconomically, 1924 showed more visible consolidation in new network sectors than 1923. IBM, CFP, Telefónica, Imperial Airways, and MGM all point to an economy in which scale, systems, communications, and distribution mattered increasingly. These changes were not the main macro story of 1924, but they became major mechanisms of 20th-century growth.

In aggregate, the summed records of the 51 economies with annual MPD observations in both years rose 6.20%, a mechanical change that includes the Former USSR record (5.16% without it). World export volume rose 9.48% after 7.03% in 1923 and moved above its 1913 level. Inflation accelerated in France, Belgium and Spain, while British prices nearly stabilized after the 1923 deflation.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

**Highest-confidence data in this report:** the MPD 2023 dataset identity and its GDP per capita and population method, which give direct 1924 observations for the United States, United Kingdom, Germany, France, Japan, Italy, Canada, Belgium, Netherlands, Australia, and several European or settler economies. These are still reconstructed real purchasing-power estimates, not official 1924 nominal GDP figures, and the country rankings built from them are medium-confidence. Dated official records, including the Federal Reserve Board's annual report and its New York discount-rate changes, the Agent General's report on the Dawes loan, the German currency legislation, the Indian statistical abstract, the Oesterreichische Nationalbank discount schedule, the U.S. statutes and the Bank of England's Bank Rate, are also strong.

**Medium-confidence data:** MPD-based country GDP rankings for countries with direct 1924 GDP per capita observations, and reconstructed price, fiscal, consumption, credit and asset-return series from the Macrohistory Database and related reconstructions.

**Lower-confidence data:** the lowest-confidence top-20 entries are China, the former USSR, India, Indonesia, Czechoslovakia, Argentina, Mexico, Brazil, and other countries where contemporary national accounts were incomplete, colonial, fragmented, or non-market. China is especially important: it is included through a clearly labeled interpolation because MPD 2023 lacks a direct 1924 GDP-per-capita value while reporting population, so no growth rate is attributed to it. The former USSR is a reconstructed aggregate for a non-market economy under NEP, and a reasonable uncertainty band may be large. Colonial economies such as India and Indonesia are not fully comparable to independent states because fiscal, monetary, and trade policy were partly controlled externally.

**Very low comparability:** unemployment rates, CPI inflation, debt/GDP, current-account balances, budget balances, and policy rates across most countries. Definitions, territorial coverage, and institutions vary too much for the values in the country tables to be read as a harmonized panel; each is shown with its source definition, and presenting modern-looking tables with invented 1924 values would be misleading. Macrohistory short-term rates are market or reconstructed series and are given only in notes. Budget balances are not shown as values because the fiscal years behind the Macrohistory revenue and expenditure figures are not matched to calendar-year GDP; the arithmetic ratios appear in notes, as in the 1923 report. Exchange rates are Macrohistory annual levels in local currency per USD where a contemporary unit is available, on the same basis as in 1923; the exceptions are Germany, whose 1923 report showed the point stabilization rate of 4.2 trillion paper marks per USD but whose 1924 Macrohistory level crosses the conversion to the Reichsmark and is therefore not shown, and France and Australia, whose Macrohistory levels are in restated currency units. German consumer-price inflation, shown for 1923 from the Macrohistory index, is not shown for 1924 because that index spans the replacement of the paper mark. Policy rates are on the 1923 basis, with two changes: Austria, new to the ranking, shows the Oesterreichische Nationalbank end-year discount rate, and Japan, which showed the Bank of Japan commercial-bill rate of 8.03% as a value in 1923, is Data not available because that rate and the Macrohistory short rate of 9.42% for 1924 are not reconciled; the conflict is unresolved. The Belgian current-account field is a merchandise trade balance.

**Soviet and non-market economies:** former USSR GDP is especially uncertain. Even when MPD provides a direct value, the underlying economy involved weak price signals, civil-war recovery, changing borders, and administrative data limitations. A rough uncertainty band of plus or minus 20-30 percent is appropriate. The national-income reconstruction behind the record has not been matched to the territory of its population series, so the implied +28.65% change is not presented as growth.

**Colonial territories and modern labels:** India, Indonesia, and other entries use territorial labels for economies that were not sovereign in 1924. Indonesia refers to the Dutch East Indies territory. The MPD India record covers undivided India, dated to 1 October, not British India alone and not the post-1947 state. The Indian fiscal, banking, price and cotton-mill figures, by contrast, come from British India tables that list the Indian States separately and use fiscal years ending in March. Germany uses 1936 frontiers for 1919-1945, Austria and Czechoslovakia are reconstructed on later territories, and other Eastern European records use later frontiers.

**Conflicting sources:** U.S. unemployment is 5.5% in the Lebergott reconstruction and 5.0% in the Macrohistory Database; the Lebergott figure is shown, as in 1923. U.K. government debt is 188.81% of GDP in the Macrohistory Database and 179.70% in the Bank of England millennium dataset (version 3.1), the source of all Bank of England values cited, and U.K. unemployment is 7.2% and 7.12% respectively, with insured unemployment at 10.4% on a narrower denominator; the Macrohistory values are shown for consistency with other countries. The Macrohistory central-government budget arithmetic for Britain gives a surplus of 1.37% of GDP, while the Bank of England's public-sector net lending is -0.30%; the difference is unresolved. In Australia, the annual unemployment reconstruction fell while December trade-union unemployment rose; the two series are not reconciled. The Dow Jones rise of 26.16% and the rate-of-return reconstruction's U.S. price change of 18.83% use different portfolios, as do the London index's +11.39% and the reconstruction's +15.22%, and the retrospective Nikkei-style +7.10% and the reconstruction's Japanese price change of -1.78%. The Le Bris-Hautcoeur Paris index reproduces the French rate-of-return figures because the two share a lineage, so it is not independent corroboration. The U.S. long rate of 3.88% is the December point of a linear monthly path whose annual mean is 3.97%. Mexican crude production for 1923 is 119.6 million barrels in INEGI's historical statistics and 149.6 million in Meyer's table; the conflict is unresolved, so no 1924 growth rate is shown. Dutch East Indies postal-savings category changes sum to ƒ9,000 less than the change in the printed total; the residual is unresolved. World population on 1991 borders differs by about 6.4 thousand from the authors' historical-border alternative.

**Comparisons with the 1923 report:** the 1924 Indian banking table gives Imperial Bank private deposits for 1923 as Rs 742.0 million, against Rs 741.1 million in the 1923 report; the difference is unresolved. The Federal Reserve Board's 1924 report gives a 1923 factory-employment index of 104, against 101 in the 1923 report; the difference is unresolved, so only the 1924 report's levels are compared here. The Japanese property-price series is an early proxy rather than a metropolitan residential index, a caveat that applies equally to the 1923 figure. The 1923 ranking included an interpolated Poland, which is not included in 1924.
