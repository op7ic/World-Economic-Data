# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1918

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1918 was an economic hinge year: the final year of World War I, the year of the armistice of 11 November, and the first year in which the global economy began to feel the overlapping shock of demobilization and the influenza pandemic. The world economy was not operating under normal market conditions. Large parts of Europe were under war planning, rationing, price controls, shipping controls, capital controls, and military procurement systems. International trade was shaped less by comparative advantage than by blockades, troop logistics, shipping scarcity, convoy systems, foreign exchange controls, and government allocation.

The main macroeconomic pattern was a transfer of economic weight toward the United States and, to a lesser extent, Japan, while continental Europe and Russia were physically, fiscally, and institutionally weakened. The United States entered 1918 with industrial momentum from Allied demand and war mobilization, while Britain remained financially central but heavily indebted and dependent on wartime borrowing. Germany, Austria, France, Belgium, and Russia were deeply distorted by war damage, labor mobilization, food shortages, and the exhaustion of public finance. Japan benefited from an export and manufacturing boom as European competitors were displaced from Asian markets.

Inflation was one of the dominant facts of the year. Wartime borrowing, monetary expansion, shortages, shipping bottlenecks, and price controls created very high price pressure in many countries. Gold remained officially fixed at the prewar U.S. parity of $20.67 per troy ounce, but that figure did not represent a freely functioning international gold standard. The classical gold standard had effectively been suspended by wartime finance, capital controls, and exchange controls. The postwar international monetary order was therefore unsettled, and it would remain so until failed restoration attempts in the 1920s and eventual abandonment in the 1930s.

The top 20 ranking is built from Maddison Project Database 2023 historical GDP estimates in 2011 international dollars. China, the former Russian Empire and early Soviet area, and Poland have no 1918 observations in that database, so their levels are explicit, low-confidence bridging estimates and are flagged. This matters because a country ranking for 1918 is not a modern nominal GDP ranking. It is a historical real-output ranking under conditions of war, empire, colonial administration, and weak national accounts. ## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1918

**Theme 1: War economy reached its peak, then turned toward demobilization.** The final year of World War I saw the peak of state-directed production in the belligerent economies. Governments directed steel, coal, railways, shipping, food, credit, and labor toward military production and logistics. In the United States, the war economy expanded sharply even though direct U.S. participation was late relative to Europe. Britain, France, Germany, Austria, Italy, Canada, Australia, and Japan were already deeply integrated into wartime procurement and public borrowing systems.

The economic significance was not simply the level of military output. The deeper change was institutional. War boards, ministries of munitions, food administrations, exchange controls, and government purchasing systems changed the relationship between firms and the state. When the armistice arrived, a large part of demand that had been guaranteed by the state suddenly faced cancellation, creating a demobilization problem that would shape 1919 and 1920.

**Theme 2: Inflation, debt, and monetary disorder replaced the prewar gold standard order.** By 1918, the pre-1914 monetary regime was broken in practice. Gold parities still existed on paper in some cases, but convertibility, capital mobility, and market exchange rates were restricted by war controls. Governments financed war through taxation, borrowing, and central bank accommodation. The result was a broad inflationary environment, especially in Europe but also in the United States.

This theme mattered because it created the postwar policy problem: how to normalize money, debt, and exchange rates after years of emergency finance. The unresolved choice between austerity, inflation, devaluation, and restoration of gold parity became one of the central policy conflicts of the 1920s. In hindsight, it also foreshadowed later debt and monetary crises, especially in Central Europe.

**Theme 3: The United States moved closer to global financial leadership.** The United States was already the largest economy in real output terms before 1918, but the war accelerated its financial rise. European allies relied heavily on U.S. production, credit, and supplies. Britain remained an indispensable financial and imperial power, but war borrowing weakened its balance sheet. The U.S. dollar gained relative importance because U.S. banks, Treasury finance, and industrial exports were central to the Allied war effort.

The forward linkage is direct: the 1920s world economy would be marked by a larger U.S. role in international credit, commodity demand, and industrial technology. The shift did not instantly displace sterling, but 1918 made clear that the prewar London-centered order had been weakened.

**Theme 4: The influenza pandemic created a global labor and demand shock.** The 1918 influenza pandemic overlapped with the final military phase of the war and demobilization. It reduced labor supply, disrupted production and transport, increased health costs, and damaged household welfare. The shock was global, but mortality and economic effects were uneven. India experienced especially severe mortality. Military camps, ports, troop movements, and dense cities were important transmission environments.

The economic significance of the pandemic is difficult to measure because it coincided with war and postwar transition. Still, the pandemic was a real macro and micro shock: it affected labor availability, consumer demand, local services, health systems, and household savings. The recessionary effects in the United States were brief relative to the scale of mortality, but global welfare losses were enormous.

**Theme 5: Empires collapsed and statistical geography became unstable.** 1918 was a year of political-economic boundary change. The Russian Empire had already collapsed into revolution and civil war. The German, Austro-Hungarian, and Ottoman empires were moving toward dissolution. Poland re-emerged as a state late in the year. These events created direct economic dislocation through border changes, currency fragmentation, fiscal breakdown, transport disruption, and loss of administrative continuity.

This theme matters for data. A 1918 GDP estimate for "Russia," "former USSR," "Austria," or "Poland" is not the same kind of object as a modern national accounts observation. It is often a territorial or reconstructed estimate. Rankings involving these economies carry very high uncertainty.

**Theme 6: Japan and parts of Asia gained from wartime trade displacement.** Japan experienced a wartime export and manufacturing boom as European competitors could not supply Asian and Pacific markets as before. Japanese shipbuilding, textiles, chemicals, and light manufacturing expanded, while wartime profits and credit growth helped produce speculative pressures. India, Indonesia, and the Philippines were still colonial or colonial-linked economies with large agricultural bases and external trade shaped by imperial controls and wartime shipping scarcity.

The forward linkage was uneven. Japan entered the 1920s with expanded industrial capacity but also inflation, social tension, and financial fragility. Many colonial economies did not receive the same development benefits because gains from commodity production were constrained by imperial trade structures, unequal bargaining power, and limited domestic industrial policy autonomy.

**Theme 7: Food, fuel, and shipping were binding constraints.** The year was defined by shortages of food, coal, oil, shipping, and rail capacity. Germany and Austria faced severe supply shortages under blockade and war exhaustion. Britain and the Allies had to manage U-boat risk, convoy systems, shipping allocation, and food imports. Oil demand rose with mechanized war, naval fuel, transport, and industrial needs, while coal remained the dominant energy source.

The forward linkage was the postwar adjustment problem. Food shortages, transport bottlenecks, and fuel constraints did not disappear when fighting stopped. They fed social unrest, strikes, and inflation in 1919 and 1920.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

**World GDP and growth rate:** Data not available as a precise official 1918 global national accounts aggregate. Maddison Project style estimates allow reconstruction of many country totals in 2011 international dollars, but coverage gaps and wartime borders limit confidence. The top 20 estimates in Section 4 sum to approximately 3.69 trillion 2011 international dollars when the explicitly flagged bridging estimates for China, the former USSR/Russia area, and Poland are included. The 40 territories with Maddison Project Database 2023 observations for 1918 sum to 3,237.6 billion, against 3,244.9 billion in 1917 (-0.23%), but the sum leaves out large territories and overlapping borders are not resolved. Neither figure is a world GDP estimate or a world growth rate.

**Global trade volume:** The Federico-Tena world export-volume index (1913=100) fell 11.26%, from 84.39 in 1917 to 74.89. The index includes the authors' imputations for 51 wartime observations of belligerent and new polities, so it is a reconstruction rather than a census of world trade. Exports in current borders were $22,337 million against $22,302 million in 1917 in current dollars, a nominal measure from incomplete country coverage. Qualitatively, trade was heavily distorted by war, naval blockades, shipping shortage, submarine risk, convoy allocation, and state procurement. Trade among the Allied powers and their suppliers was prioritized. Central Powers trade was sharply restricted by blockade and the loss of normal shipping access.

**World population:** About 1,829.6 million, down 6.2 million (-0.34%) from 1917, in the Federico-Tena January 2026 reconstruction summed from 1991-border country series; the historical-border World26 variant gives 1,829.56 million (-0.34%). The net change is a model reconstruction, not a count of pandemic or war deaths.

**Reserve currency dynamics:** The prewar sterling-centered system was weakened, but not replaced overnight. The U.S. dollar became more important because the United States was the leading supplier and creditor to the Allies. Gold parities existed formally, but the classical gold standard was not functioning normally because convertibility, capital mobility, and free exchange markets were constrained by war controls.

**Global inflation trends:** Inflation was widespread. It reflected wartime borrowing, monetary expansion, shortages, rationing, shipping bottlenecks, military demand, and reduced civilian supply. U.S. consumer inflation was very high by historical standards, and British inflation was also severe. Comparable CPI data for many countries are not available or not comparable.

**International capital flows:** Capital flowed toward war finance. The United States became a central creditor and supplier to Allied economies. Britain remained financially important but was heavily burdened by war borrowing. Central Powers were largely cut off from normal international capital markets and financed war internally through borrowing and money creation.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

**United States:** The Federal Reserve was still a young institution. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York raised its discount rates on April 6, with separate rates by maturity and collateral. The policy stance supported war finance and the market for government securities. Credit conditions were shaped by Treasury borrowing, Liberty Loans, and bank participation in public finance. In the Federal Reserve Board's discussion of the weekly reporting banks of New York, loans and investments other than those tied to government securities stayed near $4.1 billion while 11 banks with about $350 million of resources joined the reporting group, so the stable total does not show unchanged credit at the same banks.

**United Kingdom:** Bank Rate was 5 percent throughout 1918. Britain used public borrowing, taxation, exchange controls, and imperial financial networks to sustain the war effort. Public debt was very high by the end of the war.

**Germany and Austria:** Data not available for directly comparable modern policy-rate series. Monetary conditions were dominated by war finance, Reichsbank and Austro-Hungarian Bank accommodation, debt issuance, shortages, and exchange controls. The financial effects became more visible after the war through inflation and currency instability.

**Japan:** The Bank of Japan raised its commercial-bill discount rate twice in the final months of the year. Monetary and credit conditions were expansionary in the sense that wartime export profits, industrial growth, and speculative activity expanded rapidly. The Bank of Japan and financial authorities faced rising prices and credit pressures.

**Exchange rate regime context:** Bretton Woods did not exist in 1918. The prewar classical gold standard had been suspended or heavily constrained by war controls in practice. Exchange rates were affected by capital controls, official support, wartime finance, and limited convertibility rather than free market gold flows.

**Stock markets and bonds:** Major stock exchanges existed in New York, London, Paris, Berlin, Amsterdam, Tokyo, Toronto, and elsewhere, but annual index levels and returns are available only for selected markets and reconstructed national portfolios. Government bond markets were dominated by war loans.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

There were no institutions such as the EEC or ASEAN in 1918. The central geopolitical economic fact was World War I. The Allied blockade restricted Central Powers access to food, raw materials, and shipping. German submarine warfare had already forced major adjustments in Allied shipping and convoy systems. U.S. participation changed the balance of resources, supplies, and credit.

Major trade tensions were not modern tariff disputes so much as wartime control systems. Governments determined shipping priorities, import licensing, exports of strategic goods, and access to foreign exchange. The armistice did not instantly normalize trade. The armistice of November 11 was a 30-day renewable agreement rather than a peace treaty, and its terms maintained the Allied blockade. Ports, railways, credit lines, and shipping capacity had to be redirected from military to civilian use.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil was becoming more economically important, though coal still dominated energy. U.S. oil production and pricing mattered because the United States was the leading oil producer and a central supplier. Wartime logistics, naval fuel, trucks, aircraft, and industrial production increased demand for liquid fuels.

In the Imperial Mineral Resources Bureau's 1921 summary, in long tons with coal including brown coal, U.S. coal output rose 4.12% to 605.5 million, U.K. output fell 8.35% to 227.7 million and German output 0.64% to 257.0 million. U.S. petroleum output rose 6.15% to 50.85 million long tons and Mexican output 15.44% to 9.12 million, while Russian output fell 62.41% to 3.14 million and Persian output rose 20.64% to 1.13 million. Metals such as steel, copper, nickel, and aluminum were strategic inputs, but wartime controls and government procurement make market price comparisons difficult. Agricultural conditions were severe in many regions. Food shortages and inflation were major causes of social unrest, especially in Germany, Austria, and parts of the collapsing Russian imperial economy.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor markets were disrupted by mobilization, casualties, troop movements, women entering war industries, colonial labor mobilization, and pandemic mortality. Unemployment rates are not comparable across countries and are often unavailable. The more relevant labor indicators were labor scarcity in war industries, strikes, real wage pressure, food access, and demobilization risk.

The influenza pandemic created an additional global labor shock. It removed workers temporarily through illness and permanently through mortality. The burden was uneven, and India was among the worst affected regions. Demographically, 1918 also intensified the economic consequences of wartime deaths, disability, orphanhood, and household income loss.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States was the largest economy in the 1918 ranking and was central to the Allied resource position. Its economy had moved from a prewar exporter and creditor with limited direct military involvement to a mobilized war economy. The state expanded control over railways, food, fuel, shipping, finance, and industrial procurement. The combination of Allied demand, U.S. war production, and government borrowing produced strong real output growth, even with inflation and allocation controls.

The Federal Reserve supported war finance by maintaining credit conditions compatible with Treasury borrowing and securities markets. Household consumption was constrained by inflation, patriotic bond drives, food campaigns, and the diversion of output to war uses. The post-armistice risk was not lack of wartime demand but the sudden need to unwind contracts, demobilize soldiers, and absorb workers back into civilian production.

The War Finance Corporation was created by the act of April 5 and received its presidential business authority on May 20. By November 30 it had authorized $42.2 million of advances to public utilities, advanced $40.9 million, been repaid $12.0 million and held $28.9 million outstanding; advances to canners were $211,500. These are partial-year flows and a stock, not a measure of the Corporation's effect on confidence.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Leading sectors included steel, shipbuilding, rail transport, oil, chemicals, agriculture, finance, and war-related manufacturing. Banking was heavily connected to Liberty Loan distribution and government securities; Macrohistory bank loans rose 8.22%. Consumer goods faced shortages and price increases, and Barro-Ursua real consumption per person fell 2.60%. The nominal house-price index rose 7.79%. The influenza pandemic reduced labor availability and household welfare, but available research suggests the U.S. macro downturn associated with influenza was short and overlapped with end-of-war adjustment.

**Economic narrative:**

Britain ended the war still a major industrial, imperial, shipping, and financial power, but with a much weaker fiscal position than before 1914. The war economy involved public borrowing, taxation, rationing, shipping controls, imports of food and raw materials, and coordination with the United States and empire. Bank Rate stayed at 5 percent, but the monetary regime was not a normal gold-standard regime.

The country faced the transition from military procurement to civilian production, while also managing labor expectations, returning soldiers, housing pressure, and inflation. Sterling remained important internationally, but Britain's role as the unrivaled financial center was diminished by war debts and dependence on U.S. resources.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Coal, shipping, finance, armaments, textiles, and food distribution were central. Railways and shipping were under wartime direction. ### 5B. Corporate landscape

A global top-10 or top-20 list by revenue or market capitalization for 1918 is not available in a consistent, verifiable cross-country form. The closest reliable corporate analysis is sectoral and institutional rather than ranking-based. Large firms in steel, oil, railways, shipping, chemicals, armaments, banking, and textiles were central to the war economy, but market value and revenue data are not comparable across wartime accounting systems.

Major corporate events were often not conventional mergers or IPOs. They were state controls, procurement contracts, requisitions, shipping allocation, railway control, and war-loan finance. In the United States, war industries were coordinated through government boards and Treasury finance. In Britain and France, ministries and control systems shaped production and allocation. In Germany and Austria, shortages and war finance created a more coercive allocation environment.

Nationalizations and state controls were economically significant. Railways, shipping, food supply, and fuel distribution were subject to emergency control in many belligerent countries. These arrangements did not always become permanent nationalizations, but they changed firm behavior and expectations. In the United States, a joint resolution of July 16 and a proclamation of July 22 placed telegraph and telephone systems under federal operational control from August 1; existing managers could continue, regular dividends and interest could be paid, and compensation was provided, so control did not transfer corporate ownership.

Named company events illustrate the period without forming a ranking. Chevrolet became part of General Motors on May 2, 1918, and its 1918 range included the Chevrolet Model T one-ton truck. In Japan, Matsushita began on March 7, 1918 with three people and had about 20 employees by the end of the year; the company's history dates its attachment plug to 1918.

### 5C. Innovation & technology

The economically important technologies of 1918 were not consumer digital technologies but industrial, military, medical, and communications technologies. Aviation expanded rapidly as an industry and as a technological field. Radio and wireless communication gained practical importance. Chemical production, industrial standardization, vehicle logistics, and mass procurement systems advanced under wartime pressure.

Public health knowledge and infrastructure were also tested by influenza. The pandemic exposed weaknesses in medical capacity, data collection, hospital organization, and urban public health management. The economic implication was a stronger later case for health administration, epidemiological surveillance, and public-health investment, though these changes were uneven and slow.

### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Labor markets were dominated by mobilization and scarcity, not by modern unemployment rates. Women entered war industries in large numbers in several belligerent economies, though postwar labor-market outcomes varied. Skilled workers in munitions, shipbuilding, railways, engineering, chemicals, and mining were strategically important.

Wage dynamics are difficult to compare across countries because nominal wages, rationing, subsidies, military pay, and inflation all interacted. In many places, real wages were squeezed by food prices. Labor unrest increased where workers perceived profits and prices rising faster than wages. In the United States, Proclamation 1440 of April 8 confirmed the National War Labor Board, whose principles included mediation and conciliation, the right to organize and bargain collectively, equal pay for women replacing men, the eight-hour day where existing law required it, and no strikes or lockouts during the war; these were stated principles, not measured outcomes.

Migration was economically significant through troop movements, refugees, displaced populations, demobilization, colonial labor recruitment, and postwar border changes. The pandemic further disrupted human capital by reducing survival, health, and household stability.

### 6C. Currencies

Currency markets were not normal floating markets. Gold convertibility, capital mobility, and free foreign exchange trading were restricted by war. The U.S. dollar became more important because the United States was a supplier and creditor. Sterling remained important but weakened in relative financial position. The German mark, Austrian crown, and Russian currencies faced severe postwar instability.

In annual Macrohistory rates, units per dollar fell 10.78% for the guilder, 8.66% for the mark, 8.17% for the Swiss franc, 5.87% for the peseta and 2.57% for the French franc, and rose 4.70% for the lira and 5.94% for the Belgian franc; sterling was unchanged. Devaluations and revaluations in the later postwar period cannot be projected backward mechanically into 1918.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The active crises of 1918 were unusually severe and overlapping. The first was the final military and economic phase of World War I. The second was the influenza pandemic. The third was state collapse and civil conflict in the former Russian Empire. The fourth was the fiscal and monetary overhang created by war finance. The fifth was food and fuel scarcity.

Near-misses and systemic vulnerabilities included the risk of Allied shipping failure, food shortages in urban Europe, social unrest from inflation, fragile banking systems exposed to government debt, and the difficulty of demobilizing millions of soldiers without mass unemployment or political instability. Some risks materialized immediately, such as revolution and state collapse in Central and Eastern Europe. Others unfolded later, such as the 1920-21 recession, postwar debt disputes, reparations conflict, and currency crises.

With hindsight, 1918 also shows the danger of restoring prewar monetary and fiscal assumptions after a war that had changed debt levels, social expectations, and international creditor positions. That hindsight should be used carefully. Policymakers in 1918 did not have modern national accounts, modern pandemic data, or knowledge of the exact path of the 1920s.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1918 vs. 1917)

The largest change from 1917 to 1918 was the transition from total war to armistice. In 1917, the economic system was still adapting to U.S. entry into the war, Russian revolution, and continuing military escalation. In 1918, U.S. mobilization reached much greater intensity, Russia's economic collapse deepened, Germany and Austria reached exhaustion, and the Allied resource advantage became decisive.

The United States accelerated from a supplier and new belligerent into the central industrial and financial support of the Allied side. Britain remained central but became more visibly debt-burdened. France and Belgium faced severe damage and reconstruction needs. Germany and Austria moved from constrained war economies into defeat, regime change, and monetary vulnerability.

The influenza pandemic was the most important new global non-military shock. It changed labor availability, mortality, public health conditions, and household welfare. Unlike many economic shocks, it was not limited to industrial or financial centers. It hit agrarian and colonial economies hard, with India especially affected.

In sector terms, 1918 was the peak and break point for war production. Military procurement, steel, shipbuilding, chemicals, railways, and logistics remained central, but the armistice created sudden uncertainty for firms built around state demand. Agriculture and food distribution remained politically sensitive because inflation and shortages persisted after fighting stopped. Among the Macrohistory consumer-price series, inflation rose by the most points in Spain (+28.77), the Netherlands (+12.87) and France (+12.24), and fell by the most in Germany (-26.96), Switzerland (-8.31) and Canada (-5.32).

The distribution of global economic influence shifted further toward the United States and Japan. The shift did not mean immediate U.S. global monetary dominance or Japanese regional dominance without limits, but it did mark a structural weakening of prewar European supremacy.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

**Most reliable areas:** U.S. and British monetary-policy rates, selected U.S. and British inflation estimates, U.S. oil and gold price data, and Maddison-style GDP estimates for countries with direct 1918 observations are relatively stronger. Even here, modern comparability should not be overstated.

**Moderate-confidence areas:** Western European GDP estimates, Japan's aggregate GDP estimate, and settler-economy estimates for Canada and Australia are usable but affected by wartime controls, mobilization, and historical reconstruction methods. France, Belgium, Germany, Italy, and Austria require special caution because war damage and military allocation distort welfare comparisons. Macrohistory, Barro-Ursua and Federico-Tena series are retrospective reconstructions.

**Weakest ranking areas:** China, the former USSR/Russia area, and Poland are the weakest entries in the top-20 table. None has a 1918 observation in the Maddison Project Database 2023. The former USSR/Russia area requires population interpolation and is affected by revolution and civil war. Poland is a territorial estimate for lands that became a state only at the end of 1918.

**Conflicting sources:** Maddison 2010 and the Maddison Project Database 2023 use different price bases and methods, so their GDP levels are not directly comparable.
