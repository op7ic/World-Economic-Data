# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1923

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1923 was a year of uneven postwar reconstruction, monetary trauma, and early 1920s expansion. The United States was the clear global output leader and, by Maddison Project Database estimates, expanded strongly after the 1920-1921 recession. Western Europe remained split between recovery and fragility: Britain had a modest recovery but persistent unemployment and weak staple industries; France was rebuilding while pressing Germany over reparations; Germany endured the defining monetary catastrophe of the year, the Ruhr occupation and hyperinflation. Summed across the 50 economies with annual GDP estimates for both years in the Maddison Project Database 2023, recorded GDP was 3,888.3 billion 2011 international dollars, 4.64% above the same records in 1922; this is a mechanical change in source-defined records that include the Former USSR, not a measured world growth rate. World export volume rose 7.03% but remained below its 1913 level.

A historically accurate GDP ranking cannot be made from modern nominal GDP tables. The ranking below uses Maddison Project Database 2023 GDP per capita and population in 2011 international dollars. Where MPD 2023 has a 1923 country observation, it is used directly. Where an economically essential country lacks a 1923 GDP per capita observation, such as China and Poland, the table uses a transparent linear interpolation between the nearest MPD GDP per capita observations and MPD population for 1923. These interpolated entries are flagged. This method puts the United States first, China second, the United Kingdom third, India fourth, and Germany fifth.

The year's dominant macro story was not a synchronized world cycle. It was a set of country-specific adjustments to the First World War settlement. Germany's crisis was rooted in reparations conflict, fiscal weakness, the Ruhr occupation, and money creation; the Rentenbank decree of 15 October and the introduction of Rentenmark notes on 15 November marked the turn to stabilization. Japan's economy was hit by the Great Kanto earthquake in September, with huge losses concentrated in Tokyo and Yokohama. The Soviet Union, formally created in late 1922, was recovering under the New Economic Policy. Italy consolidated early Fascist economic policy under a fiscal and business-confidence agenda. China remained politically fragmented under warlord-era conditions, with treaty-port commerce and regional finance more meaningful than unified national macro policy.

The global monetary system remained transitional. Bretton Woods did not exist. The classical gold standard had not been fully restored. The U.S. dollar was on gold, sterling was still off gold, and several European currencies were unstable or under reconstruction. International finance was shifting toward a dollar-sterling dual center, with New York gaining importance while London remained central to trade finance. Commodity markets were generally calmer than in the immediate postwar boom-bust: U.S. crude oil first purchase prices averaged $1.34 per barrel in 1923, down 16.77% from $1.61 in 1922, while the official U.S. gold price remained $20.67 per troy ounce under the gold standard framework. U.S. consumer prices rose 1.8% after a 6.1% fall in 1922, and British consumer prices fell a further 6.0%.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1923

**1. The German hyperinflation and the end of passive resistance.** Germany's 1923 crisis was the most dramatic macroeconomic event of the year. French and Belgian occupation of the Ruhr from 11 January, after Germany's reparations default, triggered passive resistance, disrupted coal and industrial production, and drove fiscal expenditures that were financed through money creation. The Papiermark collapsed, and by late 1923 the Rentenmark stabilization halted the inflationary spiral. The Rentenbank decree of 15 October set the bank's capital and base reserve at 3,200 million Rentenmark, which also capped the note issue, and made the notes redeemable into Rentenbriefe rather than gold coin; the notes were introduced on 15 November. The immediate effect was social and financial destruction: cash balances, savings, fixed incomes, and wage contracts were upended. The forward linkage is direct: stabilization in late 1923 set the stage for the Dawes Plan in 1924, renewed foreign lending, and the fragile mid-1920s German recovery.

**2. U.S. industrial expansion after the 1920-1921 recession.** The United States entered 1923 with powerful industrial momentum. Maddison-based real GDP for the United States rose about 12.5 percent from 1922 to 1923 using GDP per capita multiplied by population. The Federal Reserve Board's annual-average index of manufacturing production (1919=100) rose from 103 to 124 (+20.39%) and its mining index from 95 to 132 (+38.95%), but the seasonally adjusted index of basic production peaked at 127 in May and ended the year at 110, below December 1922's 116. Autos, electrical equipment, construction, consumer durables, advertising, and installment credit were the visible microeconomic channels. The Federal Reserve's New York discount rate moved from 4.0 percent early in the year to 4.5 percent later in the year, indicating firmer but not crisis-level monetary conditions. The forward linkage was the maturing of the U.S. "Roaring Twenties" expansion and the buildup of private credit and equity-market optimism later in the decade.

**3. A divided Europe: reconstruction, stabilization, and postwar unemployment.** Europe was not a single economy in 1923. France and Belgium pushed the Ruhr strategy and were still reconstructing war-damaged regions. Britain experienced modest output growth but continued to carry high unemployment in coal, shipbuilding, textiles, and export industries. Austria was under League of Nations financial reconstruction, and its new central bank began operations on 1 January 1923; Poland faced currency instability ahead of the zloty reform of 1924. Consumer prices rose 14.75% in Belgium and 11.76% in France in the Macrohistory Database while they fell in Britain and the Netherlands. This theme matters because the postwar settlement linked fiscal burdens, reparations, exchange rates, and political legitimacy. The forward linkage was the mid-1920s stabilization wave, including Germany's Dawes Plan, Britain's 1925 gold return, and several Eastern European monetary reforms.

**4. Non-Western economies were large but statistically uneven.** China and India were among the largest economies by output volume, but their 1923 statistical base is much weaker than U.S. or Western European data. China's MPD 2023 GDP per capita is not directly observed for 1923, so the China entry uses interpolation between 1913 and 1929 GDP per capita observations combined with the MPD 1923 population figure. India has a direct 1923 MPD entry, but it reflects a colonial economy dominated by agriculture and subject to monsoon, commodity, and administrative data limitations. This matters because modern narratives often underweight large Asian economies when using only official national accounts. The forward linkage is the long-run divergence between output scale and income per head.

**5. Disaster as macro-financial shock in Japan.** The Great Kanto earthquake struck Japan on 1 September 1923. It was a localized but enormous economic shock to the Tokyo-Yokohama region, damaging firms, banks, transport, housing, and inventories. The Bank of Japan and government responded through special financial support and earthquake bills, helping prevent immediate credit collapse but leaving balance-sheet problems that contributed to later banking fragility. A payments moratorium followed on 7 September, and an ordinance of 27 September set a ceiling of 100 million yen on government indemnity for Bank of Japan losses on rediscounted earthquake bills; the moratorium was lifted in October. Bank of Japan notes issued rose from 1,136.0 million yen on 18 August to 1,356.7 million on 15 September (+19.43%), and its claims on the private sector from 263.2 million to 503.9 million yen (+91.44%). The forward linkage was the 1927 Japanese financial crisis and the acceleration of urban reconstruction, building standards, and state intervention in financial stabilization.

**6. Early interwar capital flows and international financial governance.** The League of Nations financial reconstruction of Austria and other stabilization efforts showed an emerging model of multilateral oversight, conditional lending, and external guarantees. At the same time, U.S. capital was becoming more important in European stabilization, while London retained deep trade-finance networks. This theme is significant because it foreshadowed later international financial institutions, even though the 1920s system remained ad hoc, creditor-driven, and politically fragile.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

**World GDP and growth.** A fully official, annually observed world GDP figure for 1923 is not available in the same sense as modern World Bank or IMF data. Using MPD 2023 country data with direct observations plus clearly labeled GDP per capita interpolation where needed, the available-country sum for 1923 is approximately **4.673 trillion 2011 international dollars** across 76 countries with usable GDP per capita and population data. This is not a complete modern national-accounts world total. It is a Maddison-style historical estimate and should be treated as approximate. Without interpolation, the 51 economies with an annual 1923 MPD observation sum to 3,902.2 billion 2011 international dollars. The 50 of them with an annual 1922 observation sum to 3,888.3 billion against 3,715.8 billion in 1922, a mechanical change of +4.64% that includes the Former USSR record, whose growth is not presented as comparable-territory growth, and excludes China and Poland. Neither sum is a certified partition of the world economy.

**World population.** World population reached about 1,885.3 million in the Federico-Tena reconstruction on 1991 borders, up 17.8 million or 0.95% from 1922. Population is not divided into a partial GDP sum to give world income per person.

**Global trade volume.** The Federico-Tena world export-volume index (1913=100) rose from 87.68 in 1922 to 93.84 in 1923 (+7.03%), so world trade was still below its prewar volume. Trade was recovering from the postwar slump but remained distorted by reparations, exchange instability, transport reorganization, and trade barriers.

**Reserve currency dynamics.** The world was in a partial gold-standard restoration phase. The U.S. dollar remained convertible into gold at the official parity. Sterling was not yet restored to gold and would not return until 1925. Central-bank reserve composition data for 1923 are Data not available, but the institutional direction was toward a dollar-sterling system rather than the pre-1914 sterling-dominated gold standard.

**Global inflation trends.** No consistent global CPI aggregate is available. The dominant inflation event was Germany's hyperinflation. Austria was stabilizing after its earlier postwar inflation. The United States and Britain were beyond the sharp 1920-1921 deflation but not in a high-inflation regime: U.S. consumer prices rose 1.8% in the Bureau of Labor Statistics index, while British prices fell 6.00% in the Macrohistory Database. Belgium (+14.75%) and France (+11.76%) saw renewed inflation as their currencies weakened, while prices fell in the Netherlands (-3.36%), Japan (-0.90%) and Italy (-0.58%). Many developing and colonial economies lack comparable CPI data.

**Key commodity prices.** U.S. crude oil first purchase prices averaged **$1.34 per barrel** in 1923, against $1.61 in 1922. The official U.S. gold price was **$20.67 per troy ounce**. Copper, wheat, rice, rubber, and sugar prices varied by market and contract; no single world price exists, but London quotations for selected grades are shown in section 3D.

**International capital flow direction.** Capital was moving from the United States and Britain toward European reconstruction, stabilization loans, and commodity-export economies. League of Nations loans and private banking syndicates were especially important for Central Europe. Capital flows to Latin America and the Dominions remained tied to railways, ports, land, mining, and public works. Aggregate flow totals are Data not available.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

**United States.** The New York Federal Reserve discount rate was 4.0 percent early in 1923 and 4.5 percent for much of the remainder of the year. Monetary policy was firmer than in the immediate recovery phase but not restrictive enough to stop industrial expansion. The number of Federal Reserve member banks fell from 9,859 on 29 December 1922 to 9,774 on 31 December 1923, a net loss of 85; membership losses included 102 insolvencies or suspensions, and these are membership changes, not a count of all U.S. bank failures. Total bank loans in the Macrohistory Database rose 10.56%.

**United Kingdom.** Bank Rate was 3 percent in the first half of 1923 and 4 percent in the second half. Britain was preparing, politically and financially, for eventual gold restoration, but the economy remained burdened by unemployment and weak exports. The Macrohistory short-term rate averaged 2.62%, little changed from 2.57% in 1922.

**Germany.** Standard interest-rate comparisons break down under hyperinflation. The Reichsbank and money market operated in a collapsing unit of account until the late-1923 stabilization. The relevant policy shift was not a conventional rate decision but the fiscal-monetary stop to passive resistance and the creation of the Rentenmark. The Rentenbank's note issue was limited to its capital and base reserve of 3,200 million Rentenmark, an authorization ceiling rather than the amount actually issued.

**Japan.** The Great Kanto earthquake forced extraordinary liquidity and credit measures. The Bank of Japan's emergency support helped prevent an immediate credit crunch but transferred risks into bank balance sheets and bills that later became politically and financially contentious. The Bank of Japan's commercial-bill discount rate remained at 8.03%, the rate in force since November 1919, while the Macrohistory short-term series averaged 9.56%.

**Other economies.** Official discount rates with a documented definition are Data not available for 1923 for France, Belgium, Italy, the Netherlands, Spain and Czechoslovakia. Macrohistory short-term rates rose in Belgium (4.71% to 5.44%) and France (4.29% to 4.39%) and fell in the Netherlands (3.55% to 2.62%), Italy (5.80% to 5.38%) and Spain (5.5% to 5.0%); these are market or reconstructed series, not policy rates. In British India, the Imperial Bank's rate on demand loans against government paper ended December 1923 at 7%, as a year earlier.

**Exchange-rate regime.** Bretton Woods did not exist. Exchange rates were a mixture of gold-linked currencies, managed floats, unstable paper currencies, and colonial pegs. The German mark collapsed before the Rentenmark reform. Sterling remained off gold. The U.S. dollar was the strongest anchor among major currencies.

**Credit conditions and equities.** U.S. credit conditions supported consumer durables and construction, while Germany's credit markets were disorganized by inflation. The Dow Jones Industrial Average return for 1923 was about **-3.25 percent** after a strong 1922; the U.S. equity portfolio in the rate-of-return reconstruction shows a -2.62% capital change and a +3.42% total return. ### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The Ruhr occupation was the main geopolitical-economic event in Europe. France and Belgium occupied the Ruhr after Germany's reparations default, seeking payment in kind and pressure on German fiscal compliance. The result was not a clean extraction of resources but a wider crisis in German production, finance, and politics.

Trade disputes were less institutionalized than in the later GATT era. Tariffs, exchange controls, reparations deliveries, colonial preferences, and bilateral debt settlements shaped flows. The United States had adopted the Fordney-McCumber Tariff in 1922, and its protectionist effects continued into 1923. Regional integration institutions such as the EEC did not exist. ASEAN did not exist. The League of Nations played a financial and technical role but not a free-trade integration role. World export volume nevertheless rose 7.03% in the Federico-Tena index.

The Washington naval treaty of February 1922 took effect only when ratifications were deposited on 17 August 1923, after U.S. presidential ratification on 9 June; it was proclaimed in the United States on 21 August. Between the United States and Mexico, an aide-memoire of 28 August proposed the renewal of diplomatic relations on 31 August, accreditation on 3 September and the signature of claims conventions on 7 September; these were planned steps in the diplomatic record.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil markets were still pre-OPEC. The United States was a dominant producer, and Mexico remained important but was past its most spectacular early production phase. U.S. crude oil first purchase prices fell to $1.34 per barrel in 1923 from the higher postwar levels of 1920-1922, including $1.61 in 1922 (-16.77%). Coal remained central to European industry, making the Ruhr conflict economically decisive. Electricity, petroleum products, and autos were transforming the U.S. industrial structure, while much of Asia, Latin America, and Africa remained tied to agricultural and mining export cycles.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

No comparable global unemployment rate exists for 1923. The best-supported general statement is that labor-market conditions differed sharply by country. U.S. employment improved with industrial recovery: the Lebergott reconstruction puts unemployment at 3.2% after 7.6% in 1922, and the Federal Reserve Board's factory-employment index rose from 90 to 101. Britain had persistent unemployment in staple export industries, at 8.1% in the Macrohistory Database after 9.8%. Germany's hyperinflation disrupted wage bargaining and household cash management, and measured unemployment rose from 0.7% to 4.5%. Japan's disaster caused regional displacement and reconstruction labor demand. Urbanization continued, especially in the United States, Japan, Latin American capitals, and treaty-port China. Migration was economically important but increasingly constrained by quota regimes and postwar border controls.

World population rose to about 1,885.3 million (+0.95%) in the Federico-Tena reconstruction. In British India, average daily employment in cotton mills fell from 321,790 in fiscal 1922-23 to 314,103 in fiscal 1923-24 (-2.39%) even as the number of mills rose from 242 to 259.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States was the dominant economy of 1923 by a wide margin. Recovery from the 1920-1921 contraction had turned into a broad industrial upswing. Autos, petroleum products, electrical machinery, telephones, construction, advertising, and consumer finance formed the microeconomic core of the expansion. The economy was not free of weakness: agriculture remained under pressure relative to urban industry, and income gains were uneven. The Federal Reserve Board's annual-average indices (1919=100) show manufacturing up from 103 to 124 (+20.39%), mining from 95 to 132 (+38.95%) and factory employment from 90 to 101, while agricultural marketings slipped from 110 to 108.

Policy was not radically expansionary, but credit was available enough to support private investment and durable-goods consumption. The Federal Reserve moved rates modestly higher, and the equity market cooled after 1922. The expansion also peaked within the year: the seasonally adjusted index of basic production rose from 116 in December 1922 to 127 in May 1923 and then fell to 110 in December, 13.39% below the May level. Hindsight risk: the same consumer credit, real-estate enthusiasm, and equity-market optimism that supported the 1920s boom later became vulnerabilities.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Ford and General Motors competed in a rapidly expanding auto market. Ford's U.S. production rose from 1,373,331 to 2,120,898 vehicles (+54.43%), including 1,817,891 cars and 193,234 trucks; the total also counts tractors and Lincoln output. Radio broadcasting and electrical appliances were moving from novelty to commercial industry. Chain retail, mail order, and national advertising helped create a more integrated consumer market; the Federal Reserve Board's index of department-store sales, a nominal measure, rose from 111 to 124 (+11.71%). Banking conditions were much healthier than in 1920-1921, but rural banks remained exposed to farm distress, and Federal Reserve membership fell by 85 banks. Real consumption per person in the Barro-Ursúa reconstruction rose 7.69%, total bank loans rose 10.56%, and the Macrohistory nominal house-price index rose 2.92%. Newspaper asking-price indices rose 0.21% in New York City and 10.76% in Boston, while asking rents rose 0.68% in New York and 16.59% in Boston; these are hedonic asking-price estimates sampled mostly in late May, not transaction prices.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The German hyperinflation was the year's systemic crisis. It exposed how fiscal breakdown, external claims, monetary accommodation, and political conflict could destroy a currency. The immediate crisis was resolved in late 1923, but the social memory and distributional shock persisted. The Rentenmark was redeemable into Rentenbriefe rather than gold, so its credibility depended on fiscal discipline and the limit on issue.

The Great Kanto earthquake was the major natural-disaster shock. It was not only a humanitarian and urban disaster but also a financial shock, because damaged firms and banks required extraordinary support. Hindsight links the earthquake-bill problem to later Japanese banking fragility. The government indemnity was capped at 100 million yen, but the Bank of Japan's private-sector claims nearly doubled within a month.

Austria, Poland, and other Central and Eastern European states faced stabilization risk. The League of Nations model could restore confidence, but it often imposed fiscal discipline under external oversight, creating political tension.

Banking fragility appeared outside the main crisis economies. The Home Bank of Canada failed in August, and in September a rescue plan for the Banco di Roma was presented in Italy. In the United States, the seasonally adjusted index of basic production fell 13.39% from its May peak by December.

Commodity-export economies faced price and demand volatility. Argentina, Canada, Australia, Brazil, Mexico, and the Dutch East Indies all depended on external markets. A fall in commodity prices or tighter global credit could transmit quickly into incomes and fiscal accounts. London coffee and wheat quotations fell while sugar and cotton rose, and Australia's external deficit widened to 7.49% of GDP.

Hindsight risk: the U.S. boom was becoming more credit-intensive. This did not make a crash inevitable in 1923, but consumer credit, real-estate expansion, and later equity speculation became more important as the decade advanced.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1923 vs. 1922)

The most important structural break was Germany's collapse. MPD real GDP for Germany fell about 16.9 percent from 1922 to 1923, after growth of 8.8 percent in 1922. This was not a normal recession; it was a currency, fiscal, political, and industrial crisis tied to the Ruhr occupation and hyperinflation.

The United States strengthened dramatically, rising about 12.5 percent in the MPD real GDP estimate, against 4.9 percent in 1922. The shift from post-recession recovery to consumer-industrial expansion became clearer. Autos, construction, and electrical sectors were the mechanisms. Consumer prices turned from a 6.1% fall to a 1.8% rise, and the New York discount rate moved up from 4% to 4.5%.

The United Kingdom moved above India and Germany in the ranking because of modest British growth, Indian contraction, and German collapse. This ranking movement should not be misread as a return to prewar British dominance: Britain's structural problems remained acute. British growth slowed from 9.6 percent to 3.2 percent, and Bank Rate rose from 3% to 4%.

The former USSR rose above Italy due to strong recovery from a depressed post-civil-war base. This was a rebound under NEP, not yet the later Stalinist industrialization drive. The movement is a change in GDP levels; the implied growth rate is not a comparable-territory measure.

China remained second in the interpolated ranking. Its position was stable by aggregate scale, but the data are weaker than for advanced economies. Its political fragmentation meant that aggregate GDP did not translate into unified state capacity.

Japan's aggregate GDP grew slightly in MPD data, but the year-over-year comparison hides the September earthquake's concentrated destruction. A local shock in Tokyo-Yokohama had national financial consequences.

In aggregate, the summed records of the 50 economies with annual MPD observations rose 4.64%, a mechanical change that includes the Former USSR record. World export volume rose 7.03% after 15.29% in 1922, and the U.S. crude oil first-purchase price fell 16.77%. Inflation re-emerged in France and Belgium, while Britain and the Netherlands continued to deflate.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

**Highest-confidence data in this report:** the MPD 2023 dataset identity, GDP per capita and population method, U.S. and U.K. policy-rate series, EIA crude oil price, and the broad historical facts of Germany's Ruhr-hyperinflation crisis and Japan's Great Kanto earthquake. Dated official records, including the Federal Reserve Board's production indices and membership counts, the Bank of Japan's discount-rate table and balance sheet, the Indian statistical abstract, the Rentenbank decree and treaty texts, are also strong.

**Medium-confidence data:** MPD-based country GDP rankings for countries with direct 1923 GDP per capita observations. MPD estimates are not official national accounts in the modern sense, but they are the appropriate source for long-run cross-country historical GDP comparisons. Reconstructed price, fiscal, consumption, credit and asset-return series from the Macrohistory Database and related reconstructions are moderate in quality.

**Lower-confidence data:** China and Poland GDP levels because GDP per capita is interpolated. The inclusion of China in the top ranks is robust, but the precise 1923 value is not. Poland's top-20 inclusion is plausible but weaker than direct-observation countries. Neither has an annual 1923 observation, so no growth rate is attributed to them; ranked by annual observations only, the top 20 would include Sweden and Switzerland instead.

**Very low comparability:** unemployment rates, CPI inflation, debt/GDP, current-account balances, budget balances, and policy rates across most countries. Definitions, territorial coverage, and institutions vary too much for the values in the country tables to be read as a harmonized panel; each is shown with its source definition. Macrohistory short-term rates are market or reconstructed series and are given only in notes. Budget balances are not shown as values for 1923 because the fiscal years behind the Macrohistory revenue and expenditure figures are not matched to calendar-year GDP; the arithmetic ratios appear in notes, whereas the 1922 report showed them as values. Exchange rates for 1923 are Macrohistory annual levels in local currency per USD, where a contemporary unit is available (the exception is Germany, whose value is the point stabilization rate of 4.2 trillion paper marks per USD reached in late 1923), while the 1922 report used New York December monthly means in U.S. cents per unit (with an annual-average exception for Mexico); the two bases are not directly comparable, and no December means are available for 1923. Policy rates likewise differ in basis: the 1922 report showed end-December official discount rates compiled by the League of Nations as values for Germany, France, Belgium, Italy, the Netherlands, Spain and Czechoslovakia, but no such rates with a documented definition are available for 1923, so those cells are Data not available and only Macrohistory short-term rates appear in notes. The French and Australian Macrohistory levels are in restated currency units and are not used, and the Belgian current-account field is a merchandise trade balance.

**Soviet and non-market economies:** former USSR GDP is especially uncertain. Even when MPD provides a direct value, the underlying economy involved weak price signals, civil-war recovery, changing borders, and administrative data limitations. A rough uncertainty band of plus or minus 20-30 percent is appropriate. The national-income reconstruction behind the record has not been matched to the territory of its population series, so the implied +15.69% change (+15.7% rounded) is not presented as growth.

**Colonial territories and modern labels:** India, Indonesia, and other entries use territorial labels for economies that were not sovereign in 1923. Indonesia refers to the Dutch East Indies territory. The MPD India record covers undivided India, dated to 1 October, not British India alone and not the post-1947 state. The Indian fiscal, banking, price and cotton-mill figures, by contrast, come from British India tables that list the Indian States separately and use fiscal years ending in March. Germany uses 1936 frontiers for 1919-1945, and Czechoslovakia and other Eastern European records use later frontiers.

**Conflicting sources:** U.S. unemployment is 3.2% in the Lebergott reconstruction and 2.4% in the Macrohistory Database; the Lebergott figure is shown. U.K. government debt is 195.54% of GDP in the Macrohistory Database and 185.79% in the Bank of England millennium dataset, with scope and timing unreconciled; the Macrohistory value is shown for consistency with other countries. The Bank of Japan commercial-bill rate of 8.03% differs from the Macrohistory Japanese short rate of 9.56%; the facility and averaging behind the difference are unresolved. Spanish real consumption per person rises 1.70% in the direct Barro-Ursúa data but 0.06% in the Macrohistory-linked series. The Banco de México history gives the 1923 deficit as both 58.7 and 58.6 million pesos. Federal Reserve membership additions are printed as 210 but the listed components sum to 212, and losses as 295 against 297; the net loss of 85 agrees. Indian central debt is revised between the closing figure for 31 March 1923 (Rs 3,235.9 million) and the next year's opening figure (Rs 3,236.3 million), and an alternative table reading for 31 March 1922 is not used. World population on 1991 borders differs by about 6.4 thousand from the authors' historical-border alternative. The Dow Jones return of -3.25% and the rate-of-return reconstruction's U.S. price change of -2.62% use different portfolios, and the London index's +0.08% differs from the reconstruction's -4.17% for the same reason. The Le Bris-Hautcoeur Paris index reproduces the French rate-of-return figures because the two share a lineage, so it is not independent corroboration. Japanese wholesale and retail prices differ sharply between annual means and December comparisons because of the post-earthquake price jump; these are timing differences, not conflicting estimates. The Bank of Japan Tokyo wholesale series used here gives a 1922 annual mean of 258.99, while the 1922 report cited a different Tokyo wholesale index on a 1913 base, so the two years' figures are not on the same index. The Bank of Japan Review article on the interwar Japanese economy listed in the references is by M. Shizume, not T. Okazaki, who wrote the separate study of the Great Kanto earthquake.

**Important missing data:** reserve composition, many CPI series, unemployment, corporate revenue rankings, global M&A, sovereign yield curves, most real-estate prices, and sectoral employment by country are Data not available. National CPI and unemployment remain unavailable for China, India, Indonesia, Poland, Argentina, Czechoslovakia, Mexico and Brazil; wholesale and city price indices are shown in notes but not substituted. Data not available means that no observation with the stated definition and territory is available for 1923, not that no historical record exists.
