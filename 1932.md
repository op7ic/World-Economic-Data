# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1932

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1932 was a year of continuing severe depression for much of the market-economy world, with national turning points differing; the US business-cycle trough came only in March 1933. The MPD 2023 country-coverage aggregate, covering 55 non-overlapping country rows with annual GDP and population, falls to 5,018.5 billion 2011 international dollars, a -4.48% change from the 5,253.7 billion of the same 55 rows in 1931. This is not a complete world GDP total, because many countries lack annual coverage, but it captures the largest economies and the main direction of the shock. The United States remained the largest economy by a wide margin, but its MPD-derived real aggregate fell about 15%, while Germany, France, Poland, Canada, Belgium and other trade-exposed economies also contracted.

The defining forces were deflation, collapsed trade, banking distress and the fragmentation of the gold-exchange standard. Britain and Japan had already left gold in 1931 and showed earlier signs of stabilization or recovery. France, Switzerland, the Netherlands, Belgium and several other gold-bloc countries kept a harder-money stance, which helped preserve parity but intensified deflationary pressure. The United States was still formally tied to gold in 1932, but the Federal Reserve and Congress experimented with emergency credit measures.

Microeconomically, the depression appeared as idle factories, falling farm prices, distressed mortgages, bank failures, depressed construction and a collapse in long-distance trade. Corporate revenue and market-cap rankings for 1932 are not available in reliable global form, so the corporate landscape is treated qualitatively and missing data are flagged. Agriculture and commodity exporters were hit especially hard, while Japan’s export industries and parts of Britain’s domestic housing economy were among the few large-economy bright spots.

The year also marked a policy transition. The Reconstruction Finance Corporation, the U.S. Banking Act of 1932, the Lausanne reparations settlement and the Ottawa imperial preference agreements showed that governments were moving from passive adherence to prewar rules toward emergency intervention, exchange management and trade blocs. In hindsight, 1932 sits just before the more visible reflationary turns of 1933-1934.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1932

1. **Continuing depression and global deflation.** Output fell sharply again in the United States and several other industrial economies, while national turning points differed and the US business-cycle trough came in March 1933. Falling prices raised real debt burdens, weakened banks and discouraged investment. The countries most affected included the United States, Germany, Canada, Poland, Belgium and France. The forward linkage was the policy turn toward reflation, deposit insurance, banking restructuring and managed currencies in 1933-1934.

2. **Fracture of the gold-exchange standard.** Britain, Japan and several sterling-linked economies had left gold, while the United States was still formally tied to gold and the gold bloc defended parity. The result was not one world monetary regime but competing currency blocs. The forward linkage was the U.S. 1933 suspension of gold convertibility and the 1934 Gold Reserve Act.

3. **Protectionism and trade-bloc formation.** The Ottawa Conference of 1932 and continuing tariff and quota measures reinforced the shift from multilateral trade toward managed preference zones. Primary exporters and export manufacturers were most exposed. This shaped the 1930s trade order and weakened recovery in countries dependent on foreign demand.

4. **Banking, liquidity and debt distress.** The aftermath of the 1931 European banking crisis and U.S. bank failures kept credit tight. Standstill agreements, moratoria and transfer restrictions limited international capital flows. The forward linkage was a more state-centered financial architecture, including deposit insurance in the United States, central-bank reform in Canada and industrial-bank rescues in Europe.

5. **Divergent recovery after currency depreciation.** Japan and Britain showed that leaving gold could support recovery through depreciation, lower interest rates and higher domestic prices. Australia also began to stabilize. The gold-bloc economies recovered later, a contrast that became central to later economic-historical interpretations of the Depression.

6. **State intervention moved from emergency to structure.** The USSR already operated a command economy; Italy, Germany, the United States, Brazil and others expanded state roles in credit, industry or trade. In 1932 much of this was still emergency management, but it laid the institutional groundwork for the interventionist 1930s.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

Complete world GDP for 1932 is **Data not available** in a single official source. The MPD 2023 country-coverage aggregate for the 55 non-overlapping country rows with annual data is 5,018.5 billion 2011 international dollars, down 4.48% from 5,253.7 billion for the same rows in 1931; the former USSR, Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia aggregates are not added to their constituent rows. This is a coverage aggregate, not a full world total. World trade volume and value were still depressed; the League of Nations treated the collapse of world trade as a central feature of 1932. The Federico-Tena world export-volume index (1913=100) fell from 108.77 to 95.28 (-12.41%), and nominal world exports at current borders from $18,556.4 million to $12,749.3 million (-31.29%). The League of Nations index of world manufacturing and mining (1925-29=100) fell from 88.1 to 76.9 including the USSR and from 82.2 to 69.3 excluding it. World population, reconstructed on 1991 borders, rose from 2,072.0 million to 2,099.3 million (+1.32%). Reserve-currency dynamics were unstable: sterling floated, the dollar was under pressure, the yen had depreciated, and gold-bloc currencies defended parity. Global inflation was mostly deflation in industrial countries. Oil prices were extremely low by historical standards, gold remained officially $20.67 per troy ounce in the United States, and many agricultural staples traded near depression lows. International capital flows were dominated by repayment, hoarding, standstills and restrictions rather than new lending: new foreign long-term issues in the United States fell from $229 million to $8 million.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

U.S. short rates and the New York Fed discount rate moved down during the year, but credit transmission was impaired. Year-end central-bank discount rates fell from 3.5% to 2.5% in New York, 6% to 2% in London, 7% to 4% in Berlin, 7% to 5% in Rome, 6.57% to 4.38% in Tokyo and 3% to 2.5% in Amsterdam, while Paris (2.5%) and Zurich (2%) were unchanged. The Reconstruction Finance Corporation was authorized on 22 January and the Banking Act of 27 February broadened Federal Reserve collateral and note-backing powers. Britain operated a cheap-money policy after leaving gold, and Japan’s policy under Takahashi was expansionary. Germany and many Central European economies remained constrained by banking damage and exchange controls. The exchange-rate regime was fragmented: no Bretton Woods existed, and the classical gold standard had already broken for much of the world. Equity markets were volatile and deeply depressed; the DJIA touched its Depression-era low in 1932 and ended the year below 1931. Bond markets showed a flight to safe sovereigns in Britain and the United States, while debtor and controlled economies faced segmented markets.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The Ottawa imperial preference agreements were the major trade-policy event; the UK Ottawa Agreements Act received Royal Assent on 15 November, with commencement of the new duties left to Treasury orders. The Lausanne agreement of 9 July provided for ending reparations but had not been ratified by May 1933; transitional provisions kept payments in suspense while service on market loans continued. Japan introduced measures against capital flight from 1 July. Tariffs, quotas, exchange controls and bilateral clearing arrangements substituted for multilateral openness. Japan’s position in Manchuria, unresolved reparations and inter-allied debt issues, and Central European financial weakness all affected trade and capital flows. There was no Cold War, EEC or ASEAN; regional integration took the form of empire preference, sterling-area coordination and defensive blocs.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil and many raw materials were depressed by weak demand and excess capacity. Gold hoarding and movements into central-bank reserves were central monetary phenomena. Agricultural commodities, especially wheat, coffee, sugar and cotton, remained under severe price pressure. Commodity exporters such as Argentina, Canada, Brazil, Australia and the Dutch East Indies faced income compression even where physical output remained significant.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Unemployment was historically high in industrial economies, but cross-country comparability is poor because definitions varied. Rural underemployment in China, India and colonial economies is not captured by modern unemployment concepts. Migration was constrained by quotas, weak labor demand and political barriers. Urbanization continued structurally, but the depression slowed job creation in modern sectors and increased informal and subsistence pressure. In the United States, factory employment fell from 74 to 62 and factory payrolls from 66 to 45 (1923-25=100), so payroll income fell faster than jobs. Reconstructed unemployment rates reached 23.6% in the United States, 19.7% in Australia, 18.50% in Canada, 17.2% in Germany and 17.19% in the Netherlands, but definitions differ across countries.

**Economic narrative:**

The American economy was still contracting sharply. Output, employment, construction, autos, steel and household durables all remained under severe pressure, while deflation raised real debt burdens. The Reconstruction Finance Corporation, authorized on 22 January with its authority expanded on 21 July, became the main federal credit-intervention mechanism, and the Banking Act of 27 February broadened Federal Reserve collateral and note-backing powers; it did not separate commercial from investment banking or insure deposits. Monetary easing in 1932 helped stop part of the contraction, but it did not yet restore normal credit transmission.

The contraction ran through the whole year, and the NBER business-cycle trough came only in March 1933. The League of Nations industrial index fell from 68.1 to 53.8 (1929=100), steel output from 26.362 to 13.600 million tonnes and crude-oil output from 116.682 to 107.190 million tonnes. Federal Reserve Board indices (1923-25=100) show factory employment falling from 74 to 62 and factory payrolls from 66 to 45, freight-car loadings from 75 to 56, department-store sales from 92 to 69, and construction contract awards from 63 to 28, with residential contracts down from 37 to 13. Bank suspensions fell from 2,294 in 1931 to 1,456, and deposits of suspended banks from $1,690.7 million to $715.6 million; the counts include banks that later reopened.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:**

Leading sectors were not growth engines in a modern sense. They were the least damaged pockets: utilities, some food processing and parts of government-supported finance. Contracting sectors included construction, autos, steel, railways, farm machinery and agriculture. Bank balance sheets remained fragile, consumer credit was constrained, and housing activity was deeply depressed. New domestic corporate capital issues fell from $1,551 million to $325 million and foreign issues in the United States from $229 million to $8 million. In the Macrohistory and rate-of-return reconstructions, real consumption per person fell 9.52%, total bank loans 19.12% and mortgage lending 8.37%, and the nominal house-price index fell 17.90%. The Standard Statistics common-stock index (1926=100) averaged 47.4 in December against 57.7 a year earlier. Ford introduced its V8 engine in 1932 in both the United States and Canada.

### 5B. Corporate landscape

Reliable global rankings of the largest companies by revenue or market capitalization for 1932 are **Data not available**. Large and economically important firms or groups included Standard Oil successor companies, General Motors, Ford, U.S. Steel, Royal Dutch/Shell, ICI, IG Farben, Siemens, Krupp, Mitsui, Mitsubishi, major British banks, large railway systems and colonial commodity firms. This list is qualitative and not ranked.

Major corporate and institutional developments were shaped by state finance rather than conventional merger waves. In 1932, the key mechanisms were bank support, public lending, industrial rescue, exchange controls and securities-market regulation. Private investment decisions were strongly conditioned by price expectations, foreign-exchange access and government policy.

Dated corporate events include the formation of Auto Union on 29 June from Audi, Horch and DKW, with a separate purchase and lease of Wanderer's automobile division; the collapse of the Kreuger match and finance group in March, which led the Swedish National Debt Office to support Skandinaviska Banken; and the death of the shoe maker Tomáš Baťa on 12 July. New domestic corporate capital issues in the United States fell from $1,551 million to $325 million, and the nominal share capital of Italian limited companies from 50,853 to 49,602 million lire.

### 5C. Innovation & technology

The depression slowed private R&D spending in many sectors, but technological diffusion continued. Electrification, radio broadcasting, telephone networks, aircraft development, chemicals, steel processes and motor-vehicle engineering remained economically important. Patent and R&D spending data by country for 1932 are **Data not available** in comparable global form. Infrastructure projects mattered more than laboratory breakthroughs for short-run demand, especially in countries using public works. Product development continued: Ford introduced its V8 in 1932 in the United States and Canada, and Datsun models 10 and 11 appeared in Japan. The International Telecommunication Convention was signed in Madrid on 9 December 1932, to enter into force on 1 January 1934.

### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Labor markets were characterized by high unemployment in industrial economies and underemployment in agrarian economies. Skill polarization was visible: engineers, public works employees and selected utility workers were better protected than unskilled industrial labor, while farmers faced low prices and debt. Union conditions differed sharply by country, from relatively open bargaining in Britain and Sweden to state-controlled labor systems in the USSR and Italy; Germany still had independent unions in 1932, and the principal unions were seized only in May 1933. In Japan, December factory employment rose from 73.1 to 77.0 while wage rates fell from 89.5 to 86.6 (1926=100), and Italy's registered unemployed rose from 982,321 to 1,129,654 at year-end. Migration was economically important but restricted by law, weak demand and political barriers.

### 6B. Fixed income

Government-bond data are uneven. U.K. consol yields fell from early-Depression levels, consistent with cheap-money policy; FRED reports an annual U.K. consol yield of 3.7524% for 1932. Long-term government bond yields in the Macrohistory Database, each on its own source definition, fell in the United States from 3.65% to 3.34%, the United Kingdom from 4.53% to 3.76%, Japan from 5.92% to 4.74%, Italy from 6.59% to 6.28%, Spain from 5.53% to 5.03%, the Netherlands from 4.56% to 3.55%, Australia from 6.94% to 5.25% and Switzerland from 3.86% to 3.80%, but rose in Germany from 6.14% to 7.30%, Canada from 4.65% to 5.14%, Belgium from 3.97% to 4.47% and France from 3.80% to 3.91%; these are not a harmonized ten-year panel. Credit-spread data comparable across major economies are **Data not available**.

### 6C. Currencies

Sterling and the yen were off gold, the dollar remained legally tied to gold but under strain, and gold-bloc currencies defended parity. Exchange controls and clearing arrangements proliferated. China’s silver standard insulated it from gold mechanics but left it vulnerable to silver-price movements. On calendar-year averages of New York buying rates, the dollar value of sterling fell 22.69%, the yen 42.54%, the rupee 21.96%, the peseta 15.79%, the Argentine gold peso 12.44% and the Canadian dollar 8.52%, while the gold-bloc currencies were unchanged or slightly firmer and the Shanghai tael fell only 1.51%. The Yokohama Specie Bank's December New York selling rate was $20.75 per 100 yen against $49.375 a year earlier.

### 6D. Alternative assets

Gold was a monetary asset, not a free consumer investment for all countries. In the United States, the official price remained $20.67 per troy ounce in 1932. Real estate was depressed in many U.S. and continental markets but supported by cheap money in parts of Britain and later Sweden. In the Philadelphia Fed historical housing indices, newspaper asking prices fell 16.45% in the New York City market area and 18.82% in the Boston market area, and asking rents fell 9.45% and 9.71%; these are asking prices, not completed sales. Nominal house-price indices in the rate-of-return dataset fell 17.90% in the United States, 15.60% in Germany, 10.00% in the United Kingdom, 9.28% in Belgium, 4.38% in Australia and 1.80% in Switzerland, but rose 4.46% in France; the Amsterdam Herengracht single-street index fell 15.57% and Canada's average replacement value per dwelling 9.35%, neither being a national transaction-price index. Price indexes for other major cities are **Data not available**.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

Key active risks included banking instability, sovereign and private debt standstills, exchange controls, trade fragmentation, commodity-price distress and political radicalization. In 1932, the most immediate black-swan-style risk was a further failure of the U.S. or European banking system and a deeper collapse of trade. The Lausanne settlement of 9 July reduced one source of reparations pressure but had not been ratified by May 1933 and did not restore normal capital markets. The collapse of the Kreuger group in March transmitted a corporate funding shock into Swedish banking, and the National Debt Office supported Skandinaviska Banken. US bank suspensions fell from 2,294 to 1,456 but remained high, and the banking panics of early 1933 lay ahead.

With hindsight, the main policy mistake was the persistence of deflationary exchange-rate commitments and trade barriers in countries where demand had already collapsed. This hindsight label matters: policymakers at the time were also worried about credibility, external balances, gold reserves and social order, not only output stabilization.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1932 vs. 1931)

1932 vs. 1931 was a move from severe depression toward the trough, which in the United States came in March 1933. The MPD coverage aggregate for the same 55 country rows fell another 4.48%. The United States moved from deep contraction (-6.42%) to an even sharper one (-15.07%); Germany and Canada continued to fall; Britain stabilized after leaving gold; Japan accelerated after depreciation; and Australia began to recover. The policy delta was large: emergency lending, open-market purchases, the RFC and imperial preference became more important than orthodox reliance on balanced budgets and gold convertibility. Matched measures show the shift: world export volume fell 12.41% after 11.77% in 1931; year-end discount rates fell in New York, London, Berlin, Rome, Tokyo and Amsterdam while Paris and Zurich were unchanged; sterling and the yen averaged 22.69% and 42.54% less in dollars; and US bank suspensions fell from 2,294 to 1,456.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

Data confidence is highest for broad U.S. and British monetary, price and financial indicators, because central bank, FRED, FRASER, ONS and Bank of England series are relatively accessible. Confidence is moderate for France, Germany, Japan, Canada, Australia, Sweden, the Netherlands, Belgium and Switzerland, but unemployment, fiscal and current-account definitions remain hard to compare. Confidence is lower for China, India, Indonesia, Brazil, Argentina and other developing or colonial economies because national accounts were not compiled in modern form and sector data are incomplete.

The weakest GDP evidence is for the Soviet Union and other non-market economies. MPD values for the USSR should be treated as rough PPP reconstructions with substantial, unquantified uncertainty. China’s GDP is also uncertain because of fragmented administration, limited statistical coverage and the dominance of non-market rural production. Colonial GDP estimates for India and the Dutch East Indies are better than no data but still depend on retrospective reconstruction.

Historical data conflict across sources. MPD 2023 and Maddison 2010 agree on the broad hierarchy of the largest economies but differ in lower-tail top-20 membership and sometimes in country ordering. For 1932, MPD 2023 is the controlling ranking; the older Maddison 2010 series differs around ranks 19-22, placing Sweden inside the top 20 and Switzerland below it. Many indicators, including unemployment, policy rates, current-account balances, budget balances and debt/GDP, are **Data not available** in a harmonized cross-country format for all top-20 economies.

Corporate rankings by revenue or market capitalization, global sector output by country, cross-city real-estate prices, standardized credit spreads, and comparable household debt data for 1932 are **Data not available**. Where the narrative identifies sectors or institutions, it does so qualitatively from historical sources rather than by unsupported numerical ranking.

Indicator sources and definitions. Policy rates are official discount rates in force on 31 December, not annual averages; India, Canada, Argentina and Brazil had no central bank. Exchange rates are calendar-year averages of New York buying rates in US cents per foreign unit as compiled by the League of Nations, which do not show controls or within-year moves. CPI, unemployment, debt and current-account ratios for most advanced economies come from the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database (Release 6), and equity returns and national house prices from its rate-of-return extension; these data are used under the CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 licence. United Kingdom indicators use the Bank of England millennium dataset, and United States CPI and unemployment use the Bureau of Labor Statistics and Lebergott series. Budget balances are Data not available for most economies because central-government fiscal years and coverage are not reconciled with calendar-year nominal GDP; the arithmetic ratios appear only in Notes.

Source conflicts. United Kingdom unemployment is 15.39% in the Bank of England dataset against 15.60% in the Macrohistory Database, and public debt 188.91% against 190.15% of GDP. United States real GDP fell 15.07% in MPD 2023 but 12.90% in the BEA chained-dollar series, and United Kingdom real GDP rose 0.76% in MPD 2023 but 0.07% in the Bank of England series. The Macrohistory debt ratios for France (161.02% in 1931, 103.71% in 1932) and Italy (109.31% and 89.53%) change by far more than repayment could explain, so year-on-year changes are not shown for them. The Macrohistory unemployment entry for Italy is a linked index rather than a rate and is not shown as a rate; French unemployment covers only wage and salary earners in manufacturing, mining and construction; the Belgian external balance is a merchandise trade balance. Japanese equity prices rose 51.60% in the Bank of Japan index but 7.33% in the rate-of-return reconstruction. The German current-account denominator is linked to gross national product growth for this period, not an observed national-accounts level.

GDP growth for the former USSR is withheld because the national-income source behind the MPD record has not been matched to the territory of its population series; the raw record change of -1.09% is shown only in Notes. Levels and growth in this report and the 1931 report come from the same MPD 2023 vintage. National steel and crude-oil output figures for 1932 are provisional League of Nations estimates.
