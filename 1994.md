# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1994

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1994 was the year in which the early-1990s global recovery became broad enough to lift trade, manufacturing, and capital spending, but also the year when financial markets learned that the post-recession low-rate regime was ending. World GDP in current dollars was about $27.95 trillion in the World Bank WITS/WDI summary, and world merchandise export volume growth reached about 9.5 percent according to WTO reporting. Retrospective IMF World Economic Outlook (October 2026) composites put world real growth at 3.14% on PPP weights, up from 1.95% in 1993. The surface story was recovery; the deeper story was regime transition from cheap money to tighter monetary conditions, from GATT to the WTO, and from national development models toward more open, rules-based trade systems.

The most important macro shock was the 1994 bond-market sell-off. The Federal Reserve's tightening cycle, which took the intended federal funds rate from 3% to about 5.5% by year-end, triggered a global repricing of duration risk. Long-term yields rose across major markets, real yields in industrial countries were described by the Bank of England as historically high, and leveraged fixed-income positions suffered: the Macrohistory Database records negative 1994 government bond total returns in all nine of the ten advanced economies it covers with bond data, from -2.5% in Germany to -12.5% in Spain. This mattered because it linked monetary policy in the United States to fiscal sustainability in Canada and Europe, bank fragility in Japan, mortgage and mutual-fund risk in the United States, municipal finance in Orange County, and capital-flow risk in emerging markets.

Trade institutions changed permanently. NAFTA entered into force on 1 January 1994, linking the United States, Canada, and Mexico more tightly, with tariffs eliminated progressively rather than at once. The Uruguay Round was completed with the Marrakesh Agreement on 15 April, creating the WTO effective 1 January 1995. These were not just legal events; they changed corporate location decisions, tariff expectations, dispute settlement, intellectual property rules, and the logic of global supply chains.

Emerging-market and transition stories were sharply divergent. China grew extremely fast but fought consumer-price inflation of about 24% while unifying its exchange-rate system and reforming tax sharing. Brazil launched the Real Plan and moved from hyperinflation toward stabilization. Russia remained in severe post-Soviet contraction, and Turkey contracted after an early-year currency and financing crisis. Mexico ended the year in a peso crisis, showing that liberalization, capital inflows, and exchange-rate credibility could reverse quickly. In hindsight, 1994 foreshadowed the emerging-market crises of the late 1990s, but at the time many investors still interpreted Mexico as an isolated political and policy shock rather than a broader balance-sheet warning.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1994

**1. Global recovery with a trade boom.** The recovery from the early-1990s slowdown became synchronized enough to produce a very large rebound in merchandise trade volumes. Europe and Japan were emerging from weakness, the United States was expanding strongly, and East Asia was still in a high-growth phase. WTO data record merchandise export volume growth of 9.5% and a merchandise export value of $4,090 billion (+13%). The forward linkage was direct: stronger trade in 1994 helped create optimism about globalization just as the WTO was being born.

**2. End of the easy-money interlude.** The United States began a forceful tightening cycle, and global bond markets sold off. This was historically important because it exposed duration risk in supposedly safe portfolios and reminded fiscal authorities that disinflation did not eliminate market discipline. The countries most affected were the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, continental European bond markets, and emerging markets dependent on foreign capital. The United Kingdom and Australia also tightened during the year, while the Bundesbank continued to ease its official rates.

**3. Trade architecture shifted from regional and GATT-based rules toward deeper integration.** NAFTA's implementation and the Marrakesh Agreement changed the institutional setting for firms. North America's autos, electronics, agriculture, and cross-border logistics sectors were directly affected. The WTO's 1995 launch gave the 1994 agreement forward significance beyond the year itself.

**4. Emerging-market credibility became a central macro variable.** Mexico's December devaluation showed that a country could have reform credentials, trade liberalization, and investor enthusiasm yet still face a sudden stop if external deficits, short-term liabilities, and exchange-rate expectations aligned badly. Argentina, Brazil, and other capital-flow-dependent economies absorbed the warning through risk spreads and confidence channels.

**5. Stabilization and reform in large developing economies.** Brazil's Real Plan and China's 1994 exchange-rate and tax reforms were among the most consequential domestic policy changes of the year. Brazil attacked inertial inflation and changed household price expectations. China improved export incentives and central fiscal capacity. These reforms shaped the second half of the 1990s more than many market events that received more immediate attention.

**6. Europe recovered but did not solve unemployment.** Germany, France, Spain, Belgium, Italy, and the Netherlands generally improved from the early-1990s downturn, but unemployment remained high and often structurally persistent; Spain's rate was about 24%. Maastricht convergence, ERM credibility, and fiscal consolidation limited demand policy. This linked 1994 directly to the later politics of monetary union and labor-market reform.

**7. Japan's post-bubble weakness became a long-run constraint.** Japan remained huge in nominal dollars, but its real domestic momentum was weak. Bank balance sheets, property collateral, and cautious households kept the recovery fragile. Yen strength reinforced outward investment by Japanese manufacturers, helping East Asian supply chains while weakening domestic Japanese demand.

**8. Transition-economy uncertainty remained extreme.** Russia's GDP and price data were difficult to compare with market economies. Output was still contracting, inflation was very high, and privatization was transforming ownership faster than institutions could stabilize governance. Nominal dollar rankings for Russia in 1994 should be treated as rough approximations.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

World GDP in current US dollars was about $27.95 trillion in the World Bank WITS world summary for 1994. The current World Bank WDI vintage gives $28.066 trillion (1993: $26.071 trillion), with world real growth of 3.416% (1993: 1.858%). Real world output growth was in recovery after weak 1993 performance, while merchandise trade grew much faster than output; World Bank reporting described world trade volume growth as more than doubling from the prior year. WTO reporting (press release of 2 November 1995) placed 1994 merchandise export volume growth at 9.5 percent, merchandise export value at $4,090 billion (+13%) and commercial-services exports at $1,100 billion (+8%); the first is a volume measure and the other two are current-dollar values. The same release's 8% goods-volume figure is a forecast for 1995, not a 1994 outcome. The trade-to-GDP ratio in the WITS world summary was 41.26 percent.

Reserve-currency dynamics still centered on the US dollar. Inflation patterns diverged. Advanced economies generally had low or falling inflation compared with the 1970s and 1980s, while China (24.3% WDI annual-average CPI), Brazil (2,075.9%), Russia (307.7%), and Turkey (105.2%) still faced high or extreme inflation. Brazil's Real Plan and Russia's stabilization attempts illustrate how much inflation experience differed across the major economies.

Commodity conditions were generally disinflationary rather than crisis-like. US crude oil first-purchase prices in the EIA series were in the low-to-mid teens per barrel during 1994, with monthly observations rising from roughly $10.49 in January to a mid-year peak above $15 before ending near $13.43 in December; the annual average was $13.19, down 7.4% from $14.25 in 1993. International capital flows favored reforming and high-growth economies until confidence turned late in the year. Mexico's crisis revealed the risk in short-term foreign financing; short-term portfolio financing proved reversible, while longer-lived direct investment involved different withdrawal and liquidity mechanisms. East Asia continued to attract investment through manufacturing growth, while Japan's yen strength encouraged outward direct investment. Russia attracted opportunistic capital but remained institutionally risky. ### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The Federal Reserve's tightening cycle defined the global monetary year. The intended federal funds rate ended near 5.5%, as confirmed in the 20 December FOMC record, against 3% at the end of 1993; the 15 November action separately raised the discount rate from 4% to 4.75%. Long-term yields repriced, and the bond sell-off spread internationally. The BIS later treated the 1994 bond turbulence as a benchmark event for market-risk analysis. Its 65th Annual Report shows that in the four days after the first 25-basis-point US tightening on 4 February, ten-year government yields rose by about 11 basis points in the United States, 21 in Germany, 11 in France, 25 in the United Kingdom and 16 in the Netherlands, while Japanese yields fell by about 7. The shock mattered because bond portfolios had been positioned for low inflation and low rates after the early-1990s recession; tightening and duration losses could occur with low realized inflation because expected future short rates, term premiums and leveraged positions also affect prices.

Japan remained in an ultra-low-rate environment by the standards of the time: the Bank of Japan's official discount rate stayed at 1.75%, the level set on 21 September 1993, until its next change in 1995, although an unchanged discount rate did not mean that all Japanese lending rates or credit conditions were unchanged. Germany and the hard-currency European bloc were easing from prior anti-inflation settings: Bundesbank discount/Lombard rates were cut to 5.25%/6.75% effective 18 February, 5%/6.5% on 15 April and 4.5%/6% on 13 May, and cash advances ceased from 1 January 1994. The Bundesbank's credibility still constrained other European central banks. The United Kingdom, outside the ERM after 1992, had more freedom and began tightening as recovery strengthened: the Bank of England's historical official Bank Rate table records 5.13% on 8 February, 5.63% on 12 September and 6.13% on 7 December, a different series from the clearing-bank base rate usually quoted as 5.25%, 5.75% and 6.25%. Australia's Reserve Bank raised its cash-rate objective on 17 August, 24 October and 14 December, to around 7.5%. Many emerging markets faced a different problem: domestic stabilization required high rates, while global dollar tightening made external financing more fragile.

Exchange-rate regimes were mixed. Major advanced currencies floated, but European currencies operated under the widened ERM bands left after the 1992-93 turmoil. China unified its exchange-rate system in January 1994. Argentina operated a currency-board-style one-to-one peso-dollar regime. Mexico maintained a band until the December devaluation and crisis. Brazil introduced a new currency under the Real Plan.

Stock markets were mixed and less central than bonds to the 1994 global story. Markets exposed to rising long yields, banking weakness, or emerging-market outflows were pressured, while some recovery-sensitive sectors benefited from stronger real activity. The Macrohistory Database records equity price falls of about 17% in France, 12% in Spain and Australia, 10% in the United Kingdom and 9% in Germany, against gains in Japan and Italy (section 6A).

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

NAFTA entered into force on 1 January 1994. The agreement immediately changed expectations for tariff schedules, investment, and cross-border supply chains among the United States, Canada, and Mexico, although implementation was not an instantaneous elimination of every tariff: USTR describes progressive tariff elimination together with rules of origin, investment, services and dispute-settlement provisions. Its long-run impact was especially important in autos, electronics, agriculture, logistics, and border-region manufacturing.

The Uruguay Round culminated in the Marrakesh Agreement on 15 April 1994, creating the WTO from 1 January 1995 (UN treaty registration 31874, registered on 1 June 1995). Signature, national ratification, entry into force and tariff staging are separate dates. This shifted global trade governance from the older GATT-centered system toward a broader rules framework covering goods, services, intellectual property, dispute settlement, and agriculture more extensively than before. For firms, the mechanism was a change in expected market access and sourcing rules rather than an immediately measured productivity gain, and political and security shocks could still overwhelm near-term financing conditions despite trade integration, as Mexico showed.

The Cold War was over, but its economic consequences were still present. Russia and Eastern Europe were restructuring state-owned industry and fiscal systems. Defense spending adjustments affected parts of the United States and Europe. The European Union was moving toward deeper integration and eventual monetary union, while Austria, Finland, and Sweden were preparing for EU accession in 1995.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil prices were low by later standards and did not dominate the macro year in the way interest rates did. OPEC remained relevant, but the decisive story was demand recovery and subdued price pressure rather than a supply shock. Low energy costs helped advanced-economy disinflation and supported consumers, while oil exporters faced tighter revenue conditions than in high-price eras.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor-market outcomes were sharply uneven. The United States (6.1% unemployment), Canada (10.4%), the United Kingdom (9.5%), and Australia (9.7%) saw recovery reduce unemployment, although slack remained. Continental Europe had growth but persistent high unemployment, rising in Germany, France, Italy, Spain and the Netherlands in IMF WEO data. Japan's unemployment, 2.9%, was low by international standards but high relative to its own postwar history. China and India had vast informal and rural labor markets that make headline unemployment rates misleading; China's registered urban measures cannot stand in for its rural and informal labor force.

Demographic forces were long-run rather than sudden. China's urban migration and coastal industrialization were changing labor allocation. India's young labor force created growth potential but also pressure for job creation. Europe and Japan were already facing aging trends, though the full fiscal implications were more visible in hindsight. Wage-productivity dynamics differed: US and UK labor markets became more flexible, Germany and France protected wage structures more strongly, and East Asian economies combined high investment with rising industrial wages.

China's national price table illustrates the distributional side of inflation: consumer prices rose 24.1%, food prices 31.8%, retail prices 21.7% and factory-gate industrial prices 19.5%. Higher food inflation weighed most on low-income budgets, although an exact household welfare loss cannot be derived without expenditure weights and nominal income. **Economic narrative:**

The United States was the anchor of the 1994 expansion. Output growth accelerated, unemployment fell, and the recovery that had begun earlier in the decade became more self-sustaining. The important policy break was monetary: the Federal Reserve moved from an exceptionally easy post-recession stance into a sharp tightening cycle. That shift did not stop real activity in 1994, but it repriced bonds worldwide and changed the discount-rate environment for banks, mortgage investors, pension funds, and leveraged fixed-income strategies.

Microeconomically, US growth was broad. Consumer durables, housing-related finance, computers, software, and business services were stronger than the recession years. Manufacturing benefited from domestic demand and North American integration, while defense-related regions were still adjusting to post-Cold War procurement cuts. Household borrowing and mutual-fund participation increased the transmission of interest-rate shocks from Wall Street into household balance sheets.

Rising rates raised funding costs and reduced long-duration asset values. The contemporaneous FOMC record and later SEC findings show the same mechanism from different angles: Orange County's leveraged investment pool showed that public-sector portfolios were exposed too. This does not mean that every bank's lending contracted, and a nearly flat annual equity index did not mean an absence of financial stress.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Leading sectors: technology hardware and software, financial services, autos, retail, business services. Contracting or weak areas: some defense suppliers, interest-rate-sensitive mortgage portfolios. Notable mechanisms: mutual funds and mortgage-backed securities transmitted bond losses to households and institutions. Orange County disclosed about $1.5 billion of paper losses on a $20.6 billion pool on 1 December, and the county and its pools filed Chapter 9 petitions on 6 December. Intel's 1994 annual report shows a $475 million pretax fourth-quarter charge for replacing and writing off flawed Pentium processors, alongside revenue of $11,521 million (+31.2%) and R&D of $1,111 million (+14.5%). The Macrohistory Database national house-price index rose 2.6% in nominal terms.

**Economic narrative:**

Japan remained the second-largest economy in nominal dollars, helped by yen strength as much as by domestic output. The core story was post-bubble stagnation: banks were still burdened by bad loans and collateral damage from the late-1980s asset bubble, while fiscal and monetary policy attempted to prevent outright deflationary contraction. The strong yen created pressure on exporters and encouraged more overseas production, especially in East and Southeast Asia.

At the micro level, globally competitive manufacturers still had deep technological and process advantages, but the domestic property, banking, and construction complex was weak. Personal computers, enterprise software, semiconductors, mobile telephony, and data networking were moving from specialist markets toward mainstream business investment. ### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Service-sector employment continued to gain share in advanced economies, while manufacturing remained central to Germany, Japan, Korea, Taiwan, Mexico, and parts of the United States. China and India still had large agricultural and informal labor forces, so productivity gains depended on moving workers into higher-value urban or industrial activity. Europe's high unemployment showed that macro recovery alone did not guarantee labor absorption: IMF WEO unemployment rose in 1994 in Germany (8.4%), France (10.7%), Italy (10.6%), Spain (24.1%) and the Netherlands (6.2%) even as output grew.

Wage dynamics were definition-sensitive. The United States and United Kingdom had more flexible labor markets but also stronger inequality concerns. Continental Europe had more institutional wage protection but higher unemployment. East Asia combined rapid productivity growth with rising industrial wages. Brazil's stabilization changed wage bargaining by reducing the inflation-indexation problem, while Russia's inflation and arrears damaged real wage reliability. In Mexico, the BIS records manufacturing restructuring with footwear and textiles weakening and machinery and equipment expanding; its productivity figures refer to manufacturing, not the whole economy.

### 6B. Fixed income

Fixed income was the core financial story. The BIS analysis of 1994 bond turbulence describes a sharp rise in bond-yield volatility across major bond markets. The Bank of England's November 1994 Quarterly Bulletin described real government bond yields in major industrial countries as historically high. The repricing created capital losses for holders of long-duration debt and changed the risk calculus for governments with large deficits. The US ten-year Treasury yield averaged about 7.08% over the year (mean of monthly H.15 averages). Bond yields are not total returns, and the Macrohistory Database long-rate proxies in section 6A are not necessarily matched constant-maturity ten-year yields; duration losses could occur while coupon income remained positive.

The BIS measured the immediate response to the first US tightening on 4 February: in the following four days, ten-year yields rose by about 11 basis points in the United States, 21 in Germany, 11 in France, 25 in the United Kingdom and 16 in the Netherlands, and fell by about 7 in Japan. These are short-window responses that may also reflect other news, not annual changes or an isolated causal effect; the BIS offers complementary explanations involving fundamentals, risk premiums and market dynamics.

Sovereign-debt events were not limited to formal defaults. Canada and Italy faced market scrutiny because of fiscal positions. Mexico's crisis quickly became an external-financing and sovereign-support issue without a 1994 outright default: dollar indexation protected tesobono investors from peso depreciation only conditional on payment and conversion, and it raised the government's domestic-currency liability as the peso weakened, while short maturities turned the stock of debt into immediate financing needs. Russia's fiscal weakness and inflation limited confidence even before later debt events. ### 6C. Currencies

The dollar remained the central reserve and funding currency, but the yen and Deutschmark were crucial to 1994 currency dynamics. Yen appreciation, to an annual average of 102.2 per dollar from 111.2, hurt Japanese exporters and encouraged outward investment. The Deutschmark anchored the hard-currency European bloc. Sterling floated after its 1992 ERM exit. China unified exchange rates in January. Brazil introduced the real in July. Mexico's December peso devaluation and subsequent crisis were the year's major currency shock.

Quote direction matters: when the rate is quoted in pesos per dollar, depreciation raises the quote, and a 15% rise in a band ceiling is not exactly a 15% fall in the peso's dollar value. Band limits, interbank spot rates, official annual averages and investor returns are distinct measurements. The 30 December FOMC telephone vote temporarily expanded the Federal Reserve's swap line with Mexico, conditional on appropriate terms; an available line is not a drawing. The GAO dates activation and drawings to early January 1995 and the $48.8 billion multilateral support announcement to 31 January 1995, with IMF approval of up to $17.8 billion on 1 February; the initial 12 January proposal of up to $40 billion in US guarantees was not the final agreed programme. None of these amounts was 1994 cash.

Capital controls and administrative regimes remained important. China still managed cross-border finance, India was liberalizing gradually, Argentina used convertibility, and Russia's institutions were in transition. Currency data in 1994 must therefore be read alongside regime context, not as simple free-market price series.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The active crisis of the year was Mexico's peso shock. It began with the December devaluation and quickly became a confidence crisis involving exchange-rate expectations, short-term liabilities, and foreign support. The immediate full macro damage occurred in 1995, but the vulnerability was built during 1994: the current-account deficit was between 5.4% (WDI) and 7.8% (IMF) of GDP, and the GAO compares about $10 billion of maturities over the following three months with around $6 billion of reserves in early January 1995 (figures from different dates, so not an exact coverage ratio).

The bond-market sell-off was the systemic financial shock. It did not become a banking crisis in the largest advanced economies, but it revealed how much global portfolios depended on a low-rate assumption. With hindsight, it was an early stress test for market-risk management, derivatives exposure, mutual funds, and duration-heavy institutions; Orange County's bankruptcy showed that public-sector investment pools were exposed as well.

Russia remained a transition crisis rather than a single-event shock. Output collapse, inflation, weak tax collection, and privatization under fragile institutions created risks that were visible at the time. The later 1998 crisis was not inevitable in every detail, but the fiscal and monetary weaknesses were already evident. Turkey's early-1994 currency and financing crisis, with output contraction in both the Maddison and IMF series and inflation above 100%, was a further counterexample to an unqualified global-recovery story; the BIS reports a $10 billion reduction in its trade deficit after depreciation and tightening, as exports expanded and imports contracted.

Japan's banking problem was a slow-burning vulnerability. The bad-loan issue was visible, but the length of stagnation was easier to understand in hindsight. Europe's unemployment problem was also a slow-burn risk: 1994 recovery did not remove the political and fiscal strains caused by persistent joblessness.

Brazil's Real Plan was a positive shock, but it created new risks. Price stability improved welfare and planning, yet high real interest rates, exchange-rate management, and fiscal pressures would become central issues in later years. China's 1994 reforms strengthened long-run capacity but also made inflation control and regional fiscal balances immediate challenges.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1994 vs. 1993)

The largest global change from 1993 to 1994 was the trade rebound. GATT/WTO reporting for 1993 described slow merchandise trade growth, while 1994 produced a very strong expansion: 9.5% merchandise export volume growth in WTO data and 9.5% goods-and-services volume growth in the IMF composite, against 2.7% in 1993. This was a structural acceleration because it coincided with trade-rule reform and with stronger US, European, and East Asian demand. Goods-trade comparisons should use matched GATT/WTO vintages, since the IMF composite answers a different question.

The second break was monetary. 1993 was still a low-rate, recovery-support year in many investor assumptions. 1994 became the year of higher yields and bond losses, with the intended federal funds rate up 2.5 points, UK Bank Rate up 0.75 point and the Australian cash rate up 2.75 points, while German official rates fell 1.25 points. Rising international bond yields despite easing German policy show the need to separate local instruments from global portfolio repricing. This shifted the global policy conversation from recession risk toward inflation control, fiscal credibility, and financial-market risk management.

The third break was institutional. NAFTA moved from agreement to implementation. The Uruguay Round moved from negotiation to signed institutional architecture, although the WTO's legal entry into force belongs to 1995. For firms, this meant that tariff schedules, rules of origin, dispute settlement, and cross-border investment planning became more concrete.

The fourth break was emerging-market risk perception. In early 1994, reform narratives dominated Mexico, Brazil, China, Argentina, and many other markets. By year-end, the Mexican peso crisis forced investors to distinguish reform credibility from external balance-sheet strength. That distinction became central to the Asian and Russian crises later in the decade. Growth comparisons illustrate the uncertainty: China decelerated from 9.884% to 7.576% in the Maddison series and from 13.936% to 13.102% in the IMF series, agreeing in direction but not in level, while Mexico's Maddison growth of 5.149% compares with 4.394% in the IMF and WDI series.

The fifth break was domestic stabilization in Brazil and structural reform in China. Brazil changed the nominal unit in which households and firms made decisions. China changed the exchange-rate and fiscal architecture of its high-growth model. These changes were more durable than many short-run cyclical indicators.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

GDP rankings have medium-to-high confidence for advanced economies and lower confidence for transition or special-coverage cases. The IMF WEO current-dollar ranking places the United States, Japan, Germany, France, the United Kingdom, and Italy clearly at the top. World Bank WITS/WDI figures broadly confirm the scale of the largest economies but differ for some countries. Japan's WITS GDP figure is lower than the IMF figure. Brazil, Russia, Australia, and Belgium also show meaningful source differences. These are not necessarily errors; they may reflect exchange-rate treatment, database revisions, national-account vintages, and source coverage. Mexico's IMF and WDI GDP levels are essentially identical.

**Vintage differences.** The world GDP level in the text ($27.95 trillion) is the World Bank WITS world-summary figure; the current WDI vintage gives $28.066 trillion and the IMF $28,183.161 billion. Population levels are the rounded estimates used for the ranking; the Maddison Project Database 2023 population denominators differ, notably for India (911.4 million against 945.0 million), France (59.2 against 57.8), Brazil (161.5 against 159.4), Mexico (90.8 against 88.9), China (1,190.0 against 1,198.5), Argentina (34.8 against 34.0) and Spain (39.7 against 39.1). Maddison growth is a long-run real reconstruction in 2011 international dollars, not a 1994 national-accounts release, and its levels are not comparable with the current-dollar levels in the ranking.

**Material conflicts.** China's growth is 7.576% in Maddison, 13.102% in the IMF series and 11.8% in the provisional NBS communiqué. Mexico's current-account deficit is 5.358% of GDP in WDI, 7.751% in the IMF series and about 8% in contemporary BIS and Federal Reserve accounts; with GDP almost identical, the difference lies in the numerator (IMF -$42.911 billion against WDI -$29.662 billion), and the central-bank vintage behind it is unreconciled. The BIS gives Mexican saving at roughly 15% of GDP in one chapter and below 14% in another, so no saving-rate headline is shown. Brazil's 909.6% IGP-DI inflation in its sovereign filing is a general price index, not annual-average CPI (2,075.9%). The BIS annual report refers to the July 1994 Brazilian currency as the "cruzeiro real", whereas the central bank and sovereign filings identify it as the real, which replaced the cruzeiro real. UK Bank Rate (6.13%) and the clearing-bank base rate (6.25%) are different instruments. Russia's contraction is -10.709% in Maddison against -12.7% in the IMF series. Agreement between WDI and IMF series is not independent corroboration, since both draw on national sources.

Taiwan requires special caution because it is not represented in all international databases in the same way as UN member states. The figure used here follows IMF WEO and secondary historical series, and its placement should be read separately from standard WDI country coverage. Switzerland, Taiwan and Belgium carry rounded estimates rather than the sourced series used for the other seventeen economies.

Unemployment has low comparability. OECD countries have more reliable labor-force survey data, though definitions still vary. China's registered urban unemployment, India's labor-market measures, Russia's transition-era labor statistics, and Mexico's narrow urban unemployment are not comparable to US or European unemployment rates.

Fiscal data have major gaps. Government debt and budget balances depend on central versus general government, cash versus accrual, treatment of public enterprises, fiscal years and later revisions: IMF ratios for India follow an April-March fiscal year, Mexico's perimeter includes public corporations, and US, Korean, Mexican and Russian ratios are WDI central-government series. Ratios use their own source GDP denominators, never Maddison PPP output. Current-account balances are WDI or IMF balance-of-payments ratios; WITS merchandise trade balances appear only in Notes and are not substitutes. The Macrohistory Database Canadian current-account series has an unresolved currency-unit documentation issue and is not used. WDI exchange rates for Brazil, Russia and Turkey are in later redenominated units and are not shown as 1994 quotations.

The strongest claims in this document are institutional dates, the broad GDP ranking, the WTO trade-volume statement, NAFTA's entry into force, Brazil's Real Plan timing, China's 1994 exchange-rate and tax reforms, the Mexican peso devaluation, dated central-bank rate actions, and the existence of the 1994 bond-market sell-off. The weakest are rounded indicators for Switzerland, Taiwan and Belgium, Chinese and Russian real growth, and the sector matrix, which is qualitative. Remaining gaps include all-exchange index levels and exact benchmark returns, global company rankings, M&A and IPO data, city property samples, global staple prices, world oil output, reserve-currency shares, net capital-flow totals, skill wages, migration, R&D and patent panels, credit spreads, and policy-rate endpoints for most economies other than the United States, Japan, Germany, the United Kingdom and Australia. 'Data not available' means that no defensible matched figure is shown, not that no statistic exists. Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database values are used under the CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 license.
