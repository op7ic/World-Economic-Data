# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1955

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1955 was a high-growth year in the postwar world economy. An indicative sum of Maddison Project Database 2023 country estimates puts world output at roughly 10.84 trillion 2011 international dollars, about 6.6 percent above 1954; this is an indicative sum of country records, not a certified world total. It is not a 1955 current-dollar estimate. It is a real purchasing-power-parity historical reconstruction. The strongest common pattern was synchronized industrial recovery: the United States rebounded from the 1953-1954 downturn, Western Europe expanded at exceptional speed, Japan accelerated, and the Soviet bloc continued heavy-industrial growth under planning.

The macroeconomic regime was still Bretton Woods. The dollar was tied to gold at $35 per troy ounce, most major currencies were pegged or tightly managed, capital controls were common, and external balance constrained domestic policy. The year therefore combined rapid growth with tightening. The U.S. Federal Reserve raised discount rates, the Bank of England maintained a high Bank Rate, and several European countries used monetary or credit restraint to prevent trade and reserve stress. Inflation was moderate in most advanced economies, but it was severe in several developing economies, including Brazil, Indonesia, Mexico, Chile, Bolivia, Korea, Paraguay, Turkey, Nicaragua, Thailand, and Burma in IMF reporting.

Monetary tightening was neither universal nor uniform. U.S., British, West German, Japanese and Swedish official discount rates rose, but Brazil's fell and Canada's first fell and then rose. Canada operated a floating exchange rate. Official parities, parallel quotations and multiple-rate import systems must be kept distinct, particularly in Japan, Argentina, Indonesia and Spain. Trade data are firmer than output aggregates: the Bank for International Settlements gives export-volume indices of 105 in 1954 and 113 in 1955, implying 7.62% growth (8% in its rounded preliminary statement), with coverage that excludes the Communist-area economies.

Microeconomically, 1955 was a year of visible scale. The first Fortune 500 list appeared, the mass automobile and consumer-durables economy deepened in North America, Japan introduced the Sony TR-55 transistor radio, and franchising and leisure services gained symbolic milestones through McDonald's and Disneyland. Heavy industry still dominated much of Europe and the socialist world, while agriculture remained central in India, China, Indonesia, and other low-income economies. Vehicle finance, housing construction and electronics linked household demand to investment, and Banco de México's annual report gives particularly detailed figures on manufacturing, employment, banking and reserves. Incompatible definitions remain the main caution: Zurich rent growth, the U.S. current-account classification and China's competing output reconstructions all show source conflicts that are kept visible here.

Geopolitically, the world economy was segmented. The Warsaw Pact formalized the Eastern bloc, Bandung emphasized Asian-African sovereignty and development, and European integration moved forward through the Messina process and the European Monetary Agreement. The core surprise of 1955 was not a crisis but the speed of expansion itself: the global system delivered record production and trade, yet the same speed revealed balance-of-payments, inflation, and credit-cycle vulnerabilities that would shape the later 1950s.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1955

**Theme 1: Postwar industrial boom reached record levels**

**Significance in economic history:** 1955 belongs to the high-growth phase often called the postwar golden age in Western economies. The year showed how reconstruction, productivity catch-up, consumer demand, and fixed exchange rates could coexist when reserves and trade were sufficiently supportive.

**Specific data/events in 1955:** The indicative Maddison-based country sum rose about 6.6 percent. BIS described 1955 as a year of remarkable achievement in production, trade, employment, and living standards. The United States grew more than 7 percent in this reconstruction, Germany nearly 11 percent, Japan about 8.6 percent, and Canada about 9.3 percent.

**Countries most affected:** United States, West Germany, Japan, Canada, France, Italy, Netherlands, Belgium, Sweden.

**Forward linkage:** The same growth mechanisms supported the late-1950s consumer boom and European economic integration, but they also required credit restraint and reserve management.

**Theme 2: Bretton Woods discipline shaped domestic policy**

**Significance in economic history:** Under Bretton Woods, domestic full-employment policies had to be reconciled with fixed or managed exchange rates, reserve adequacy, and capital controls. 1955 demonstrates that fixed exchange rates did not eliminate policy autonomy, but they narrowed the room for error.

**Specific data/events in 1955:** The U.S. remained the reserve anchor, with gold fixed at $35 per ounce. Official reserves outside the United States and Soviet area rose strongly from 1953 to 1955, but reserve growth did not fully match trade expansion. The Federal Reserve tightened in stages, while the Bank of England kept Bank Rate elevated.

**Countries most affected:** United States, United Kingdom, Western Europe, Japan, Canada, sterling-area economies.

**Forward linkage:** External-balance pressure remained one reason for stop-go policy in the UK and for European moves toward payment liberalization and eventually deeper integration.

**Theme 3: Developmental states and import substitution gained momentum**

**Significance in economic history:** Many postcolonial or developing economies were not simply passive commodity exporters. They were experimenting with planning, import substitution, public enterprises, and exchange controls.

**Specific data/events in 1955:** India was finishing its First Five-Year Plan. Brazil continued import-substitution industrialization and prepared for the Kubitschek investment push. Mexico grew quickly after the 1954 devaluation. Indonesia struggled with inflation and state-building. Bandung placed economic development and sovereignty at the center of Asian-African diplomacy.

**Countries most affected:** India, Brazil, Mexico, Indonesia, China, Argentina, Egypt and other Bandung participants outside the top 20.

**Forward linkage:** These strategies influenced the 1960s development model, including industrial protection, state energy companies, public infrastructure, and foreign-exchange allocation.

**Theme 4: Socialist bloc growth was large, real, and difficult to measure**

**Significance in economic history:** The USSR, China, and Poland appear in the top 20, but their GDP figures are not equivalent to market-economy national accounts. Prices, exchange rates, investment allocation, and unemployment were administered.

**Specific data/events in 1955:** The former-USSR aggregate ranked second in this reconstruction, China third, and Poland seventeenth. The Warsaw Pact was created in May 1955. China remained in the First Five-Year Plan, and Poland continued heavy-industrial planning.

**Countries most affected:** USSR, China, Poland, East Germany, Czechoslovakia, other Eastern European economies.

**Forward linkage:** Strong heavy-industry growth created impressive aggregate output but also consumer shortages, agricultural weakness, and distorted productivity signals.

**Theme 5: Corporate scale, consumer services, and technology became more visible**

**Significance in economic history:** The 1950s were not only about steel and coal. They were also the decade in which mass retail, franchising, consumer electronics, leisure, advertising, and branded goods became major economic mechanisms.

**Specific data/events in 1955:** Fortune published the first Fortune 500. McDonald's System began under Ray Kroc. Disneyland opened. Sony introduced the TR-55 transistor radio. U.S. equity markets rose strongly, and Japan's stock market advanced as well.

**Countries most affected:** United States, Japan, Canada, Western Europe.

**Forward linkage:** These developments foreshadowed the 1960s consumer economy and the later rise of Japanese electronics, global franchising, and corporate rankings as a way to measure industrial capitalism.

**Theme 6: Commodity stability hid uneven producer-country stress**

**Significance in economic history:** Oil and gold were stable in headline price terms, but many primary producers faced balance-of-payments and inflation pressure.

**Specific data/events in 1955:** Oil remained in the early postwar fixed-price range of about $1.93 per barrel in historical crude-oil series. Gold stayed fixed at $35 per ounce. Copper prices were strong, with U.S. historical series showing about 37.5 cents per pound for 1955. IMF reporting noted deterioration in primary producers' external positions in both 1954 and 1955.

**Countries most affected:** Brazil, Indonesia, Mexico, Argentina, Canada, Australia, South Africa, Venezuela and other commodity exporters.

**Forward linkage:** The uneven commodity environment shaped development finance, import controls, and later debates over commodity stabilization and terms of trade.

**Theme 7: Expansion tested capacity**

Contemporary industrial and trade records support a vigorous recovery, especially in North America and Western Europe. This matters historically because reconstruction increasingly gave way to capacity and labor constraints. Its forward implication was greater reliance on productivity rather than unlimited rebuilding demand, not a guaranteed continuation of 1955 growth.

**Theme 8: Credit policy became more selective**

Official discount changes and the Federal Reserve's discussion of household finance show intervention in a boom, not merely a response to crisis. Countries differed in their instruments and timing, and the increases of 1956 belong to the following year.

**Theme 9: External adjustment followed multiple routes**

Canada floated; Argentina and Indonesia altered multiple-rate systems; the UK tightened while maintaining sterling's parity. These alternatives affected household imports and investment differently. Bretton Woods was a framework, not a universal single-rate regime.

**Theme 10: Development depended on institutions and imported equipment**

India's conversion of the Imperial Bank into the State Bank of India and Mexico's credit allocation supply concrete mechanisms beyond broad planning labels. Their effects on inclusion, productivity and welfare are not quantified.

**Theme 11: Consumer technology and finance expanded together**

Transistor radios and automobiles illustrate product and process innovation; consumer instalment finance spread purchasing costs over future income. Neither an invention date nor higher lending proves aggregate productivity growth.

**Theme 12: Geopolitical integration remained segmented**

Bandung, the Warsaw Pact and the Messina process shaped trade and political cooperation. The European Monetary Agreement was adopted in 1955, but it did not imply immediate universal currency convertibility. Later institutions, including the EEC and OPEC, are not backdated into this year.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

**United States:** The Federal Reserve tightened as output rebounded. The discount rate moved from 1.5 percent at the start of 1955 to about 2.5 percent by late 1955. Equity markets were strong, with the S&P predecessor index (the S&P 500 began in 1957) returning about 26.4 percent and the Dow Jones Industrial Average about 20.8 percent.

**United Kingdom:** The Bank of England Bank Rate was around 4.5 percent for much of 1955. The UK used monetary restraint to protect sterling and manage demand. This was a classic stop-go environment: growth was desired, but imports, reserves, and sterling confidence constrained policy.

**Germany and Western Europe:** There was no Eurozone. West Germany and other Western European economies used national central banks, exchange controls, and the European Payments Union framework. Credit discipline was important because rapid growth generated imports and reserve pressure. The European Monetary Agreement was adopted in 1955 as part of the transition toward more liberal payments.

**Japan:** Japan's fixed yen-dollar setting, export recovery, and bank-credit system supported high growth. IMF reporting described greater price stability and reduced bank indebtedness to the central bank compared with earlier conditions. The Bank of Japan's official discount rate moved from 5.84% to 7.30% on 10 August.

**Exchange-rate regime:** Bretton Woods was fully in place. Major currencies were pegged or tightly managed, gold remained fixed in dollars, and capital controls were widespread. Canada was the main exception among the advanced economies, with a floating dollar. Non-market economies used administrative rates that are not comparable with market exchange rates.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

1955 was economically shaped by the Cold War. The Warsaw Pact formalized Soviet-bloc alignment. The Bandung Conference gave political expression to Asian-African economic sovereignty, development needs, and nonalignment. These events mattered because trade, aid, technology transfer, and capital flows were often filtered through bloc politics; their economic significance lay in the organization of security, aid and development priorities, not in a measured single-year trade effect.

In Western Europe, integration advanced. The Messina process in 1955 set the stage for the Treaties of Rome in 1957. The European Payments Union was still central to payments clearing, and the European Monetary Agreement was adopted on 29 July 1955 for the later transition; its later operational role is distinct from its adoption. The United States remained the key external economic and security partner for Western Europe and Japan.

Major global tariff disputes were less defining than payments liberalization, exchange controls, and bloc restrictions. Data not available for a single comprehensive 1955 tariff-change, embargo or sanctions table.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Energy markets were stable by later standards. Oil prices remained low and relatively fixed, with production dominated by major international oil companies and major producing regions outside the later 1970s OPEC-price-shock regime. OPEC did not exist until 1960.

Gold was fixed at $35 per ounce and remained the core monetary commodity. Non-Soviet gold production was heavily influenced by South Africa. Copper prices were strong, reflecting industrial demand. Agricultural conditions were more country-specific, and several primary producers experienced external stress even while industrial economies boomed.

**Production and price measures.** BIS preliminary physical production estimates give crude oil 707→789 million metric tons, steel 223→267 million tons, electricity 1,355→1,520 billion kWh and refined copper 3.440→3.795 million tons. Calculated changes are 11.60%, 19.73%, 12.18% and 10.32%. These are the report's estimates of world production, and their coverage completeness is not independently certified; copper is refinery output, not mine metal content. ### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor-market data are not comparable across countries. The United States had an annual unemployment rate around 4.4 percent, while many Western European economies operated near full employment but with different statistical definitions. The UK Bank of England reconstruction consistent with the labour force survey gives 1.55%. Socialist economies did not measure unemployment in comparable ways; neither administered employment nor the absence of a survey makes unemployment zero. Spanish and Canadian historical labor series carry particular denominator and source-break caveats.

Demographically, the postwar baby boom and migration were major forces. North America, Australia, and parts of Western Europe experienced household formation, housing demand, and urban expansion. India, China, Indonesia, Brazil, and Mexico had large and fast-growing populations, making development more difficult because output growth had to outrun population growth to raise living standards. The population in every country header belongs to MPD's territorial reconstruction; it is not a contemporaneous census count.

**Labor mobility and productivity.** BIS describes increased cross-border labor mobility in northern and western Europe. **Economic narrative:**

The United States was the clear core economy of 1955. Output rebounded strongly after the 1953-1954 downturn, and the recovery was led by durable consumer goods, housing, automobiles, defense-related production, and business investment. The Federal Reserve responded to the boom by raising discount rates during the year, making 1955 a useful case of postwar monetary tightening under a fixed-exchange-rate regime rather than a pure demand-management experiment.

Microeconomically, the mass-consumption economy became more visible. The first Fortune 500 list appeared in 1955, led by large integrated industrial and oil firms. General Motors, Ford, U.S. Steel, General Electric, AT&T, Standard Oil companies, and retail chains embodied scale economies, national distribution, and managerial capitalism. Consumer credit and suburban housing supported demand, but they also created the kind of cyclical sensitivity that policymakers watched closely.

The rebound was transmitted through durable goods, business investment and household borrowing. The Federal Reserve's own 1955 annual report records consumer credit increasing by more than $6 billion, against less than $1 billion in 1954, and describes longer maturities and smaller down payments, a sign of credit intensification rather than proof that banks were universally sound. BEA data give real GDP growth of 7.13%, distinct from the MPD reconstruction, and industrial production growth of 12.79%.

Automobiles and housing connected household demand to steel, glass, rubber and finance. Retrospective official housing starts in the BIS report rose from 1.220 to 1.330 million nonfarm units. Federal guarantees and insurance covered well over two-fifths of outstanding home-mortgage debt at year-end, according to the Federal Reserve report; this is a financing-stock share, not the share of all houses or all new lending. City asking prices and rents diverged materially, as section 6D shows.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Automobiles and housing were leading growth engines. Defense procurement remained important in aerospace, electronics, shipbuilding, and machine tools. Services and branded retailing expanded with suburbanization. McDonald's corporate expansion began under Ray Kroc in 1955, and Disneyland opened in California, signaling the rising economic role of franchising, leisure, and family-oriented consumer services. Banking conditions were tighter than in 1954 as lending terms lengthened, and equity markets delivered strong returns. Nonfarm housing starts rose from 1.220 to 1.330 million, and consumer credit grew by more than $6 billion.

**Economic narrative:**

The Soviet economy remained the second-largest aggregate in this reconstruction, but its measured GDP is much less certain than that of market economies. The 1955 Soviet story was continued heavy-industry expansion under central planning, with military, metallurgy, energy, and machinery sectors favored over household consumption. The Warsaw Pact was formed in May 1955, formalizing the economic and military geography of the Eastern bloc.

The year also sat between Stalinist mobilization and the policy adjustments of the late 1950s. Industrial output and urban employment expanded, but household welfare, housing, and consumer goods lagged. ### 5B. Corporate landscape

The corporate landscape of 1955 was dominated by large industrial, oil, utility, telecommunications, and transport firms. In the United States, the first Fortune 500 list, published in May 1955, made this structure visible. The Library of Congress gives its leading ten archival names as General Motors, Jersey Standard (the Exxon predecessor Standard Oil of New Jersey), U.S. Steel, General Electric, Esmark, Chrysler, Armour, Gulf Oil, Mobil and DuPont; some are later archive labels rather than contemporaneous corporate names. Ford was a major producer of the period but does not appear on the first list. Fortune's first list was a U.S. industrial selection, not a global census, and the 1956 archive list is a publication-year ranking rather than a uniform calendar-1955 accounting panel. Globally, comparable top-company rankings are less complete, so a global top-20 revenue or market-capitalization list for 1955 is Data not available.

Major corporate events in 1955 were less about mega-mergers than about business-model emergence. McDonald's System began under Ray Kroc, a milestone in franchising and standardized food service. Disneyland opened, marking a new form of large-scale themed leisure. Sony's TR-55 transistor radio showed Japan's move into advanced consumer electronics. In Europe, large incumbents in autos, chemicals, steel, banking, and energy were central to reconstruction growth. The conversion of the Imperial Bank into the State Bank of India on 1 July was a concrete ownership event. The McDonald's and Disneyland milestones are corporate-history events, not quantitative contributions to GDP.

Data not available for a complete global M&A, IPO, bankruptcy, nationalization or market-cap ranking for 1955. National stock-exchange archives exist, but they do not form a global corporate event database, and the absence of a recorded event does not prove that none occurred.

### 5C. Innovation & technology

The most economically relevant technology trends were transistors, aviation, computing, nuclear energy, telecommunications, and industrial automation. The transistor was moving from laboratory breakthrough to commercial electronics. Sony's 1955 transistor radio was not yet a global mass-market revolution by itself, but it was a signal of Japan's technology trajectory. Defense R&D, especially in the United States and USSR, continued to shape electronics, materials, aircraft, and computing.

The U.S. Budget FY 2000 historical Table 9.8 reports federal outlays for conducting R&D of $1.895 billion in FY 1955 against $1.848 billion in FY 1954, a nominal increase of 2.54%. Defense accounts for $1.550 billion and nondefense $345 million. This is neither total U.S. R&D nor global R&D, and retrospective agency headings such as NASA do not imply the agency existed in 1955. Infrastructure megaprojects mattered. Highway construction, port modernization, power stations, rail and shipping investment, irrigation, and urban housing shaped productivity. ### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

The major labor-market shift was the movement from agriculture into manufacturing and services. In Japan, Italy, France, Germany, Brazil, Mexico, and India, the direction of labor movement varied in speed but was structurally similar. In richer economies, unions and collective bargaining played major roles. In socialist economies, labor allocation was administrative. In developing economies, underemployment and informal work mattered more than measured unemployment.

Education and human capital were long-run growth drivers, but 1955 cross-country education-spending and attainment figures are Data not available. Migration was economically important in Canada and Australia, while rural-to-urban migration mattered across Europe, Latin America, and Asia.

Mexico's annual report states that average nominal wages increased about 15% but qualifies the real-wage improvement; its wholesale index is not an appropriate household deflator. Minimum-wage changes announced late in 1955 took effect on 1 January 1956 and are excluded from 1955 realized wage growth. Its railway figures distinguish tons, ton-km, passengers and passenger-km rather than choosing the largest number as generic transport growth. ### 6C. Currencies

Major currencies operated under Bretton Woods pegs or managed parity systems. The dollar-gold link was central, sterling was a major reserve and trading currency, and the yen was fixed. The Canadian dollar floated. Capital controls were normal, not exceptional. Socialist and many developing economies used administrative exchange rates, multiple rates, or tight exchange allocation. For these countries, exchange-rate figures are not comparable with market rates.

The country tables separate official parities from annual averages and parallel quotations. The BIS parity table is dated mid-May 1956, so only dated unchanged parities or 1955 regime facts are carried back, not its Canadian market quotation. Argentina's effective export proceeds ranged by commodity after levies, and Indonesia's export and import rates were different transaction prices. Exchange controls therefore affected relative prices even without a change to a basic parity. A global reserve-currency composition panel and a comprehensive inventory of capital-control changes are Data not available.

### 6D. Alternative assets

Gold was fixed at $35 per ounce, so its official price did not behave like a floating alternative asset; the official price is not proof of a zero return after storage, dealing restrictions or a parallel-market premium. Real estate markets benefited from housing demand in the United States, Canada, Australia, and parts of Western Europe. Commodity-linked assets benefited from industrial demand, especially copper and energy, but broad commodity-investment vehicles did not resemble modern markets.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The main active macro risk in 1955 was overheating under fixed exchange rates. Strong growth increased imports, stressed reserves, and forced credit tightening. This was especially visible in the UK and in European policy discussions. In the United States, the risk was not external convertibility pressure in the same way, but a domestic credit and asset-cycle risk after rapid recovery: U.S. consumer debt and mortgage finance grew rapidly while lending terms lengthened. This was a contemporaneous credit concern, not a sign of an already realized systemic banking crisis.

Developing-country inflation was a second major risk. IMF reporting identified several countries with price rises above 10 percent. The mechanism differed by country but often involved fiscal pressure, credit expansion, exchange-rate changes, import constraints, and commodity-export volatility. External deficits and exchange restrictions in several economies provided another constraint on investment and consumption.

A third risk was geopolitical segmentation. The Warsaw Pact and Bandung both reflected a world economy divided by security blocs and development politics. Trade and capital flows were not purely economic. They were shaped by alliances, sanctions, aid, and strategic technology restrictions. Argentine exchange and political changes and administrative pricing elsewhere further complicated capital allocation.

**Manufacturing failure:** the Cutter polio-vaccine incident illustrates a material quality-control and regulatory risk in scaling a new technology. The CDC surveillance report of 14 May 1955 distinguished vaccinated cases, household contacts and possible community transmission. Later CDC retrospective totals are not projected back into what investigators knew on that date, and the event is not used to generalize that vaccination or the whole healthcare sector failed.

With hindsight, one can see stresses building toward the 1956 Suez crisis and toward renewed stop-go problems in sterling. That hindsight should not be confused with what was obvious in 1955. Contemporary observers saw strong growth and balance-of-payments pressure; they did not yet know the exact path of the 1956 shocks. No quantitative counterfactual, imminent global-crash forecast or comprehensive crisis-free certificate is offered.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1955 vs. 1954)

The biggest shift from 1954 to 1955 was acceleration. The United States moved from post-recession weakness into a strong expansion: U.S. MPD growth shifted from −0.66% to +7.07%, a 7.73 pp acceleration in that series; contemporary GNP and modern BEA GDP use different concepts and vintages. Western Europe continued rapid growth but at an even more strained pace in some countries. Japan strengthened. Canada and Mexico posted strong output gains in the MPD reconstruction, and Mexico's own real GNP estimate accelerated from 7.7% to 9.9% while its reserves rose $202.9 million. China's apparent MPD reversal is not narrated as a confirmed national contraction and rebound, because the NBS and Wu reconstructions differ.

Monetary policy shifted toward restraint. The Federal Reserve raised discount rates through the year, and UK Bank Rate remained high. This was a concrete policy pivot from recovery support toward managing boom conditions, credit, and external balance. Official rate paths tightened in several major economies but not uniformly: Canada's within-year cut was followed by increases, and Brazil reduced its rate.

Global reserves improved from the depressed early-postwar position, yet trade grew so fast that reserve adequacy remained a concern. This created a paradox: 1955 looked strong, but the financial architecture still required controls, coordination, and discipline. The BIS export-volume index, on its limited geography, rose 105→113, against 100→105 in 1954; the acceleration is 2.62 percentage points using one vintage and rounded index levels.

The adjusted top-20 ranking changed at the margin. Belgium entered, while the historical Pakistan aggregate slipped just outside. The largest economies remained stable: United States, former USSR, China, Germany, United Kingdom, France, India, Japan, and Italy. Germany passed the UK and France passed India; the former USSR and Germany remain source-territory constructs, not harmonized sovereign accounts.

Housing supply diverged: France and Italy expanded completions while the UK and Netherlands contracted them. U.S. newspaper asking rents fell in several cities even where asking prices rose, so no blanket urban-property boom is asserted. Mexico's two wholesale-inflation timing measures and its late-announced 1956 minimum-wage increases remain distinct.

Geopolitically, 1955 became more clearly multipolar within a Cold War frame. The Warsaw Pact formalized one side. Bandung showed that many Asian and African countries wanted development autonomy rather than simple alignment. The Messina process showed Western Europe moving toward deeper economic integration.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

**Highest-confidence data:** The highest-confidence elements are the existence and structure of the Bretton Woods regime, the fixed gold price, the broad postwar expansion, U.S. and UK policy-rate direction, and the institutional events of 1955 such as the Warsaw Pact, Bandung, the European Monetary Agreement, and the corporate milestones listed above. Original BIS tables, dates and footnotes, Banco de México observations, China's official restatement table, the dated French equity endpoints and the source-defined city comparisons are also firm as source observations. National source families often share lineages, so they provide useful contrasts rather than automatic independent cross-validation.

**Medium-confidence data:** Maddison Project real GDP, population, and per-capita values are the most coherent way to rank 1955 economies, but they are reconstructed historical estimates. The ranking is robust for the top few economies, but less robust near the bottom of the top 20. No independent, harmonized second source exists for the ranking; Our World in Data's presentation of Maddison shares the same lineage, and PWT does not eliminate historical uncertainty. The Bank of England millennium dataset for the UK, the U.S. BEA, BLS and industrial-production observations, Toyota and Volkswagen records and the named index benchmarks are attributed to their publishers; the Macrohistory Database series are source-defined reconstructions.

**Lowest-confidence data:** Soviet bloc, China, Poland, and many developing-country GDP estimates are weak compared with U.S., UK, Canadian, or Western European data. The uncertainty is especially high where prices were administered, exchange rates were non-market, or statistical capacity was limited. For Soviet bloc and non-market economies, an uncertainty band of plus or minus 20-30 percent is reasonable for interpretation.

**Indicators not comparable:** Unemployment is not comparable across countries in 1955. A U.S. labor-force unemployment rate, a West European registered unemployment count, a socialist economy's plan-allocation measure, and rural underemployment in India or Indonesia are different concepts. Inflation is also problematic where prices were controlled or multiple exchange rates existed. Administered market concepts and closed exchanges are inapplicable, not missing numeric zeros.

**Indicator treatment:** Germany's CPI, unemployment, policy-rate, debt and current-account values refer to West Germany only and appear in the notes, while the DM parity is shown as the value. Parallel exchange quotations from the Macrohistory Database appear only in the notes, with official parities shown as values. The Soviet state-retail index, Poland's cost-of-living index and the Mexico City wholesale index are not presented as national CPIs. Canada's current-account ratio mixes currency units and is shown as Data not available.

**Important missing data:** A complete 1955 table for budget balance/GDP, and for government debt/GDP, current account/GDP and policy interest rates across all top-20 countries, is Data not available. A certified world GDP total and growth rate, an all-country global trade-volume percentage, global R&D spending, a global corporate market-cap ranking, a global M&A table, patent counts, skill-specific labor statistics, exchange-specific index levels and most international city prices are also Data not available.

**Potential conflicts across sources:** Maddison and PWT use different base years, methods, and coverage. Current-dollar GDP rankings would produce a different list from real-PPP rankings, especially for exchange-controlled economies. Modern borders also create issues: MPD Germany differs from the 1955 political reality, and former USSR aggregates differ from present successor-state rows. The rankings here are therefore historical real-PPP estimates, not definitive nominal market valuations. China's MPD growth (Wu reconstruction, 9.06%) differs from the NBS retrospective 6.9%. The BIS cost-of-living table reports Japan at +2.9% while the JST annual CPI gives −1.19%; the BIS timing and index definition have not been reconciled, so neither is substituted for the other. The U.S. current balance is −$345 million in JST and +$1,440 million in the BIS. The two 1955 oil prices ($1.93 posted Arabian Light and the $2.77 U.S. first-purchase average) are distinct benchmarks. Zurich rent growth differs between the municipal yearbook and HSSO (section 6D).

**Russian constituent record:** RUS is excluded because the retained former-USSR aggregate overlaps it. Its raw rank of sixth does not create a twenty-first independent economy. The excluded reconstruction is 479.242 billion 2011 international dollars, population 111.125 million and GDP per capita 4,312.62; these figures are a constituent-series diagnostic, and no value is added to the USSR.

**Divided Germany:** The DEU record does not replace separate West German and East German political economies. West German monetary statistics cannot describe eastern Germany, and eastern administrative credit, enterprises and fiscal accounts are Data not available.

**Whole Pakistan:** The historical state comprised western and eastern wings. Pakistan's devaluation took effect on 1 August 1955, while the BIS parity table records 31 July; these are effective-date and parity-record conventions, not necessarily a contradiction. The old 9.264 rupees per pound became 13.333.

**Dependencies and omitted territories:** A complete sovereign and dependency concordance for 1955 is Data not available. Colonial trade, metropolitan GDP and dependency output must be reconciled before world aggregation.
