# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1942

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1942 was the decisive year in the wartime transformation of the world economy. Governments redirected labor, capital, commodities, credit, and household purchasing power toward state-priority production. The United States recorded the largest measurable expansion among major economies, while the United Kingdom and Canada operated at very high mobilization levels. Germany, Japan, and Italy were increasingly constrained by resources, transport, and the costs of controlling wider economic spaces.

The year also exposes the limits of historical GDP measurement. The ranking below uses Angus Maddison's original 2010 downloadable GDP levels in million 1990 international Geary-Khamis dollars, cross-checked against Maddison Project Database 2023 and Our World in Data methodology notes. China is the largest gap: the original Maddison 2010 GDP table has no explicit 1942 China GDP value, even though China was almost certainly a major economy by population and historical scale. It is flagged rather than guessed. The two Maddison vintages also disagree on the size of several 1942 changes: the Maddison Project Database 2023 puts U.S. growth at 10.91% against 20.0% in Maddison 2010 and 18.9% in the BEA series, and Italy's contraction at 5.59% against 1.2%.

The macroeconomic regime was financial repression, price control, rationing, exchange control, and directed credit. In the United States, the Federal Reserve pegged Treasury bills near 3/8 percent and implicitly capped long bonds near 2.5 percent. At the micro level, heavy industry, transport, energy, food distribution, and defense-related manufacturing expanded or received priority, while civilian durable goods, housing, normal retail trade, and discretionary consumption contracted. The Federal Reserve's industrial production index rose 16.03% and durable manufacturing 29.53%, yet civilian durable output in December was about half its 1935-1939 average. Banks in the United States and Canada added government securities while private lending stagnated or fell, and Indian scheduled-bank advances declined as demand liabilities rose.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1942

**1. Total-war mobilization became the central macroeconomic policy regime.** The largest economies were no longer managed primarily through peacetime fiscal and monetary tools. They were managed through procurement, rationing, labor direction, shipping allocation, and raw-material control. The United States, United Kingdom, Soviet Union, Germany, Japan, Canada, and Australia were most affected. The forward linkage was immediate: by 1943 the Allied production base became increasingly decisive, while Axis economies faced harder resource limits.

**2. Financial repression and cheap money replaced market-clearing bond finance.** The U.S. Treasury-Federal Reserve arrangement and British cheap-money policy show that public borrowing costs were politically managed. This mattered because war budgets were too large to finance at unconstrained market rates without destabilizing debt markets. U.S. member-bank holdings of government securities rose from USD 19.5 billion to USD 37.5 billion while their loans fell 10.7%. The later 1951 Treasury-Fed Accord in the United States is best understood as an exit from the wartime regime that began in 1942.

**3. Occupation economies and extraction shaped European output.** France, the Netherlands, Belgium, Austria, and other occupied territories were not simply smaller national economies. Their fiscal systems, factories, transport networks, and food supplies were tied into the German war economy. The economic significance was double: Germany gained resources, but extraction damaged occupied economies and created hidden resistance, black markets, and welfare losses not captured by GDP alone. Exchange rates in occupied countries were fixed by the occupier, and recorded prices covered controlled transactions only.

**4. Logistics became a binding economic constraint.** Shipping, rail, oil distribution, and port capacity determined whether nominal control over resources could become usable output. Japan's early territorial gains did not remove its shipping vulnerability. Britain's survival depended on imported supplies and Atlantic logistics. The Soviet Union's evacuation and relocation of industry depended on rail and administrative capacity. This theme carried forward into 1943-1945 as transport bottlenecks increasingly shaped production possibilities.

**5. Food and household welfare became central economic risks.** GDP aggregates conceal distributional failures. India is the most important warning case: aggregate output and population scale remained enormous, but food prices, transport stress, and wartime procurement created conditions that would contribute to the Bengal famine in 1943. BIS records a year-end cost-of-living rise of 46% in India, against 12% in 1941. Similar but less catastrophic mechanisms appeared in occupied Europe through rationing, requisition, and black markets.

**6. Neutrality did not mean normal economics.** Sweden, Switzerland, Spain, Turkey, and Argentina were not insulated from the war. Trade routes, commodity prices, exchange controls, and diplomatic constraints changed their microeconomies. Neutral countries faced scarcity, trade bargaining, and state intervention even without full mobilization. Turkey's year-end cost of living rose 94%, and its November wealth tax fell disproportionately on non-Muslim citizens.

**7. Wartime industrialization accelerated long-run structural change.** Canada, Australia, Mexico, and Brazil used wartime demand and import scarcity to deepen domestic manufacturing. The effect was uneven and often state-led. Mexico's manufacturing index rose 11.65% while crude petroleum fell 18.34%, and Brazil organized the state mining company Companhia Vale do Rio Doce in June. The forward link was stronger postwar industrial capacity and, in Latin America, the reinforcement of import-substitution policy.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

World GDP and global growth for 1942 are **Data not available**. Maddison 2010 provides many country GDP levels but no annual world total for the war years, and China, the Soviet Union, and Indonesia have no usable annual 1942 observation, so a sum of available countries would be biased. Global trade volume is also **Data not available** as one comparable number; the Federico-Tena world trade reconstruction ends in 1938. Qualitatively, trade was redirected from normal commerce into Lend-Lease, convoyed supply, imperial systems, clearing arrangements, blockade, occupation transfers, and exchange controls.

World population was about 2,323.4 million in 1942 against 2,304.4 million in 1941 (+0.83%) in the Gapminder version 8 reconstruction, an annual estimate that does not measure war deaths or migration directly.

Reserve-currency dynamics were pre-Bretton Woods. The U.S. dollar was tied to the statutory gold price of $35 per ounce, sterling-area controls remained important, and many currencies were not freely convertible. Inflation was generally pressured by shortages and fiscal expansion, but CPI series were distorted by rationing, price ceilings, and black markets. Commodity prices for oil, metals, wheat, and other staples often reflected administered prices rather than free-market scarcity.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The clearest monetary event was U.S. yield-curve control: Treasury bills near 3/8 percent and long bonds near 2.5 percent to stabilize war finance. The Federal Reserve announced purchases of Treasury bills at a 3/8% discount rate in late April; the average three-month bill yield for 1942 was 0.326%, against 0.103% in 1941, a comparison affected by the end of the bills' tax exemption in March 1941. Britain maintained a cheap-money regime with the 2 percent Bank Rate and directed credit. Germany, Japan, Italy, the Soviet Union, and occupied Europe relied more on administrative credit allocation than conventional monetary policy.

Official discount rates were almost all unchanged during the year: the Reichsbank at 3.50%, the Bank of Japan at 3.285%, the Bank of Italy at 4.50%, the Banque de France at 1.75%, the Bank of Canada at 2.50%, the Reserve Bank of India at 3.00%, and the Swiss National Bank at 1.50%. Mexico was the exception, raising its discount rate from 4.00% to 4.50% from 4 June. Bank balance sheets show how war finance worked: Canadian chartered banks added CAD 534 million of securities while loans in Canada were flat, and Indian scheduled-bank advances fell while demand liabilities rose by about 45%.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

Trade policy was dominated by war, not tariff bargaining. Lend-Lease linked the United States to Britain, the Soviet Union, China, and other Allied recipients. Axis trade depended on occupation, bilateral clearing, and transport control. Neutral countries faced scarcity, bargaining pressure, and capital controls despite not being fully mobilized.

Latin American merchandise balances widened under rising nominal exports and import scarcity: Argentina's surplus rose from 363 to 702 million pesos, Brazil's from 1,215 to 2,851 million cruzeiros, and Mexico's balance moved from -185 to +239 million pesos. These are nominal merchandise balances, not current-account balances or real volumes. The United States-Mexico trade treaty was signed at the end of December but took effect only on 30 January 1943. In occupied Asia, Japan introduced military-note systems, and China operated with several rival currencies rather than one convertible national unit.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil, coal, steel, copper, rubber, wheat, rice, and shipping capacity were strategic constraints. The United States had large oil and refining capacity, while Germany and Japan faced tighter access problems. Physical availability mattered more than quoted price. Agriculture was equally strategic: North America and Australia supplied Allied networks, while India and occupied Europe faced food-distribution stress.

U.S. wheat production rose from 946 to 981 million bushels and Canada's from 312 to 593 million bushels. BIS estimated world gold output, partly by estimate, at 36.5 million fine ounces against 40.0 million in 1941 (-8.75%), with South Africa down from 14.386 to 14.121 million ounces. Mexico's crude-petroleum index fell 18.34% on annual averages while its electricity index rose 3.96%. Mexico's silver purchase price was set at 45 US cents per troy ounce from 31 August.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Comparable global employment data are **Data not available**. The dominant labor fact was reallocation: military service, female labor-force entry, forced labor, migration, and rural-urban shifts changed production possibilities. Wage data are not comparable because rationing and controlled prices changed the meaning of money income.

Where national series exist, they show very tight labor markets in the Allied economies: historical unemployment estimates fell to 4.7% in the United States, 0.5% in the United Kingdom, 3.15% in Canada, and 1.9% in Australia. U.S. factory employment rose 15.29% and factory payrolls 44.66%, the gap reflecting longer hours and overtime as well as wage rates. Neutral Sweden moved the other way, with unemployment rising from 4.67% to 7.5%. Forced labor and deportation in German-controlled Europe were coercion, not voluntary labor supply.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States was the largest measured economy in 1942 and the country with the most visible year-over-year expansion. Maddison 2010 gives a 1942 GDP level of 1,318,809 million 1990 international Geary-Khamis dollars, up about 20.0 percent from 1941. BEA/FRED real GDP, using a different modern U.S. national-accounts series, shows a very similar surge from 1,681.049 to 1,998.542 billion chained 2017 dollars, equal to roughly 18.9 percent. The difference is a source-method issue rather than a contradiction. The dominant story was mobilization: production, employment, logistics, and finance were reorganized around federal procurement.

Mobilization changed the composition of output more than its headline rate. The Federal Reserve industrial production index (1935-1939=100) rose from 156 to 181 (+16.03%) and durable manufacturing from 193 to 250 (+29.53%), while nondurables rose only from 135 to 141. The Board estimated that war and Lend-Lease uses absorbed roughly 60% of factory and mine output by year-end, and civilian durable production in December was about half its 1935-1939 average. Contemporary estimates put 1942 GNP at USD 151.6 billion in current prices and USD 133.2 billion at 1939 prices, against the later BEA nominal GDP estimate of USD 165.952 billion; these are different concepts and vintages.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Leading sectors were industrial production, shipbuilding, aircraft, vehicles, steel, chemicals, petroleum, and logistics. Executive Order 9024 created the War Production Board on 16 January to direct conversion and priorities. Factory employment (1939=100) rose from 132.1 to 152.3 (+15.29%) and factory payrolls from 167.5 to 242.3 (+44.66%), reflecting hours and overtime as well as headcount. Member-bank holdings of government securities rose from USD 19,539 million to USD 37,546 million while loans fell from USD 18,021 million to USD 16,088 million. In the F.W. Dodge sample of 37 Eastern States, the nominal value index of residential construction contracts (1923-1925=100) fell from 89 to 82 (-7.87%) while other construction rose from 149 to 235 (+57.72%); these are not national real construction measures. January price-control legislation, May ceilings based on March prices and the October stabilization extension constrained prices and wages; the July Little Steel formula referred to a 15% cost-of-living increase since January 1941. Detroit newspaper asking prices for houses rose 22.20% and asking rents 10.64% (historical newspaper samples, usually late May; the 1942 rent figure is a pooled static-window estimate). Chicago Pile-1 achieved the first controlled chain reaction on 2 December.

**Economic narrative:**

Germany remained one of the largest measured economies in 1942, but its aggregate size concealed rising strain. Maddison 2010 reports GDP of 406,582 million 1990 international dollars, only about 1.4 percent above 1941. Harrison's wartime-economic work indicates that Germany's military burden was already extremely high by 1942. Expansion increasingly depended on occupied territories, administrative extraction, controlled labor allocation, and narrowing civilian consumption.

Controlled official prices understated scarcity outside legal allocation channels, and the official cost-of-living index cannot measure the welfare of deported or forced workers. German share prices rose despite near-stagnant output: the Ronge top-30 reconstruction gives a price return of +10.36% and a total return of +14.26% (1941: +7.48%), under guiding quotations issued on 12 May 1942 rather than free price discovery.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Leading sectors were heavy industry, coal, steel, chemicals, rail transport, and state-directed defense production. The guiding share quotations of 12 May 1942 preceded the rigid price stop of January 1943 and the suspension of February 1943. Ownership transfers under persecution and confiscation were coercive expropriation, not ordinary corporate activity. The Macrohistory Database long-term government bond yield was unchanged at 4.35%.

### 5B. Corporate landscape

A reliable global top-10 or top-20 company ranking by 1942 revenue or market capitalization is **Data not available**. Corporate significance was concentrated in oil, steel, chemicals, transport, shipbuilding, banking, insurance, and state procurement networks. In command and occupied economies, ministries and allocation agencies often mattered more than ordinary corporate boards.

Dated corporate and institutional events illustrate the pattern. The U.S. War Production Board was created on 16 January to direct conversion and priorities. Brazil's Decree-Law 4.352 of 1 June organized Companhia Vale do Rio Doce as a state mining and railway company. The Bank of Japan was reorganized on 1 May. Banca d'Italia reported six banking concentrations, and Toyota produced 16,302 vehicles against 14,611 in 1941. Turkey's wealth tax and confiscations under German occupation were coercive transfers of ownership, not voluntary mergers or ordinary business failures. Comprehensive IPO, bankruptcy and business-entry totals are **Data not available**.

### 5C. Innovation & technology

1942 favored applied innovation: production engineering, logistics, radio, radar-related technologies, early computing work, medicine, and materials science. Comparable global R&D spending and patent statistics are **Data not available**, partly because secrecy and wartime controls distorted normal publication and filing. The sharpest dated milestone was Chicago Pile-1, which achieved the first self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction on 2 December; it was an experimental result, not the start of commercial nuclear power.

### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Labor markets tightened in Allied production economies and were forcibly reorganized in Axis and occupied economies. Women entered industrial and administrative roles in larger numbers, while skilled workers in engineering, transport, and machine operation became bottlenecks. Wages are difficult to compare because rationing and controlled prices changed the meaning of money income. Wage rules differed: Canada's cost-of-living bonus paid 25 cents a week for each index point, and the U.S. Little Steel formula of July referred to a 15% cost-of-living increase since January 1941. In Germany, BIS gives December 1942 hourly wages at 85.9 and the official cost of living at 89.5 (1928=100).

### 6B. Fixed income

The most important fixed-income fact was policy control. The United States held Treasury bills near 3/8 percent and long bonds near 2.5 percent. Britain maintained cheap money. In belligerent economies, bond yields were managed to support public finance.

U.S. annual-average yields in the Federal Reserve's tables were 0.326% for three-month Treasury bills (1941: 0.103%, tax-exempt until March 1941 and taxable thereafter, so the comparison has a tax-basis break), 2.04% for partially tax-exempt long Treasury bonds (1941: 1.95%), 2.83% for Moody's Aaa corporate bonds (1941: 2.77%) and 4.28% for Baa bonds (1941: 4.33%), so the Aaa-Baa spread narrowed from 156 to 145 basis points. The League of Nations series for the same partially tax-exempt bonds gives 2.09% (1941: 2.05%) on an end-of-month convention. Long-term government bond yields in the Macrohistory Database, whose instruments differ by country and are not all ten-year benchmarks, were little changed: United States 2.11% (1941: 2.07%), United Kingdom 3.03% (3.13%), Germany 4.35% (4.35%), Japan 3.76% (3.82%), France 3.09% (3.13%), Italy 5.67% (5.61%), Belgium 4.01% (4.13%), Netherlands 3.10% (3.25%), Canada 3.06% (3.10%), Sweden 3.23% (3.39%), Australia 3.24% (3.23%) and Switzerland 3.15% (3.39%, a federal and federal-railway bond basket). Mexico's debt agreement of 5 November rescheduled external debt, with principal amortization starting in 1948.

### 6C. Currencies

Currency markets were controlled. The dollar was tied to gold at $35 per ounce, sterling was managed through exchange controls, and Axis or occupied currencies were embedded in administrative clearing systems. Comparable free-market exchange-rate moves are **Data not available**.

Official quotations in the Macrohistory Database barely moved: 2.49 Reichsmarks, 0.2478 pounds, 1.88 guilders, 31.11 Belgian francs, 4.19 kronor and 4.30 Swiss francs per U.S. dollar, all controlled rates. Changes came through rate channels: Spain abolished its voluntary exchange rates on 26 July, moving the dollar from 12.56 pesetas in that channel to the official 10.95; Argentina kept an official buying rate of 335.82 pesos per USD 100 alongside preferential and free rates; Brazil required 30% of export proceeds to be surrendered at 16.50 cruzeiros per dollar against a free rate of 19.47, and renamed the milreis the cruzeiro one for one on 1 November. Mexico's December average selling rate was 4.851 pesos per dollar (December 1941: 4.859). Turkey removed its 40% export premium in May. Japan introduced military yen in Hong Kong on 5 January.

### 6D. Alternative assets

Gold was not a freely traded modern asset class. Real estate data for major cities are **Data not available** on a comparable basis. In shortage economies, farmland, commodities, and durable goods often became informal stores of value.

Selected city series show how differently controls and wartime demand affected housing. Detroit newspaper asking prices for houses rose 22.20% and asking rents 10.64% (Philadelphia Fed historical housing indices, newspaper samples usually taken in late May; the 1942 rent estimate pools a static window). Zurich's three-room dwelling rent index rose from 205 to 206 (1914=100) and the two-to-four-room index was unchanged at 194, reflecting rent control. In Norway, under the 1940-1954 price freeze on owner-occupied housing, the Oslo inner-city repeat-sales index rose 15.27% while Bergen's fell 16.73%. Asking prices and rent indices are not completed-sale prices or rental yields.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The largest active crisis was the global war economy itself. It created simultaneous supply shocks, fiscal shocks, monetary repression, labor shocks, and trade shocks. The year 1942 also contained turning points that are visible with hindsight: the Allied production base began to outweigh Axis capacity, while Germany and Japan became increasingly stretched by logistics and resource constraints.

Food crisis risk was one of the most important economic vulnerabilities. India is the clearest case. The Bengal famine peaked in 1943, but the conditions that made famine possible were building in 1942: food-price pressure, transport constraints, wartime procurement, and regional supply failures. BIS records year-end cost-of-living inflation of 46% and wholesale price inflation of 55% in India. Occupied Europe also faced severe food and fuel stress, although outcomes varied by region.

A second crisis was financial sustainability. Governments financed extraordinary spending through borrowing, taxation, compulsory or semi-compulsory savings, central-bank support, and price controls. With hindsight, these policies worked better in countries with deep domestic financial systems and administrative capacity. They were more fragile where fiscal capacity was low or where occupation disrupted trust. Public-debt ratios rose sharply: Germany's to 136% of GDP from 102%, the United Kingdom's to 153.2% from 133.7%, and Japan's and Italy's above 100%. Italy's November share-purchase decree almost halted official share trading in Milan, and Turkey's discriminatory wealth tax of 11 November imposed unequal burdens on minority-owned enterprises.

A third vulnerability was the mismatch between GDP and welfare. Some economies showed rising measured output while household consumption fell. The United States, Canada, and Australia grew strongly, but civilian choice narrowed. Germany and Japan maintained or redirected production, but welfare and future sustainability deteriorated. The Soviet Union sustained priority output under extreme stress, but the social cost was immense.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1942 vs. 1941)

The clearest year-over-year shift was the full entry of the United States into wartime production. Maddison 2010 shows U.S. GDP rising about 20.0 percent; BEA/FRED shows about 18.9 percent in its own real GDP series. That change alone altered the global economic balance. Canada and Australia also accelerated sharply in the available data, reflecting Allied mobilization and Pacific/Atlantic supply needs. The composition of U.S. output shifted more than its growth rate: industrial production rose 16.03% after a larger rise in 1941, and U.S. CPI inflation doubled to 10.9% from 5.1%.

Germany and Japan did not collapse in 1942, but their economic position changed from expansion to overextension. Germany's Maddison GDP rose only about 1.4 percent, while Japan's declined slightly. Italy declined. France, Belgium, and the Netherlands fell under the burden of occupation and extraction. These shifts show why 1942 was a structural break rather than a normal war year.

India's aggregate output edged down while food and price stresses intensified. Brazil declined in Maddison's table, while Mexico expanded strongly through North American wartime demand. Argentina grew modestly and remained tied to agricultural export constraints. Sweden grew slightly; Switzerland contracted slightly; Turkey expanded but remained constrained by neutrality and mobilization costs. Consumer-price inflation eased in Britain (7.1% from 10.8%), Sweden (6.85% from 13.47%), and Spain (14.11% from 19.82%), but accelerated in India and Turkey. Bank portfolios shifted further toward government paper in the United States and Canada, while Indian scheduled-bank advances fell.

In the available-data top-20 ranking, China and Indonesia exit because the Maddison 2010 table lacks 1942 GDP values for them, and Turkey and Switzerland enter, with Hungary just below. China remains the major unranked data gap.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

**Highest confidence:** U.S. real GDP growth and financial-policy facts have high confidence because BEA/FRED, Federal Reserve annual reports and Federal Reserve history provide specific data. Dated official discount rates from the League of Nations' Money and Banking and the BIS annual report, and dated balance-sheet figures from central-bank reports, are also firm, although each covers a specific institution and observation date. The Maddison 2010 GDP values for many countries are internally consistent and downloadable, but they are historical reconstructions, not contemporary national accounts.

**Moderate confidence:** United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, Sweden, Switzerland, and some Western European values have moderate confidence as reconstructed GDP levels. However, inflation, unemployment, current-account balances, budget balances, and debt/GDP are often not comparable across wartime accounting systems. The inflation, unemployment, debt and current-account ratios shown are retrospective series from the Macrohistory Database; debt coverage differs by country (federal debt only for the United States and Switzerland), and price indices under rationing record controlled prices only.

**Low confidence:** Soviet GDP, occupied economies, China, India, and many developing economies have low confidence. For the Soviet Union, Maddison 2010 repeats the same aggregate value from 1941 through 1945, which is not a credible annual path. For China, the accessible Maddison 2010 GDP sheet does not provide a 1942 GDP value. For India, aggregate GDP exists but welfare conditions and food-market stress cannot be inferred from GDP alone. Belgium has no observed household price index for 1940-1945; the Macrohistory Database figure is reconstructed from equity price indices and is not shown as CPI.

**Historical data conflicts:** Maddison 2010 uses 1990 international Geary-Khamis dollars, while Maddison Project 2023/OWID use 2011 international dollars and a different benchmark method. The values should not be mixed without conversion. U.S. growth differs between Maddison 2010 and BEA/FRED because they are different statistical systems. The difference is reported rather than forced into one number. The 2023 vintage gives markedly different 1942 growth for several economies: United States +10.91% (Maddison 2010 +20.0%), Italy -5.59% (-1.2%), Japan +0.41% (-0.5%, a sign difference), Mexico +5.72% (+7.1%), Spain +7.16% (+5.6%), Sweden +1.29% (+2.3%) and Switzerland -4.47% (-2.5%). U.S. CPI inflation is 10.9% in the BLS annual average and 10.88% in the Macrohistory Database, and 9.0% December to December. Year-end cost-of-living changes reported by BIS (Australia 9%, Sweden 5%, Switzerland 9%) differ from annual-average indices for the same countries because the periods differ. Official exchange rates for Germany and the occupied countries were administered parities; the Macrohistory Database quotations for France and Australia are restated in later currency units and the Italian quotation shows an unexplained break, so they are not shown. Japanese and Canadian exchange-rate series change source in 1942, and Spain's discount-rate series starts in 1942, so no matched change is shown for them.

**Ranking consistency:** the 1942 levels are consistent with the 1941 levels in this series (the same Maddison 2010 vintage), so the growth column can be read across the two years. Switzerland and Turkey were outside the 1941 top 20, and their 1941 growth comparators in the Maddison 2010 vintage are not shown.

**Metrics often unavailable:** global GDP for 1942, global trade volume, comparable unemployment, comparable CPI, current accounts, government debt/GDP, exchange rates with market meaning, stock-index returns across all major exchanges, corporate rankings, patent activity, and R&D spending are either unavailable or not reliable enough for research-grade numeric reporting. Calendar-year budget balances as a share of GDP are not established for any of the 20 economies because fiscal years, off-budget war spending and denominators do not match. These items are reported as **Data not available**.
