# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1926

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1926 was a late-interwar expansion year, but not a simple boom year. The world economy was recovering from the First World War, postwar inflation, debt overhangs, and trade disruption, while also rebuilding an international monetary order around the gold-exchange standard. The United States was far ahead as the largest economy; China and India had huge aggregate scale but much lower GDP per capita; Germany, the United Kingdom, the Former USSR, France, Japan, and Italy formed the next industrial tier. The year also exposed the tension between monetary stabilization and real-sector adjustment. In the Federico-Tena reconstruction, world export volume rose 3.41% while nominal exports in current US dollars fell 3.73%, so more goods crossed borders without higher export receipts.

The dominant macroeconomic force was the return to currency discipline. Britain had returned to gold in 1925 at the prewar parity, a decision that weighed heavily on coal, textiles, shipbuilding, and export regions. France entered a dramatic stabilization under Poincare in 1926, while Italy announced the lira revaluation policy known as Quota 90. Germany continued operating under the Dawes Plan stabilization framework. These choices reduced currency chaos but often shifted adjustment costs onto wages, employment, exports, and debtor balance sheets. Policy moved in different directions: the Reichsbank cut its discount rate from 9% to 6%, the Bank of England held 5%, the Banque de France raised its rate to 7.5% in August before ending at 6.5%, and the Bank of Japan's commercial-bill rate fell to 6.57% on 4 October.

Microeconomically, 1926 was defined by divergent sectoral experiences. The United States saw consumer durables, automobiles, electric utilities, roads, radios, and credit-linked retailing expand, with real GDP up 5.88% in the Maddison Project Database 2023, but bank suspensions rose from 612 to 956. Britain suffered coal conflict and a general strike, and its output fell 3.67%. Japan looked stable in GDP terms but carried banking fragility into the 1927 Showa financial crisis. The Soviet Union was still in NEP recovery, with state industry and market agriculture coexisting. China was fragmented by the Warlord Era and the Northern Expedition, making any aggregate GDP estimate unusually uncertain.

The year therefore sits at an inflection point. It was close enough to 1929 that historians can see vulnerabilities building: commodity dependence, gold-standard rigidity, external-debt dependence, bank balance-sheet weakness, and rising equity-market speculation. But it was also early enough that many contemporaries saw stabilization as success. Risks that later mattered are labeled as such, rather than presented as obvious to policymakers in 1926.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1926

**1. Gold-exchange-standard reconstruction and its costs.** The interwar monetary order was moving from postwar floating and inflation toward renewed gold links. The economic significance was high: stabilization lowered currency risk and helped revive international lending, but it also made domestic adjustment harder for countries that stabilized at overvalued exchange rates. Britain was the clearest case in 1926, while France and Italy moved through stabilization in different directions. The French franc's annual-mean dollar value fell 31.98% even as the Poincare program restored confidence, and the Bank of Italy became the sole note issuer on 1 July. The forward link is direct: gold-standard rigidity later transmitted and amplified the Great Depression.

**2. The United States as the global demand and credit anchor.** The U.S. economy was much larger than any rival and combined mass production with deep financial markets. Automobiles, construction, utilities, and consumer credit made the American expansion more microeconomically modern than most of Europe; Federal Reserve indices show industrial production rising from 104 to 108 (1923-25=100). The forward link is also important: U.S. credit conditions and securities-market psychology later mattered for global capital flows and the 1929 shock.

**3. Postwar European stabilization remained uneven.** Germany, France, Belgium, Italy, Poland, and Czechoslovakia were all still dealing with the consequences of war finance, border changes, reparations, inflation, and debt. Growth could be positive while balance sheets remained fragile. The countries most affected were Germany through foreign-credit dependence, France through public-debt stabilization, Italy through exchange-rate revaluation, and Poland through state-building and currency stabilization; the zloty's annual-mean dollar value fell 36.99%, and consumer prices rose 30.43% in France and 19.31% in Belgium.

**4. Labor conflict as macro adjustment.** The British General Strike, from 4 to 12 May, and the coal lockout that lasted until late November showed how exchange-rate policy, global coal competition, productivity problems, and wage bargaining could combine into a national economic crisis. The theme is not just a labor event; it is a mechanism by which deflationary policy was transmitted to households and regions. The forward link was the 1927 Trade Disputes and Trade Unions Act and the continuing decline of older British staple industries.

**5. Commodity-export economies looked healthy but concentrated.** Argentina, Canada, Australia, Brazil, Indonesia, Mexico, and parts of India depended heavily on agricultural or extractive exports. In 1926, many benefited from trade normalization and capital flows. Annual quotations nevertheless fell for Middling American cotton (-25.63%), Rio coffee (-9.41%) and Chile copper bars (-6.12%), while English wheat rose 2.08%. With hindsight, their vulnerability was concentration: a downturn in commodity prices or external credit would quickly affect fiscal revenue, exchange rates, and employment.

**6. Non-market and politically fragmented economies are difficult to measure.** The Former USSR, China, and many colonial economies are not comparable to market economies with modern national accounts. MPD estimates are valuable, but China and Poland have no direct annual MPD GDP per capita for 1926 and their levels are interpolated, and the Former USSR series is a former-country estimate for a non-market economy whose territory and source joins are not matched. Related vintages can disagree even on sign: Japan's growth is -0.04% in MPD 2023 and +0.89% in Maddison 2010. The forward link is methodological: these data should be used for scale and direction, not precision.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The central financial fact of 1926 was the gold-exchange-standard environment. The United States operated as a gold-standard reserve center. Britain had restored sterling's prewar gold parity in 1925, putting pressure on export industries. France stabilized after the Poincare crisis, and Italy moved toward Quota 90. Germany's Reichsmark stabilization and Dawes Plan framework supported a return of foreign lending. Japan had not yet resolved its banking weaknesses, which would erupt in 1927.

Interest-rate comparisons are difficult because central-bank operating frameworks differed and policy rates were often discount rates, not modern overnight targets. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York set 4% on 8 January, 3.5% on 23 April and 4% on 13 August, while the other eleven Reserve Banks kept 4% all year; the Board reported about US$65 million of securities purchases in the spring and a reduction of about US$75 million from August. The Reichsbank cut its rate on 12 January, 27 March, 7 June and 6 July, reaching 6%. The Banque de France moved to 7.5% on 2 August and to 6.5% on 16 December. Bank Rate stayed at 5% in Britain, the Bank of Italy at 7%, De Nederlandsche Bank at 3.5% and the Riksbank at 4.5%. The Bank of Japan's commercial-bill rate fell from 7.30% to 6.57% on 4 October; the Macrohistory short-term rate for Japan of 8.83% is not reconciled to that facility. ### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

Trade was shaped by the postwar treaty system, reparations, tariffs, colonial preferences, and the restoration of monetary stability. Germany's admission to the League of Nations in 1926 improved diplomatic normalization, while the Dawes Plan continued to structure reparations and international lending. The Bank of Italy reported a continental steel agreement signed on 30 September by German, French, Belgian and Luxembourg producers, initially allocating production for the following three months; a cartel agreement is not evidence of later quota compliance. China entered the Northern Expedition, which affected fiscal control, railways, military finance, and treaty-port commerce; a US diplomatic telegram of 3 October records arguments over Canton duties and proposals for resistance, including possible naval force, not an implemented blockade. Colonial economies such as India, Indonesia, and parts of Africa were deeply integrated into imperial trade networks, but without sovereign macro policy.

Regional integration in the modern post-1945 sense did not yet exist. There was no EEC, Bretton Woods system, IMF, World Bank, ASEAN, or WTO. The relevant institutions were the League of Nations, central banks, imperial administrations, reparations agencies, and private financial houses. Bilateral tensions were often about exchange rates, debts, reparations, tariffs, and colonial trade rather than modern trade agreements.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Coal remained the central industrial fuel, but oil's strategic and commercial importance was rising. The British coal crisis was both a sectoral and macroeconomic event. Oil production and refining supported the growth of automobiles, shipping, and military logistics, but OPEC did not exist. Metals such as copper, iron ore, steel inputs, and tin mattered for construction, electrification, and manufacturing. Agricultural staples shaped welfare in low-income economies and export earnings in countries such as Canada, Argentina, Australia, Brazil, India, and Indonesia. ### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Global employment statistics for 1926 are not comparable. Most countries lacked modern labor-force surveys, and unemployment definitions differed sharply: U.S. unemployment of 1.9% is Lebergott's civilian-labor-force reconstruction, British unemployment of 8.67% is a different reconstruction in the Bank of England millennium dataset, and the French figure covers only wage earners in manufacturing, mining and construction. The most visible labor event was Britain's General Strike and coal lockout. U.S. factory employment rose from 95 to 96 and payrolls from 107 to 109 (1919=100), and British India's cotton mills employed 2.24% fewer workers despite 2.81% more looms. Demographically, high-population, low-income economies such as China and India remained enormous in aggregate GDP because of population scale, while settler economies such as the United States, Canada, Australia, and Argentina had much higher GDP per person. Urbanization, migration, and mechanization were slowly reshaping labor demand, especially in North America, Western Europe, and Japan.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States remained the largest economy by a wide margin. MPD places the 1926 economy at about 1.37 trillion 2011 international dollars, with real output up about 5.9 percent from 1925. The growth pattern was the classic high-productivity mid-1920s expansion: autos, roads, residential and commercial construction, electric utilities, telephones, radios, mass retailing, and consumer credit all reinforced each other. Monetary conditions were not yet the late-1927 speculative accelerator, but financial markets were liquid and confidence remained high.

The important 1926 macro story was not crisis but imbalance building under prosperity. Farm incomes lagged urban manufacturing; productivity gains were unevenly distributed; securities markets became more active; and consumer installment credit grew with durable-goods demand. In hindsight, the year sits inside the pre-1929 leverage and asset-price cycle, although contemporaries mostly read it as a normal continuation of post-1921 recovery.

Federal Reserve indices (1923-25=100) support the direction of the expansion: industrial production rose from 104 to 108, manufacturing from 105 to 108 and minerals from 99 to 107, while factory employment rose from 95 to 96 and payrolls from 107 to 109 (1919=100). The Federal Reserve Board reported about US$65 million of securities purchases in the spring and a reduction of about US$75 million from August, and the New York bank moved its discount rate from 4% to 3.5% in April and back to 4% in August. Motor-vehicle output was curtailed in the autumn.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Automobiles and associated steel, glass, rubber, petroleum, road building, and finance were the most visible growth complex. Radio broadcasting and household appliances were still smaller than autos, but they shaped expectations about future corporate earnings. Banking appeared healthy, yet rural banks remained more fragile than large city banks. Bank suspensions rose from 612 to 956, involving US$272.488 million of deposits, while 147 previously suspended banks reopened. Member-bank real-estate loans were US$2.65 billion on 30 June, 12% of all loans. A nominal department-store sales index rose 3.82% and the value of building contracts 6.22%, which is not construction volume. Real consumption per capita in the Barro-Ursúa reconstruction rose 2.86% and nominal bank credit 7.70%, while the Macrohistory house-price index fell 2.62%; newspaper asking-price indices rose 8.32% in New York City and 3.27% in Boston, and asking rents fell 2.38% and 3.68%.

China was almost certainly the second-largest economy in aggregate scale because of its population, but the 1926 GDP figure is one of the least secure in the ranking. The economic setting was the late Warlord Era. The Northern Expedition began in 1926, seeking to reunify a fragmented political and fiscal system. Treaty-port commerce, Shanghai finance, regional industrialization, railways, land taxes, customs revenue, and military finance all operated inside a divided sovereignty structure. The main economic mechanism was not a national policy cycle but the interaction of local militarized taxation, trade flows, and regional manufacturing.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Agriculture dominated employment and household welfare. The modern sector was concentrated in treaty ports, textiles, shipping, banking, and light manufacturing. Political risk increased transaction costs, while Shanghai remained a critical node for finance, cotton textiles, and foreign trade.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The active crisis of 1926 was the British coal and labor crisis. It was not a financial panic, but it was systemic for the United Kingdom because it combined export competitiveness, gold parity, wage bargaining, coal productivity, transport, and public order. The General Strike ended after nine days, but the miners' dispute continued until late November and left lasting effects on labor law and coal communities.

France faced a currency and debt-confidence crisis that stabilized after Poincare's return. Italy chose a politically powerful but economically restrictive lira policy. Japan carried unresolved bank and corporate balance-sheet problems into 1927. Germany's recovery depended heavily on foreign capital. The Former USSR's NEP recovery masked political conflict over grain procurement and industrialization. China faced military and fiscal fragmentation. Commodity exporters depended on global demand and credit. In the United States, bank suspensions rose from 612 to 956, involving US$272.488 million of deposits, and Mexico's new central bank reported difficult gold, silver and foreign-exchange conditions.

Hindsight-only vulnerabilities were also accumulating. The U.S. was not yet in the final speculative surge, but consumer credit, securities-market enthusiasm, and sectoral imbalances were already visible. Gold-standard restoration looked like stabilization in 1926, but with hindsight it reduced flexibility in the face of later shocks. Commodity-export concentration looked manageable in a growing world economy, but became dangerous when prices and credit fell after 1929.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1926 vs. 1925)

The largest structural change from 1925 to 1926 was the shift from postwar monetary debate toward active stabilization. Britain had already returned to gold in 1925 and experienced the real-sector consequences in 1926. France moved from currency crisis to stabilization. Italy announced Quota 90 and consolidated note issue in the Bank of Italy from 1 July. Germany continued under Dawes Plan stabilization, and the Reichsbank's discount rate fell from 9% to 6%. Czechoslovakia's national bank began operations on 1 April. This made 1926 a year of restored financial order but also of deflationary and distributional pressure.

The top-20 ranking changed internally but not in membership. The United Kingdom fell from third to fifth in the MPD/interpolation screen because output contracted, with growth moving from +4.86% in 1925 to -3.67%. India and Germany moved ahead of it. The Former USSR's raw MPD series rose from a lower postwar recovery base, but its change is not a comparable growth rate. The United States widened its lead over every other economy, with growth accelerating from 1.72% to 5.88%. Commodity exporters such as Canada, Argentina, Mexico, Indonesia, and the Netherlands showed strong or solid growth. World export volume rose 3.41% while nominal exports fell 3.73%.

New risks emerged in 1926. Britain's labor conflict showed how gold parity could turn sectoral weakness into national crisis. Japan's unresolved banking weaknesses were close to eruption. France's stabilization created a strong franc regime that later affected gold flows and international adjustment. U.S. financial markets remained orderly, but the ingredients of late-1920s speculative finance became more visible, and U.S. bank suspensions rose by more than half.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

Data confidence is highest for the GDP per capita and population values directly observed in MPD 2023 and cross-described by OWID. It is lower for countries where MPD has benchmark rather than annual GDP per capita data. In the top 20, China and Poland use log-linear interpolation for GDP per capita; China is especially uncertain because political fragmentation and weak national statistics make a precise 1926 GDP concept impossible. Because these levels are interpolated, their implied growth rates (+0.47% and +2.81%) are not shown as observed growth. Former USSR is a former-country and non-market estimate; uncertainty should be treated as at least plus or minus 20-30 percent for broad comparisons. Its MPD series rises 14.84%, but the territory and source joins between its GDP and population records, which draw on Markevich and Harrison's national-income estimates, are not matched, so the change is not shown as comparable growth. Related vintages differ: Maddison 2010, in 1990 Geary-Khamis dollars, gives Japan +0.89% against -0.04% in MPD 2023, and the two share much of their lineage, so they are not independent corroboration.

Indicator coverage now rests on named sources with differing definitions. Consumer prices, unemployment, debt and current-account ratios for most European and settler economies come from the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database, which uses its own nominal GDP denominators, not the MPD real PPP levels; the British values come instead from the Bank of England millennium dataset, and the U.S. CPI and unemployment from the Bureau of Labor Statistics and Lebergott. U.S. unemployment is 1.9% in Lebergott against 1.8% in the Macrohistory series. Policy rates are named central-bank discount rates at year-end, except Japan, whose value is the commercial-bill rate from 4 October. Where only a market rate, an undefined aggregation or a deposit rate exists (Spain, Belgium, Australia, India), the value is Data not available and the proxy is given in the Notes. Budget ratios are Data not available except for the United Kingdom, because revenue and expenditure series are not matched to calendar-year GDP. The Belgian current-account field is a merchandise trade balance and is not shown. Exchange rates switch basis from earlier years in this series: 1926 values are annual means of New York buying rates in US dollars per local unit, except sterling, which uses the Bank of England annual rate (US$4.85775, against a Federal Reserve Board mean of US$4.858235). The Belgian annual mean spans the change from franc to belga and is not shown as a value. French unemployment covers only industrial wage earners and is not shown as a rate. The Japanese current account and debt use a historical national-income denominator.

The share of the British GDP decline due to the General Strike is not established: the strike lasted nine days and the coal dispute about seven months, and no decomposition of the annual decline exists. The Dow Jones endpoint of about 157.20 (S&P Dow Jones Indices) is a single price index level, not a broad market measure, and the broader Macrohistory U.S. series shows a price gain of 8.27%.

Historical GDP rankings are also sensitive to territorial definitions. MPD country entities are not the same as empires, colonies, customs unions, or modern sovereign states. British India, China, the Dutch East Indies, the Former USSR, and former-country series such as Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia require special caution. India's MPD record is undivided India dated to 1 October, and Germany's is on 1936 frontiers. The ranking should be read as a real-PPP country/entity scale estimate, not as a nominal-dollar list and not as a list of fully sovereign modern states.

The weakest claims in this document are those involving global aggregates, cross-country financial-market returns outside the United States and United Kingdom, and precise macro indicators for colonial or non-market economies. For China, the Former USSR, Indonesia, Poland, Argentina, Czechoslovakia, Mexico and Brazil, no national CPI, unemployment, debt, current-account or budget ratio with a documented definition is available for 1926. Household debt and saving, bank asset quality, company rankings, healthcare, telecommunications and defense statistics are Data not available. The strongest claims are the qualitative identification of monetary stabilization, the British coal/general strike crisis, the Poincare stabilization, the Quota 90 announcement, the Soviet NEP recovery context, dated central-bank rate changes, and the broad GDP scale ranking derived from MPD/OWID methodology.
