# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1927

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1927 was a late-cycle expansion year in the interwar world economy. The dominant macro frame was not a single global crisis, but a fragile prosperity built on the restored gold-exchange standard, large United States capital exports, European reconstruction after the inflationary disruptions of the early 1920s, and fast industrial growth in selected economies. The Maddison Project Database 2023 estimates the United States as by far the largest economy, with estimated GDP of about 1.378 trillion 2011 international dollars, but its real growth was only 0.39%. Germany and the United Kingdom moved ahead of India in the 1927 Maddison ranking because both European economies rebounded strongly from 1926 disruptions (10.00% and 8.05%), while India was nearly flat in real aggregate terms and France, Italy and Mexico contracted.

The year also exposed the weaknesses of the interwar financial architecture. The United States eased money-market conditions in 1927, partly in a world where Britain remained under pressure after returning to gold in 1925: the New York discount rate fell from 4% to 3.5% effective 6 August, although from November open-market purchases no longer fully offset gold exports. The Bank of England cut Bank Rate from 5.0 percent to 4.5 percent effective 21 April. France was moving toward formal stabilization after the Poincare stabilization, Italy was enforcing its high-lira policy and fixed its new monetary regime by decree on 21 December, Germany faced a Reichsbank-induced stock-market break in May, and Japan suffered the Showa Financial Crisis. To contemporaries, 1927 often looked like stabilization. In hindsight, it was also a year in which gold-standard asymmetries, cross-border credit dependence, bank balance-sheet weaknesses, and equity speculation deepened. U.S. reporting-bank loans on securities rose 15.81% between December averages while other loans fell 0.42%.

The microeconomic story was a mixture of modernity and strain. United States mass production remained the central industrial model, but automobile output weakened because Ford shut down for the transition from the Model T to the Model A; U.S. motor-vehicle output fell 20.93% in League of Nations data. Radio, electrical equipment, consumer appliances, aviation enthusiasm after Lindbergh's transatlantic flight, chemicals, artificial fibers, and modern distribution were expanding. In Germany, rationalization and heavy industry recovered but the stock-market intervention damaged confidence. In Japan, bank fragility and the bankruptcy of Suzuki & Co. revealed the limits of postwar restructuring. In colonial and semi-colonial economies, agriculture, plantation exports, mining, railways, and port-city commerce remained more important than consumer-durable industries. World merchandise-export volume rose 6.48% in the Federico-Tena reconstruction, faster than the 4.47% rise in nominal exports, because several commodity prices fell.

The main analytical caution is that 1927 predates standardized national accounts. GDP, population, and GDP per capita are Maddison-style historical estimates in constant 2011 international dollars, not official nominal national accounts. The top-20 table is generated from the Maddison Project Database 2023 full data sheet by multiplying GDP per capita by population and ranking the resulting real PPP GDP estimates. Soviet, colonial, Chinese, and developing-country figures should be treated as reconstructions rather than official measurements. ## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1927

**1. Late-cycle interwar stabilization under the gold-exchange standard.** The dominant regime was a reconstructed gold-exchange standard rather than the classical pre-1914 gold standard. Many economies had either returned to gold, were stabilizing currencies, or were managing exchange rates around gold-linked parities. The significance for economic history is that policy priorities were centered on exchange stability, reserve protection, and credibility, often at the expense of domestic labor-market or credit-cycle stabilization. In 1927, the United Kingdom kept sterling on gold after its 1925 return, France moved from de facto stabilization toward formal legal stabilization in 1928, Germany operated under post-hyperinflation stabilization, and Italy defended the high-lira policy associated with quota 90; the annual-average lira quotation in New York rose 32.57%. The forward linkage is direct: the same system would magnify the deflationary pressure of 1929-1933 when countries that stayed on gold longest generally suffered deeper contractions.

**2. United States financial dominance and credit-cycle risk.** The United States was the largest economy and the key source of international lending. The Federal Reserve's 1927 easing and open-market operations have been interpreted differently by historians. One view stresses international cooperation and support for sterling. Another stresses that easier credit fed speculation in securities and weakened discipline before the 1929 crash. What can be stated safely is that 1927 combined mild United States output softness in automobiles and steel with buoyant securities markets and large international financial influence. Reporting-bank loans on securities rose from USD 5,694 million to USD 6,594 million between December averages; this measures credit composition, not the share of speculative finance. The countries most affected included the United Kingdom, Germany, Canada, Latin American borrowers, and commodity exporters dependent on New York and London credit.

**3. European recovery, rationalization, and uneven labor markets.** Germany, the United Kingdom, Czechoslovakia, Belgium, the Netherlands, Austria, and Switzerland all ranked in the top 20 by Maddison GDP estimates. Germany's estimated real aggregate GDP rose about 10.0 percent from 1926 to 1927, while the United Kingdom rose about 8.1 percent after the disruption of the 1926 General Strike and coal stoppage. These figures are estimates, but they capture the rebound visible in industrial activity: League of Nations data show world steel output rising from 93.400 to 101.717 million metric tonnes (+8.90%), with Germany up 31.82% and Czechoslovakia 25.89%. The forward linkage is that European recovery was debt-financed and reserve-constrained. Productivity improvements and industrial rationalization existed, but older sectors such as coal, shipbuilding, and textiles remained weak in parts of Britain and continental Europe.

**4. Banking fragility in Japan and financial-sector consolidation.** Japan's Showa Financial Crisis was one of the clearest crises of 1927. The Bank of Japan's historical work identifies chronic interwar instability, incomplete restructuring after the post-World War I boom, and delayed disposal of bad loans as central causes. The bankruptcy of Suzuki & Co. and stress at the Bank of Taiwan made the crisis a corporate and banking event, not simply a monetary episode. Bank of Japan banknotes rose 49.41% between 12 March and 14 May. The forward linkage was major consolidation and a more concentrated financial structure, including greater influence of large banks and zaibatsu groups.

**5. Trade liberalization rhetoric versus tariff and cartel reality.** The League of Nations sponsored the World Economic Conference in Geneva in May 1927, reflecting a broad desire to reduce obstacles to commerce and repair the international economy; its final report was adopted on 23 May, and the American delegation reported that it made no recommendation on tariff levels. The United Nations historical trade tables and League-era sources show that world trade data for 1921-1938 were already being compiled as a major policy concern. Yet the late 1920s world economy was not on a smooth path to free trade. Tariffs, exchange restrictions, bilateral bargaining, commodity agreements, state monopolies such as Spain's new petroleum monopoly, and international cartels all remained important. The forward linkage is the late-1920s failure to prevent the larger protectionist turn after 1929.

**6. Mass production, consumer durables, and household modernization.** The microeconomic frontier was not only finance. The United States led in automobiles, radio, electrical equipment, advertising, chain stores, and installment credit. Ford's 1927 transition from the Model T to the Model A is important because it showed that even the most famous mass-production system had to adapt to changing consumer preferences and General Motors-style product differentiation. Aviation moved from heroic demonstration toward commercial imagination after Lindbergh's flight. Italian rayon output rose from about 17 million to 22 million kilograms. These industries shaped productivity, urban form, consumer credit, and expectations, even where their immediate GDP effect was limited.

**7. Colonial and commodity economies under price and credit constraints.** India, Indonesia, Brazil, Mexico, Argentina, and other non-core economies were large in population or natural resources, but their economic mechanisms differed from industrial core economies. Agriculture, plantations, mining, railways, ports, and foreign trade finance dominated. Their macro data are less complete and often filtered through colonial or foreign-exchange accounting. Selected international quotations fell for crude rubber (-24.09%), English wheat (-7.51%), Newcastle coal (-8.61%) and Chilean copper (-3.93%). Forward linkages include vulnerability to the commodity price collapse after 1929 and the later move toward import-substitution or state-guided development in many Latin American and Asian economies.

### 3A. Global Aggregates

**World GDP and growth rate:** Data not available as a complete annual world total for 1927. The sum of the 53 records with non-missing 1927 GDP per capita and population in the MPD 2023 full data sheet is about 4.699 trillion 2011 international dollars. This is an arithmetic coverage sum of source-defined territories, not a world GDP estimate: China is missing, territorial correspondence between records is not established, and the sum includes the Former USSR record whose growth is not shown in section 4.

**Top-economy concentration:** The United States alone accounts for about 1.378 trillion 2011 international dollars in the MPD 2023 calculation, roughly 29 percent of the 53-record coverage sum. The next four in the ranking are Germany, the United Kingdom, India, and the Former USSR. Because China lacks a 1927 observation in the MPD 2023 full data sheet, it is not in the top-20 ranking table even though other Maddison-based series and historical reconstructions often place China among the largest economies by aggregate output in the early twentieth century. This is one of the largest data gaps for the year. China's main New York quotation, the Shanghai tael, fell from 68.4214 to 62.0994 US cents on annual average (-9.24%), a silver-linked unit rather than a single national currency.

**Global trade volume:** The Federico-Tena world merchandise-export volume index (1913=100) rose from 114.2233 in 1926 to 121.6227 in 1927 (+6.48%). Nominal exports in current borders rose from USD 30.014 billion to USD 31.354 billion (+4.47%), so volumes grew faster than values. Service trade and capital transfers are not included. United Nations historical trade tables also cover total world exports and selected-country exports and imports for 1900-1913, 1921-1938, 1948, and 1950-1960. Trade was recovering during the mid and late 1920s but remained embedded in tariffs, unstable currencies, and postwar reparations and debt flows.

**World population:** The Federico-Tena reconstruction, revised in January 2026 on 1991 borders, rises from 1,949.3 million in 1926 to 1,970.9 million in 1927 (+1.11%). ### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The global interest-rate environment was shaped by gold-standard reserve defense and by efforts to support financial stability. In the United States, discount rates and open-market operations were eased in 1927 in a context of mild recessionary pressure and international concerns: the New York rate fell from 4% to 3.5% effective 6 August, but from November onward purchases no longer fully neutralized gold exports, so easing was not continuous through the year. In the United Kingdom, Bank Rate was 5.0 percent in the first quarter and 4.5 percent from 21 April. Germany's Reichsbank was worried about foreign inflows and stock speculation; on 12 May Berlin banks announced restrictions on securities credit, and the market broke on 13 May, the Berlin "Black Friday". Japan faced a banking panic and extraordinary support operations.

Official discount rates prevailing at the end of 1927, from the Federal Reserve's comparative tables, were 7% in Germany (6% a year earlier), 4% in France after a reduction on 29 December (6.5%), 5.48% in Japan (6.57%), 4.5% in the Netherlands (3.5%), 7% in Italy, 6% in Java, 5% in Spain, 3.5% in Switzerland and 7% at the Imperial Bank of India after 22 December. These are dated official facilities, not annual-average market rates.

Exchange-rate regimes were mostly fixed or managed around gold-linked parities. The dollar and sterling were tied to gold. The French franc was de facto stabilized around the level later formalized in 1928. The Italian lira was defended at a high value, with important consequences for competitiveness. Many colonial economies used sterling-linked or metropolitan monetary arrangements.

Credit conditions were loose enough to support securities and international lending, but not uniformly easy in the real economy. The United States had a soft automobile and steel year. Germany's equity market suffered after the Reichsbank intervention. Japan's banking sector was under acute stress. Britain faced structurally high unemployment in older export industries despite easier Bank Rate.

Stock markets were generally stronger in New York, unstable in Berlin, and stressed in Tokyo. Secondary stock-return sources put the Dow Jones Industrial Average annual return in the high 20 percent range for 1927, and the rate-of-return reconstruction gives U.S. equities a total return of 35.14%. ### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The League of Nations World Economic Conference in Geneva was the central trade and economic-coordination event of 1927. Its policy significance was the attempt to frame postwar economic recovery around freer trade, currency stabilization, rationalization, and cooperation; the final report was adopted on 23 May. However, tariff barriers and national priorities remained powerful, and the conference was advisory rather than an implemented trade agreement. The United States was not a League member but participated in economic discussions through delegations and observers.

Geopolitically, China's political fragmentation and civil conflict affected trade, customs revenue, ports, and foreign concessions; a U.S. diplomatic report of 27 July described Nanking customs and tax laws announced to take effect on 1 September, although nationwide implementation is not documented. The Soviet Union was consolidating the New Economic Policy environment but moving toward the first Five-Year Plan period that began in 1928. Colonial rule shaped India, Indonesia, and many African and Southeast Asian trade flows. Reparations and inter-Allied debts remained part of the European financial background. In Mexico, the Supreme Court petroleum ruling of 17 November was a step toward settling the oil-property dispute, with amendments and regulations following in 1928.

Regional integration was limited compared with later decades. The European Economic Community did not yet exist. ASEAN did not yet exist. The main integration mechanisms were imperial preference networks, gold-standard and sterling-area linkages, bilateral treaties, cartel arrangements, shipping networks, and financial-market connections.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Coal was still the dominant industrial fuel in much of Europe and Japan, but oil was rising in transport, industry, and geopolitics. The United States was the central oil producer and consumer; Mexico and Venezuela were important oil economies, though Venezuela is not in the MPD 2023 top-20 table for 1927 because of data coverage and aggregate size. Oil prices remained low by later standards; the combined crude-petroleum quotation in the Federico-Tena database fell 4.94%. Cheap oil reinforced the economics of automobiles, trucking, aviation, and mechanized production.

Metals were tied to industrial rebound and construction. Germany, the United States, Britain, Belgium, France, and Czechoslovakia were significant steel and machinery economies. League of Nations data show world pig-iron output rising from 78.508 to 86.414 million metric tonnes (+10.07%) and world steel from 93.400 to 101.717 million (+8.90%), while U.S. steel fell 6.96% with the automobile slowdown. Agricultural staples remained central to India, China, Indonesia, Argentina, Brazil, Canada, Australia, and Eastern Europe. Complete world production figures by commodity are Data not available.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Global employment data are not comparable across 1927 economies. Definitions of unemployment, labor-force participation, and informal work varied sharply, and many agrarian or colonial economies did not measure unemployment in modern terms. Industrial countries had clearer labor statistics, but still used different methods: the French series covers only wage and salary earners in manufacturing, mining and construction, and Italian registered counts differ from its reconstructed rate. In Italy, approximate counts of wholly unemployed persons rose from 181,493 at the end of 1926 to 414,283 at the end of 1927.

The largest demographic-economic facts were the scale of India, the Former USSR, Indonesia, and Japan, and the continuing urbanization of industrial countries. India had an MPD 2023 population estimate of about 326.4 million. The Former USSR had about 166.1 million. The United States had about 119.5 million. Indonesia had about 59.5 million. Japan had about 61.4 million.

Labor-market structure diverged strongly. The United States and Canada had rising consumer-durable and service employment. Britain had persistent structural unemployment in coal, shipbuilding, and export textiles. Germany had rationalization and industrial recovery but financial fragility. Japan had an industrial labor force linked to textiles, heavy industry, and zaibatsu networks but also a large rural base. India and Indonesia remained overwhelmingly agricultural.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States was the core economy of 1927. Its output scale, gold reserves, securities markets, and capital exports made it the anchor of the interwar financial system. Yet 1927 was not an uncomplicated boom in real activity. The American economy experienced a mild downturn from late 1926 into 1927. Automobile production weakened sharply because Ford suspended production during the changeover from the Model T to the Model A, and that weakness spilled into iron and steel.

Financial markets told a different story. Equities rose strongly, credit was available, and New York's role in international lending deepened. The policy question, debated by later historians, is whether Federal Reserve easing in 1927 was a sensible response to domestic softness and international gold-standard pressures or a contribution to asset-market excess. Both interpretations have evidence. What is not in doubt is that the United States combined unmatched industrial scale with rising financial leverage.

Federal Reserve data show the shift toward securities credit directly. December-average loans on securities at reporting member banks rose from USD 5,694 million in 1926 to USD 6,594 million in 1927 (+15.81%), while other loans, a category that includes real-estate credit, fell from USD 8,776 million to USD 8,739 million (-0.42%). The New York discount rate was cut from 4% to 3.5% effective 6 August, but from November onward open-market purchases no longer fully offset gold exports. The December-average estimated monetary gold stock fell from USD 4,484 million to USD 4,416 million (-1.52%). League of Nations production tables show U.S. steel output falling from 49.069 to 45.656 million metric tonnes (-6.96%) and motor-vehicle output from 4.301 million to 3.401 million (-20.93%).

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Ford introduced the Model A late in 1927 after the Model T era ended; General Motors' multi-brand and annual-model strategy showed the direction of consumer competition; radio manufacturing and broadcasting continued to grow; aviation received a demand and publicity shock from Lindbergh's transatlantic flight. **Economic narrative:**

Germany's 1927 rebound made it the second-largest economy in the MPD2023 annual ranking among countries with available data. The economy benefited from stabilization after the 1923 hyperinflation, industrial rationalization, and foreign lending under the Dawes Plan environment. Heavy industry, chemicals, electrical equipment, transport equipment, and urban construction all mattered.

The dominant financial event was the Reichsbank's intervention against stock-market speculation. In May 1927, pressure on Berlin banks to reduce margin credit triggered a sharp market fall. Later work argues that this intervention may have damaged investment and confidence. In 1927 itself, officials were concerned with foreign capital inflows, speculative credit, and exchange stability.

Berlin banks announced restrictions on securities credit on 12 May, and the stock market broke on 13 May. Six large Berlin banks reported October deposits of RM 6,872 million against RM 5,517 million a year earlier (+24.56%) and miscellaneous loans of RM 5,042 million against RM 3,787 million (+33.14%); these banks are a sample, not the whole system. **Micro/sectoral highlights:** Large industrial combines and cartels were central; chemicals, steel, coal, and electrical engineering were key sectors; banking was tied closely to industrial finance; household consumption was recovering but still constrained by postwar instability and reparations politics. ### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Sectoral employment was shifting toward manufacturing, transport, clerical work, finance, retail, and services in high-income economies. ### 6C. Currencies

Major currencies were organized around gold-linked parities or stabilization programs. The dollar and sterling were gold-linked; sterling averaged 486.1024 US cents in New York, almost unchanged from 485.8235. The French franc was stabilizing before formal legal stabilization in 1928; its annual average rose 21.01% against the unstable 1926 average. The German reichsmark was stabilized after hyperinflation at about 23.76 US cents. The Italian lira was defended at a high value, its annual average rising 32.57%, while the Brazilian milreis averaged 17.99% less than in 1926. Belgium's quoted unit changed to the belga. Japan had not yet returned to gold. Many colonial economies used sterling, guilder, or other metropolitan links. Capital controls varied by country; a complete table is Data not available.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The clearest realized crisis was Japan's Showa Financial Crisis. It combined corporate overextension, bank weakness, political strain, and incomplete restructuring from the postwar boom. Bank of Japan assets to the private sector rose from 230.122 million yen on 12 March to 1,289.779 million yen on 14 May and were still 910.396 million yen on 17 December, so lower note issue after the spring did not mean the underlying credit problem had disappeared. Its forward impact was consolidation and more concentrated banking power.

Germany's Black Friday in May 1927 was a financial-market shock rather than a full macro crisis. The Reichsbank's pressure on margin credit ended the equity boom. Hindsight warning: this intervention may have weakened investment and confidence, but contemporaries saw it as a response to speculation and foreign-capital concerns.

The United States asset boom was a vulnerability in hindsight. In 1927, strong stock prices were visible, but the 1929 crash and Great Depression were not predetermined. The policy mistake debate remains unsettled: some historians emphasize Fed easing and speculative credit, while others stress later tightening, gold-standard constraints, banking failures, and real shocks.

Gold-standard fragility was the largest systemic risk. Countries with surplus gold positions and countries defending overvalued parities created asymmetric adjustment pressures. The mechanism became catastrophic after 1929, but in 1927 it looked like stabilization. Italy's revaluation coincided with an output contraction of 1.81%, consumer-price deflation of 8.57% and a sharp rise in recorded unemployment.

Commodity-export dependence was another risk. Argentina, Brazil, Canada, Australia, Indonesia, Mexico, and many non-top-20 economies depended on external prices and finance; Australia's current-account deficit reached 12.10% of GDP in the Macrohistory Database. The tail risk was a simultaneous fall in commodity prices, trade, and credit. That risk materialized after 1929.

Political risks included China's fragmentation, Soviet policy radicalization, European reparations and debt tensions, and colonial unrest. Their economic effects included uncertainty over customs revenue, trade routes, investment, labor relations, and state spending.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1927 vs. 1926)

The biggest ranking change was Germany's move from third to second and the United Kingdom's move from fourth to third, pushing India from second to fourth among countries with MPD 2023 data. Germany's estimated GDP rose from 365.6 billion to 402.2 billion 2011 international dollars. The United Kingdom rose from 355.9 billion to 384.5 billion. India was almost unchanged at about 367.2 billion.

The 1926 British coal stoppage and General Strike created a low base, making 1927 look like a rebound year: British growth swung from -3.67% to +8.05% in the MPD 2023 series and League steel output more than doubled. Germany's recovery was also strong. Canada, Spain, Argentina, Czechoslovakia, Switzerland, Brazil, and Indonesia show high estimated growth in MPD 2023. France, Italy, Mexico, and India show estimated declines or stagnation, and U.S. growth slowed from 5.88% to 0.39%. World export volume rose 6.48% against 4.47% in nominal value.

Policy-wise, 1927 shifted the focus from obvious postwar crisis repair to late-cycle management. Currency stabilization was no longer only emergency policy; it became a regime. The Bank of England cut Bank Rate from 5% to 4.5%, the Federal Reserve eased from 4% to 3.5% at New York, France cut its discount rate from 6.5% at end-1926 to 4% at end-1927, Germany and the Netherlands raised their rates by one point, and Italy defended the lira. Japan moved in the opposite direction through banking crisis.

Microeconomically, the United States automobile sector changed regime. Ford's Model T era ended, the Model A arrived, and the industry moved toward more differentiated competition; U.S. motor-vehicle output fell from 4.301 million to 3.401 million. Aviation, radio, and electrical equipment gained attention. In Europe, rationalization and cartelization were more important than consumer-durable mass markets.

New risks that emerged or intensified in 1927 included securities-market leverage, cross-border capital dependence, bank balance-sheet opacity, and gold-standard asymmetry. Risks that partly resolved included immediate postwar hyperinflation and some European currency instability. But the resolution was incomplete and often created new constraints.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

**Most reliable:** MPD 2023 GDP per capita, population, and derived GDP for countries with non-missing 1927 observations; Bank of England Bank Rate; dated official discount rates and New York annual-average exchange quotations from the Federal Reserve; dated Federal Reserve bank-credit samples; broad identification of Japan's Showa Financial Crisis and the dated Bank of Japan balance-sheet statements; the existence and policy purpose of the League of Nations World Economic Conference; United States Federal Reserve annual-report narrative for automobile and industrial weakness; League of Nations physical-production tables.

**Moderate confidence:** Country narratives for industrial structure, trade dependence, and policy regime. These are grounded in economic-history literature but often lack annual 1927 quantification. Real GDP growth rates in the country tables are MPD-derived estimates, not official national-account growth rates. Macrohistory Database consumer prices, unemployment, debt, current-account and budget ratios, consumption, credit, house prices and equity returns are reproducible reconstructions whose historical accuracy varies by country; budget ratios combine fiscal-year accounts with calendar-year GDP, and unemployment definitions differ (France covers only industrial wage and salary earners).

**Low or low-medium confidence:** Former USSR GDP and growth; colonial GDP estimates for India and Indonesia; Brazil and Mexico annual levels; all country rankings near the bottom of the top 20; any direct comparison of unemployment rates across countries; the German current-account ratio, whose nominal GDP denominator is linked to Ritschl-Spoerer GNP growth; detailed equity returns outside the United States.

**Conflicts and limitations:** The MPD 2023 full data sheet has no 1927 China observation with both GDP per capita and population. Other Maddison-based and historical estimates often place China among the world's largest economies in this era. Therefore, the top-20 table is a top-20 ranking among countries with MPD 2023 full-data availability, not a claim that China was economically small. The MPD estimates use 2011 international dollars, while some older Maddison sources use 1990 international dollars. Figures across vintages are not directly interchangeable. The Former USSR record's raw MPD change of +6.40% is not shown as growth because its source territory and national-income lineage are not matched. The Bank of Japan's 5.48% end-year discount rate and the Macrohistory annual discount series of 8.14% do not reconcile, and the reason for the difference is unresolved; Germany's 7% Reichsbank rate likewise differs from the Macrohistory annual short rate of 7.26%. The British equity price change is +4.20% in the Macrohistory series and +6.64% in the Campbell-Grossman-Turner all-share index, reflecting different coverage. The early Belgian current-account field in the Macrohistory Database equals the merchandise trade balance, so no Belgian current-account ratio is shown, and the change of Belgium's quoted unit to the belga prevents an exchange-rate comparison. The Australian exchange-rate series in the Macrohistory Database is restated in later Australian dollars, so no 1927 pound quotation is shown. The French and Brazilian annual-average exchange changes are dominated by the 1926 base and do not describe movements within 1927. Indian price and fiscal figures refer to British India administrative accounts, while the GDP record covers undivided India.

**Data not available:** comparable global CPI inflation, standardized unemployment rates, world GDP annual total and growth, a world net capital-flow matrix, reserve-currency shares, national current-account ratios outside the Macrohistory countries, fiscal deficit and debt ratios for colonial and Latin American economies, corporate revenue rankings, global market-cap rankings, IPO and M&A aggregates, exchange-level index levels for most markets, yield curves and spreads, defense production, healthcare output and R&D spending. Data not available means that no observation with the stated definition and territory is available for 1927, not that no historical record exists.

**Weakest claims:** Bottom-of-table rank ordering, especially Austria, Switzerland, Sweden, Hungary, Brazil, and Mexico; Soviet aggregate growth; annual real GDP changes for colonial economies; qualitative corporate-sector statements where no standardized 1927 corporate dataset exists.
