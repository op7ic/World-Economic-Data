# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1921

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1921 was defined by the global aftershock of World War I: a violent transition from wartime inflation and shortages to peacetime deflation, inventory liquidation, falling commodity prices, and labor-market stress. The downturn was most visible in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Sweden, and other commodity-linked economies, but the adjustment was global in scope. The U.S. recession reached its trough in July 1921 under the NBER chronology, while Britain faced a severe unemployment crisis and continental Europe struggled with reparations, reconstruction, currencies, and fiscal exhaustion. The volume of world exports fell 16.07% in the Federico-Tena reconstruction, the clearest single measure of the global contraction.

The year was not only a recession year. It was also a policy-regime transition. Central banks that had tightened after the wartime inflation began to ease as prices and credit demand fell. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York cut its commercial-paper discount rate in five steps from 7 percent to 4.5 percent between May and November. The Bank of England reduced Bank Rate from 7 percent to 5 percent by December, even as policymakers still aimed to restore sterling credibility. In Russia and the territories later grouped as the USSR, the New Economic Policy began a partial return to market exchange after War Communism, one of the largest ideological and institutional economic pivots of the early twentieth century.

The central data problem for 1921 is that modern national accounts did not exist for many economies. This assessment uses Maddison Project Database 2023 as the main GDP ranking source and cross-checks against Maddison Database 2010. The GDP figures below are PPP estimates in 2011 international dollars, not nominal U.S. dollars. That is a major gap and means the top-20 ranking should be read as the top 20 among economies with annual 1921 MPD GDP estimates, with China explicitly unranked rather than guessed. The British record also changes territory in 1921, when the area of the later Irish Free State leaves the series, so its 1920-1921 change is not a comparable growth rate.

Economically, the most important surprise was the speed and severity of nominal deflation. Agricultural prices, wholesale prices, and some asset prices fell sharply, transferring stress to farmers, exporters, and debtors. Deflation was not universal: reconstructed consumer prices still rose 31.08% in Germany and 18.31% in Italy, and the Austrian crown collapsed. Yet the year also set up recovery: lower inventories, easier money, and restored private incentives in some regions created the base for the later 1920s expansion. With hindsight, 1921 also marked the start of several vulnerabilities that would matter later: German reparations and currency collapse, protectionist pressure, the fragility of restored gold-standard thinking, and dependence on external commodity cycles.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1921

**1. Postwar deflation and recession.** The most visible economic theme was the collapse of wartime inflation. The United States, Britain, Canada, Sweden, and many commodity exporters moved through falling prices, inventory liquidation, and weaker employment. In the United States, annual CPI inflation was -10.5% after +15.6% in 1920, and wholesale prices fell sharply. The forward linkage was the 1922-1923 recovery in several economies, but also political pressure from farmers and workers who bore the adjustment costs.

**2. Divergent nominal regimes.** A common fall in trade did not produce a common price path. Consumer prices fell 18.45% in Sweden and 19.42% in Spain, but rose 31.08% in Germany and 18.31% in Italy, while the Austrian crown reached 5,275 per dollar at the end of the year. Japan's Tokyo wholesale index fell 22.76% on annual averages yet ended December 1.80% above December 1920, so annual deflation coexisted with a late-year rebound.

**3. Hard-money restoration versus economic pain.** Policymakers wanted to restore credibility after wartime finance. In Britain this meant an eventual return to gold at the old parity; in the United States it meant maintaining the dollar's gold parity while letting prices fall. In practice, interest rates were cut during 1921 as the slump deepened. The longer-term consequence was the restored gold-standard system of the mid-1920s, which later proved brittle.

**4. Reparations, war debts, and unstable European currencies.** Germany's reparations burden became more concrete after the London Ultimatum. The Reparation Commission's schedule of 5 May set obligations of 132 billion gold marks in A-bonds (12 billion), B-bonds (38 billion) and conditional C-bonds (82 billion), with annual payments of 2 billion plus 26% of the value of German exports; the total was an authorization, not a 1921 cash transfer. France and Belgium wanted reparations to support reconstruction, while Germany faced fiscal and external-transfer pressures. These tensions linked diplomacy to exchange rates and inflation expectations. The forward linkage is direct: 1921 helped set the stage for the 1922-1923 German hyperinflation and later stabilization.

**5. NEP as a state-versus-market turning point.** Soviet Russia's New Economic Policy began in 1921. The decree of 21 March replaced grain requisitioning with a tax in kind, allowed the surplus remaining after tax to be exchanged locally, and allowed limited private trade and small-scale enterprise. The shift mattered because it showed that ideological command policies could be reversed under production stress. It also created a mixed economy that lasted until the late 1920s.

**6. Commodity exporters under pressure.** Canada, Argentina, Australia, Brazil, Mexico, Indonesia, and other export-linked economies were highly exposed to falling prices for agricultural goods, raw materials, and energy. The shock hit rural incomes, fiscal revenue, railway traffic, and bank credit. Outcomes still differed: reconstructed real output fell 10.78% in Canada but rose 5.02% in Australia. The forward linkage was growing pressure for tariffs, marketing boards, debt relief, and state support.

**7. Protectionism and migration restriction.** The United States enacted the Emergency Tariff Act and the Emergency Quota Law in 1921. These policies reflected the domestic politics of recession: protecting farmers and limiting labor-market competition were presented as emergency responses. The forward linkage was the higher-protection environment of the 1920s.

**8. Early consumer and technology transformations.** 1921 was not a high-technology boom year by modern standards, but it mattered for future productivity and consumer markets. Insulin was discovered in 1921 in Toronto laboratory work that began in May, with the first human treatment following in January 1922. Radio commercialization accelerated, RCA's transatlantic Radio Central station opened on 5 November, chain-store and standardized-service models spread, and mass production in automobiles and consumer goods continued to scale.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

United States: The New York Fed maintained a 7 percent commercial-paper discount rate until May 5, 1921, then reduced it to 6.5% on 5 May, 6% on 16 June, 5.5% on 21 July, 5% on 22 September and 4.5% on 3 November. The rate changes were accompanied by facility changes: the preferential rate on Treasury-certificate collateral was withdrawn on 5 February, discount rates were unified across collateral classes on 16 June, and Liberty and Victory bonds were valued at market rather than par as collateral from 1 July. Credit conditions moved from tight to easier, but the transition was painful because debt burdens rose in real terms during deflation.

United Kingdom: Bank Rate began the year around 7 percent and fell through 1921, reaching about 5.5 percent in the third quarter and 5 percent at the end of December. The exchange-rate context was shaped by the desire to restore sterling credibility, even though a full return to gold came later. The pound's New York monthly mean rose from 374.20 U.S. cents in January to 415.61 cents in December.

Germany and continental Europe: Monetary conditions were fragmented and heavily affected by reparations, fiscal weakness, and currency instability. Germany's mark depreciation became more consequential after the London Ultimatum: its New York monthly mean fell from 1.6000 U.S. cents in January to 0.5258 cents in December, while the Reichsbank discount rate was unchanged at 5%. December discount rates reported by the League of Nations were 5.5% in France (6% a year earlier), 6% in Italy and Spain (unchanged), 4.5% in the Netherlands (unchanged), 5% in Belgium (5.5%), 5.5% in Sweden (7.5%) and Czechoslovakia (6%), and 7% in Austria (5%). Yield-curve data are Data not available.

Japan: The Bank of Japan's commercial-bill discount rate stayed at 8.03%, unchanged from the end of 1920. Japan was adjusting after the 1920 financial panic and the postwar trade reversal.

Stock markets: The Dow Jones Industrial Average recovered in 1921 after the 1920 decline, with historical market tables showing a positive annual return and a year-end close near 81.1. Shiller's composite index, measured as December monthly averages, rose 7.34%, and the U.S. equity total return in the rate-of-return reconstruction was 14.10%. Elsewhere the reconstructed price indices fell 10.92% for the London all-share sample, 9.22% in Paris, 20.67% in Tokyo, 15.98% in Milan and 26.74% in Stockholm, while Berlin's paper-mark index more than doubled. Exchange-level index data for Toronto and Montreal are Data not available.

Bond markets and yield curves: The reconstructed U.S. long government yield fell from 5.08% in December 1920 to 4.37% in December 1921; the monthly path in Shiller's data is a straight-line interpolation between January anchors, so only the endpoints are informative. Long government yields fell in Britain (5.32% to 5.21%), Germany and the Netherlands, and rose in France, Italy, Belgium, Australia and Spain. Comparable yield curves are Data not available. In Europe, sovereign and currency risk were heavily intertwined with war debts and reparations.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The main trade-policy shift was the U.S. Emergency Tariff Act of May 27, 1921, which imposed temporary duties on selected agricultural products for an initial six months, including 35 cents per bushel on wheat and 20% ad valorem on flour. The law reflected farmer distress and falling commodity prices. The Emergency Quota Law of 1921, enacted on 19 May with its main restrictions effective 3 June, limited annual admissions of each nationality to 3% of the foreign-born of that nationality in the 1910 census, with significant exemptions, and had labor-market and demographic consequences.

The most important geopolitical-economic issue was the postwar settlement. Germany's reparations, Allied war debts, French and Belgian reconstruction claims, and British fiscal restraint all influenced capital flows and exchange-rate expectations. The London schedule of 5 May required an initial payment of 1 billion gold marks within 25 days; on 23 June the British Chancellor told the Commons it had been met partly in cash and partly in three-month bills. The Washington Conference on the Limitation of Armament opened on 12 November 1921 and ran to 6 February 1922; its naval treaties were signed in 1922. In March the U.S. Secretary of State endorsed international consortium lending to China for railways, canals and other public utilities, an approval rather than a disbursement. Regional integration institutions such as the EEC and ASEAN did not exist. Trade was still organized through empires, bilateral relations, shipping networks, and national tariff systems.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil prices weakened sharply in 1921 after the postwar boom. U.S. crude production remained important and oil demand was increasingly linked to automobiles, trucks, and mechanized industry. Mexican crude output rose from 157.1 million to 193.4 million barrels (+23.13%) in the official historical statistics, showing that physical production and export income moved differently. The price decline stressed producers but supported later energy consumption.

Agricultural commodities were central to the recession. Wheat, cotton, wool, meat, coffee, and sugar markets were affected by the fall in wartime demand, restored production, and tighter credit. English Gazette wheat fell 9.72% on annual averages, but retail wheat prices in India rose 16.13%, so the pass-through to households differed sharply by market. Canada, Argentina, Australia, Brazil, India, and the Dutch East Indies were directly exposed.

Metals and minerals also weakened as construction and industrial demand slowed. Chilean copper fell 28.67% and Newcastle steam coal 43.28% on annual average quotations. Coal remained the dominant industrial fuel in Europe and Japan, while oil was becoming more important in transport and industry.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor markets were disrupted by demobilization, wage cuts, industrial disputes, and weak export demand. Britain experienced a major unemployment crisis in 1921, with coal and heavy industry especially affected; the Macrohistory Database unemployment series rises from 2.0% to 11.3%. U.S. unemployment estimates differ by method, but all show a sharp deterioration from 1920: Lebergott's reconstruction rises from 4.0% to 11.9%, or from 1.67 million to 5.01 million unemployed. Reconstructed unemployment also rose in Sweden (2.2% to 8.3%), the Netherlands (3.2% to 5.7%) and Australia (3.4% to 5.8%), while Germany's inflationary rebound kept its reconstructed rate at 1.2%.

Migration policy changed materially. The U.S. Emergency Quota Law reduced immigration inflows and marked a shift toward numerical restriction. World population grew 0.71% in the Federico-Tena reconstruction. Urbanization continued in many economies, but 1921 was not a single demographic turning point globally. **Economic narrative:**

The United States was still the largest economy by a wide margin, but 1921 was the trough year of the sharp postwar recession. The NBER chronology places the U.S. contraction from January 1920 to July 1921. The MPD 2023 GDP estimate shows a real GDP decline of about 2.9 percent from 1920 to 1921, while consumer prices fell 10.5 percent and nominal GDP 16.7 percent. This was a hard nominal adjustment rather than a modern demand-management cycle: prices, wages, inventories, farm incomes, and credit conditions all reset after the wartime boom. The contemporary Federal Reserve manufacturing index averaged 85.9 in 1921 against 98.0 in 1920 (-12.35%), and Lebergott's unemployment estimate tripled.

Policy moved from restraint to easing during the year. The New York Federal Reserve Bank kept a 7 percent commercial-paper discount rate until May 5, 1921, then reduced rates in steps to 4.5 percent by November as market rates and commodity prices fell, while also changing the terms on which Treasury and Liberty-bond collateral was accepted. Net gold imports of USD 667 million added to bank reserves. Fiscal policy also moved toward peacetime retrenchment, and the federal debt stock declined slightly between June observations even as the debt ratio rose. The year is remembered by some economists as a rapid liquidation and recovery episode, but that interpretation is contested because the social costs for farmers, workers, and indebted households were substantial.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Farm prices collapsed, inventories were liquidated, and many rural banks and borrowers were under stress. The Emergency Tariff Act of May 27, 1921 protected selected agricultural products, including a duty of 35 cents per bushel on wheat. Manufacturing moved from wartime and postwar overstocking toward lower prices and renewed output. Real consumption per person fell 6.53% in the Barro-Ursúa reconstruction and nominal bank loans fell 5.65% (Macrohistory Database). The national nominal house-price index fell 0.88%, while newspaper asking-price indices rose 0.24% in New York City and 2.63% in Boston and asking rents fell 6.65% in New York but rose 12.98% in Boston. Consumer business models were changing at the margin: White Castle was founded in 1921, radio commercialization accelerated after the 1920 broadcast breakthrough, RCA opened its Radio Central transmitting station on Long Island on 5 November with two 200-kW alternators, and the automobile industry continued to scale through Ford, General Motors, and related suppliers.

**Economic narrative:**

India, under British rule, ranked second among economies with MPD annual estimates. Its aggregate size came from population, not high income: GDP per capita was only about 1,082 in 2011 international dollars. India was tied to sterling finance, imperial procurement patterns, railways, jute, cotton textiles, tea, and grain markets. The London Ultimatum of May 1921 clarified Allied reparations demands and required payments in gold or foreign currency. ### 5C. Innovation & technology

The most important 1921 innovation with long-run economic implications was the discovery of insulin by researchers at the University of Toronto. Laboratory work began in May 1921, and the first successful human treatment followed in January 1922. Its immediate economic effect was limited by the early stage of production and clinical use, but the long-run effect on health, labor supply, life expectancy, and the pharmaceutical industry was enormous.

Radio commercialization was moving from demonstration to business infrastructure. KDKA's 1920 broadcast is usually treated as a key starting point, and the early 1920s saw rapid station growth. RCA's Radio Central station linked transmitting, receiving and traffic offices for international messages from November 1921. In manufacturing, automobiles, electrical equipment, chemicals, and machine tools continued incremental improvements rather than one single 1921 breakthrough.

### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Labor-market shifts were dominated by demobilization, unemployment, wage cuts, and industrial conflict. The slump hit coal, shipbuilding, textiles, agriculture, and export manufacturing especially hard. Skilled workers in engineering and manufacturing faced cyclical layoffs, while agricultural workers and farmers faced income compression through price declines. Reconstructed unemployment reached 11.9% in the United States (Lebergott), 11.3% in Britain and 8.3% in Sweden, while sectoral employment rose in some places, such as British-India cotton mills.

Union and labor movements remained powerful in Britain, Italy, Germany, and parts of the United States. In Italy, labor conflict was part of a broader political crisis. In Britain, coal was the central flashpoint. Immigration restriction in the United States, through quotas of 3% of each nationality's foreign-born population in 1910, changed labor supply dynamics and signaled a new political economy of borders.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The active crisis of 1921 was the postwar deflationary recession. It combined falling prices, tighter real debt burdens, weak employment, and inventory liquidation. In the United States the downturn officially ended in July, but farm distress and debt problems persisted. In Britain, unemployment and coal-sector conflict were severe. Falling nominal GDP raised debt ratios even where debt stocks were flat or falling: U.S. federal debt declined between June observations while its ratio to GDP rose 5.04 points.

Europe's systemic risk was the unresolved war-finance problem. Germany owed reparations; France and Belgium expected reparations to support reconstruction; Britain and France had war debts; the United States was the strongest creditor. This was not only a diplomatic issue. It affected exchange rates, budgets, central-bank policy, and private expectations. The distinction between the 132 billion gold marks authorized and the cash, bills and deliveries actually transferred meant that currency demand could move well before fiscal receipts were realized.

Concentrated bank exposures were a second channel. The Banca Italiana di Sconto's lending to Ansaldo showed how losses at a single industrial client could trigger deposit withdrawals and a bank closure, and how aggregate loan growth could hide poor asset quality.

The Former USSR faced a political-economic survival crisis after civil war, famine conditions, and production collapse. The New Economic Policy was a response to systemic failure in provisioning and incentives.

Hindsight risk: The 1921 reparations and currency situation in Germany was an early warning for the hyperinflation of 1922-1923. That full outcome was not mechanically inevitable in 1921, but the vulnerabilities were visible.

Hindsight risk: The attempt to restore prewar gold-standard norms helped create a fragile interwar financial architecture. That fragility would matter in the late 1920s and early 1930s, but in 1921 many policymakers still saw gold restoration as credibility, not as a future constraint.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1921 vs. 1920)

The biggest change from 1920 to 1921 was the transition from inflationary postwar boom conditions to deflationary recession. In the United States, CPI inflation moved from +15.6% in 1920 to -10.5% in 1921, and Lebergott's unemployment estimate rose 7.9 points to 11.9%. Wholesale and commodity prices dropped, and world export volume fell 16.07%. Credit conditions began tight and shifted toward easing as the downturn deepened. Germany and Italy were the main exceptions, with consumer-price inflation slowing but still positive.

GDP rankings changed in ways that reflect real shocks, territorial changes and data gaps. The United States remained number one. India moved from third to second and Germany from fourth to third, above the United Kingdom, whose 1921 record no longer includes the territory of the later Irish Free State; Maddison 2010 reverses the Germany-Britain ordering. Former USSR fell from seventh to eighth among available estimates and Italy rose from eighth to seventh. Canada fell from twelfth to fourteenth because of a severe contraction. Argentina rose from 13 to 11, the Netherlands from 15 to 12, Belgium from 14 to 13, Czechoslovakia from 17 to 15, Spain from 11 to 10, Mexico from 18 to 17 and Brazil from 19 to 18, while France, Japan, Indonesia and Australia kept their ranks. Austria (from 22) and Sweden (from 21) entered the available-estimate top 20, while Switzerland fell to twenty-first and Poland became unrankable for 1921 because GDP per capita is missing. These rank comparisons exclude China, which has no 1921 GDP estimate.

Policy changed from restraint toward easing. The New York Fed cut its discount rate from 7% to 4.5%, and December discount rates were lower than a year earlier in Britain (5% against 7%), France, Belgium, Sweden and Czechoslovakia, unchanged in Germany, Italy, Spain, the Netherlands and Japan, and higher in Austria (7% against 5%). In trade and migration, the United States moved toward protection and restriction through the Emergency Tariff Act and Emergency Quota Law. In Russia, the NEP began a structural pivot away from War Communism. In Germany, the London Ultimatum made reparations more concrete and intensified the link between fiscal capacity and currency expectations.

The new risks that emerged were currency instability, political radicalization in some European economies, agricultural debt distress, bank-industry contagion of the Sconto type, and the political appeal of protectionism. The risks that partly resolved were the immediate wartime shortages and the peak of the inflationary surge. The world economy did not return to the prewar order; it entered an unstable interwar transition.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

Highest-confidence data in this document: the existence and date range of the U.S. business-cycle contraction; U.S. CPI and wholesale-price deflation; Federal Reserve and Bank of England rate changes and the League of Nations December discount rates; the U.S. Emergency Tariff Act and Emergency Quota Law and their statutory terms; the reparations schedule of 5 May; the timing and institutional content of the New Economic Policy; Federal Reserve Board monthly exchange-rate means; and official statistical tables for India and Mexico.

Medium-confidence data: MPD 2023 GDP rankings and growth rates for countries with complete annual GDP per capita and population entries; the Macrohistory Database consumer-price, unemployment, debt, external-balance and fiscal series; the Federico-Tena trade, population and commodity-price reconstructions; and the reconstructed equity, bond and house-price returns. These are retrospective reconstructions, not contemporary national accounts. They are useful for relative scale and direction, but they should not be treated like modern national accounts. Fiscal ratios combine fiscal-year budgets with calendar-year GDP, and debt definitions and dates differ by country.

Low-confidence or high-uncertainty data: Former USSR GDP, Czechoslovakia and Austria after postwar boundary changes, colonial economies such as India and the Dutch East Indies, and countries where population and income series are reconstructed from sparse historical data. For Soviet territories and other disrupted economies, uncertainty can easily be very large. The former-USSR record rests on a national-income reconstruction whose territory has not been matched to the MPD population series, so its -9.7% change is not shown as growth, consistent with the 1920 report.

Territorial and vintage breaks: the MPD 2023 United Kingdom record includes the whole of Ireland in 1920 but only the reduced territory in 1921 (population 46.8 to 44.1 million), so the published -8.9% change is not British growth and is withheld; the territory change also contributes to Britain's fall from second to fourth rank. Germany is recorded on its 1936 frontiers, and the Eastern European records use later borders, so partial territorial overlaps are possible. The 1920 report in this series ranks China second with a GDP level, but MPD 2023 has no 1921 Chinese GDP per capita; the 1921 ranks and rank changes are therefore computed among economies with estimates in both years, excluding China.

Source conflicts: MPD 2023 and Maddison 2010 differ in the 1921 Germany versus United Kingdom ranking. MPD 2023 ranks Germany slightly above the United Kingdom, while Maddison 2010 places the United Kingdom slightly above Germany. This document uses MPD 2023 because it is the newer Maddison Project release, while explicitly flagging the conflict; the two vintages share lineage and do not provide independent corroboration. U.S. unemployment is 11.9% in Lebergott's 1957 estimates, 11.7% in the Macrohistory Database and lower in alternative reconstructions. British unemployment, debt, current-account and budget ratios differ between the Macrohistory Database (11.3%, 158.4%, +3.76% and a -2.18% fiscal-year central balance) and the Bank of England millennium dataset (11.02%, 160.35%, +3.71% and -1.48% calendar-year public-sector net lending); the former is shown because its inputs are documented year by year. The Bank of Japan's 8.03% commercial-bill rate differs from the 9.2% short rate in the Macrohistory Database, and the difference is unexplained. The Macrohistory Database's Belgian current-account field is a merchandise trade balance, so it is not shown as a current account; Australia's reconstructed external deficit of 17.33% of GDP is unusually large and should be read with caution. Canadian unemployment changes source between 1920 and 1921.

Major missing data: China lacks a 1921 GDP per capita entry in MPD 2023 Full data, so its aggregate GDP is Data not available here despite its large population and historical importance. Poland has a 1920 GDP per capita entry but not a 1921 entry. Turkey, Egypt, South Africa, and several other important economies also lack complete annual 1921 data. Global GDP, capital flows, reserve composition and a world inflation or unemployment rate are not available in a consistent global 1921 framework. For India, the Dutch East Indies, the Former USSR, Mexico, Brazil, Argentina, Czechoslovakia and Austria most macro ratios are Data not available because no national price index, labor-force measure or nominal GDP denominator with a documented definition is available. Data not available means that no observation with the stated definition and territory was found for 1921, not that no historical record exists.

Unemployment comparability: Unemployment definitions differed widely, and many countries had no modern labor-force survey. Where estimates are used, they should be read as historical reconstructions, not directly comparable modern rates. French unemployment in the Macrohistory Database covers only wage earners in manufacturing, mining and construction and is therefore not shown as a national rate.

Corporate and microeconomic data: Modern-style global corporate rankings, IPO lists, M&A databases, bank balance-sheet comparables, and real-estate transaction data are Data not available. The corporate section therefore identifies important firms and business models qualitatively rather than fabricating rankings. Exchange-level index data for Toronto and Montreal are Data not available, and the Berlin, Tokyo, Amsterdam and smaller-market returns are reconstructed samples without published index levels. Bank deposit series in the country sections cover restricted sets of banks and are not household saving rates.
