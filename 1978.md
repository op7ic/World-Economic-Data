# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1978

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1978 was a late-cycle expansion year in the industrial world, but it was also the year when the weaknesses of the post-1973 order became impossible to ignore. Output growth in the main industrial economies improved from the mid-1970s recession, but inflation, unemployment, and external imbalances remained stubborn. The United States expanded rapidly in real terms while tightening monetary policy in response to accelerating inflation and a weakening dollar. Japan and West Germany remained large surplus-oriented industrial powers, but both were under pressure to support domestic demand and reduce external imbalances.

The global monetary regime was still adjusting to the collapse of Bretton Woods. Major currencies floated or were managed against each other, and exchange-rate volatility was central to the 1978 policy environment. The dollar came under severe pressure during the year. The Federal Reserve and U.S. Treasury responded on 1 November with a dollar-support program, a one-point increase in the discount rate to 9.5 percent, foreign-currency intervention resources of up to $30 billion through swaps, IMF drawings, SDR sales and foreign-currency securities, and higher reserve ratios. The program was neither a fiscal stimulus nor a return to a fixed gold parity; managed floating remained the operating framework. Japan, by contrast, cut its discount rate in March and the Bundesbank left its discount rate unchanged at 3 percent. In Europe, the European Council agreed in December 1978 to establish the European Monetary System, proposed to start on 1 January 1979; the exchange-rate mechanism actually began operating on 13 March 1979. It was an important step toward monetary coordination and, with hindsight, a distant institutional ancestor of the euro.

Energy markets looked deceptively calm for most of 1978. Nominal oil prices were broadly stable compared with the later shock, with WTI posted prices around $14.85 per barrel in the FRED monthly series and the US crude first-purchase price, a different wellhead-stage measure, averaging $9.00 per barrel, up 5.02 percent from 1977. Oil-price schedules announced by OPEC in December applied to 1979. Yet the political crisis in Iran began to affect oil production late in the year. The full second oil shock materialized in 1979, but its roots were already visible in 1978 through strikes, uncertainty, and speculative inventory behavior. World GDP in current U.S. dollars rose from $7.434 trillion to $8.804 trillion and world real growth from 3.94 percent to 4.14 percent. International bank lending, net of interbank redepositing, rose from $75 billion to $110 billion. Commodity prices more broadly weakened in the first half of 1978 and then rose strongly from mid-year into 1979, partly through dollar effects and partly through cyclical demand.

At the micro level, 1978 was a year of structural transition rather than only cyclical recovery. U.S. airline deregulation began to reshape transport economics. Intel introduced the 8086 microprocessor, which was not yet a macro event but became a foundational technology for later personal computing. China’s December 1978 Third Plenum shifted the state’s focus toward economic construction and reform and opening, one of the most important economic regime changes of the late twentieth century, although its communiqué preserved communes and production-team accounting and proposed agricultural price changes only from 1979. In Europe, industrial policy was dominated by steel, shipbuilding, coal, and employment stresses. In Latin America, Brazil and Mexico grew rapidly, but foreign borrowing and commodity dependence created vulnerabilities that became far more visible in the early 1980s.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1978

**1. Stagflation management replaced postwar demand management.** The core macroeconomic problem in 1978 was not simple recession. The industrial economies were expanding, but inflation remained high and labor-market slack had not disappeared. Policymakers faced the uncomfortable tradeoff of supporting recovery while preventing a renewed price spiral. The United States, United Kingdom, Italy, France, Canada, and several smaller European economies all faced combinations of price pressure, wage bargaining stress, and weak productivity growth. Japan and West Germany had lower inflation relative to some peers, but their surpluses added international pressure for domestic expansion.

The historical significance is that 1978 sits just before the decisive anti-inflation policy turn of 1979 to 1982. The year did not yet have the Volcker shock, but it contained the preconditions: dollar weakness, high U.S. inflation, central-bank concern, and loss of confidence in gradualist stabilization. The forward linkage is direct. By late 1979 and 1980, monetary tightening, oil-price increases, and recession risk became the defining global macro story.

**2. The dollar crisis and the search for monetary anchors.** After Bretton Woods, exchange rates became a policy variable rather than a fixed institutional foundation. In 1978, the dollar weakened sharply against the Deutsche mark, yen, and Swiss franc. The U.S. authorities responded in November with a program to defend the dollar, including a higher discount rate, reserve-ratio changes, and mobilized foreign-currency resources. From late May to end-October the dollar fell about 23 percent against the yen, 25.5 percent against the Swiss franc and 18.5 percent against the Deutsche mark. This was a signal that floating exchange rates still required official intervention when disorderly movements threatened inflation expectations and international confidence.

Europe’s response was institutional. The European Council agreed in December to create the European Monetary System, with a proposed start on 1 January 1979, the ECU at its center and an exchange-rate mechanism designed to reduce intra-European volatility, with proposed margins generally of ±2.25 percent and a wider option for currencies then floating; the mechanism actually began operating on 13 March 1979. The October snake realignment and Norway’s December departure from the snake were separate 1978 events. The countries most affected were West Germany, France, Italy, the Netherlands, Belgium, Denmark, Ireland, and the United Kingdom, although participation details differed. The forward linkage runs through the EMS, the Exchange Rate Mechanism, the Maastricht process, and eventually the euro.

**3. Energy calm before the second oil shock.** Oil prices did not yet display the full 1979 shock in most 1978 price series. OPEC price conditions were relatively stable, and non-OPEC production was increasing. But the Iranian revolution began late in 1978 and soon cut Iranian production sharply. The EIA and Federal Reserve History sources both frame the Iranian disruption as the key supply event behind the second oil shock, even while noting that speculative hoarding and fear of further disruption amplified price effects.

The BIS describes oil prices for most grades as broadly unchanged from July 1977 until late 1978, and OPEC’s December decision scheduled quarterly price increases for 1979. Oil exporters such as Saudi Arabia, Iran, Kuwait, Iraq, and the United Arab Emirates were directly affected, but the larger macro effect fell on oil-importing economies: the United States, Japan, Western Europe, India, and many developing countries. The forward linkage is the 1979 to 1980 energy-price surge, renewed inflation, current-account stress, and tighter monetary policy.

**4. East Asia’s industrial ascent and China’s policy inflection.** Japan remained the second-largest market economy by nominal GDP in the primary ranking and was a global leader in automobiles, electronics, precision manufacturing, and export discipline. Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Singapore were not all in the top 20 by nominal GDP, but their export-led growth models were central to the decade’s structural change. China, still poor on a per capita basis, recorded very rapid output growth in the national-accounts series and entered a decisive reform phase in December 1978.

The historical significance is that 1978 marks the transition from a Japan-centered East Asian industrial story to a broader regional story in which China eventually became central. The forward linkage is unusually strong: decollectivization experiments, special economic zones, foreign technology importation, and export manufacturing integration shaped the global economy from the 1980s onward.

**5. Developing-country growth financed by debt.** Brazil and Mexico were among the largest developing economies in 1978 and both grew strongly. Non-oil developing borrowers’ new international bank credit rose from $11.1 billion to $24.7 billion in the BIS reporting sample, although broader 1978 coverage exaggerates the comparison, and Brazil and Poland were among the largest takers. Mexico’s growth was tied to oil discoveries, state-led energy investment, and rising international borrowing. Brazil remained in a state-led industrialization model with large public enterprises, infrastructure, and external financing. India’s growth improved but remained constrained by controls, public-sector dominance, and low per capita income. China had rapid growth but from a low per capita base and with a still-planned economy.

The forward linkage is the early-1980s debt crisis. In 1978, international capital flows to middle-income borrowers looked like a development engine. With hindsight, rising U.S. interest rates, oil-price volatility, and currency risk turned those flows into a source of systemic vulnerability.

**6. Microeconomic liberalization began to appear inside still-interventionist economies.** The U.S. Airline Deregulation Act of 1978 was a major microeconomic reform. It shifted domestic air transport away from regulated routes and fares toward competition, while preserving safety regulation. The June US money-market certificate and the April approval of nationwide automated clearing-house settlement also changed banking practice. This was not yet the broad deregulation wave of the 1980s, but it was a clear example of a policy regime moving from administrative allocation toward market contestability.

Similar pressures were visible elsewhere, but in different forms. European countries were still managing steel, coal, shipbuilding, and employment through public intervention. China moved toward reform and opening, while still retaining a dominant state plan. Latin American economies relied heavily on public enterprises and directed credit. The forward linkage is the broader 1980s debate over deregulation, privatization, industrial policy, and state ownership.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The United States tightened during 1978. Federal Reserve annual-report records show successive discount-rate increases from 6 percent at end-1977 to 7 percent in May, 7.75 percent in August, 8.5 percent in October and 9.5 percent in November, the last approved on 1 November. December requests by some Reserve Banks for 9.75 and 10 percent were rejected, so the year-end rate was 9.5 percent. The November move was tied to a dollar-support program and anti-inflation credibility. U.S. monetary policy was therefore shifting from recovery support toward inflation and exchange-rate defense.

Japan and West Germany had stronger currencies and lower inflation pressure than many peers, but they were not free from policy dilemmas. Both faced pressure from the United States and other trading partners to support domestic demand and reduce surpluses. The Deutsche mark and yen strengthened against the dollar, affecting exporters and international policy bargaining. The Bank of Japan cut its commercial-bill discount and designated-collateral loan rate from 4.25 to 3.5 percent effective 16 March. The Bundesbank discount rate remained 3 percent and the Lombard rate 3.5 percent throughout the year.

The United Kingdom remained in an incomes-policy and industrial-relations regime, and Minimum Lending Rate ended the year at 12.5 percent against 7 percent at end-1977; until 25 May it was normally tied to Treasury-bill tender results and thereafter set administratively. Sterling was influenced by North Sea oil, fiscal policy, and confidence in anti-inflation policy. France and Italy faced weaker-currency and inflation concerns relative to West Germany. European monetary negotiations culminated in the December EMS agreement.

In the United States, the June money-market certificate, with a $10,000 minimum, a six-month term and a Treasury-bill-related ceiling, helped banks and thrifts compete for funds as market rates rose, although it did not remove mortgage-cost pressure.

Equity-market performance was uneven and often subdued in major advanced markets. U.S. stocks produced a positive total return but only a small price return, reflecting inflation, rising rates, and late-cycle valuation pressure. Bond markets faced upward yield pressure as inflation and policy tightening intensified. JST long-term rates were 8.41 percent in the United States, 6.09 percent in Japan, 5.79 percent in West Germany and 12.03 percent in the United Kingdom (see 6B).

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

Trade politics in 1978 were shaped by imbalances rather than by a single global tariff shock. The United States was concerned about deficits, dollar weakness, and import competition. Japan faced pressure over exports, especially automobiles and manufactured goods. West Germany faced similar surplus-country pressure. The European Community was simultaneously dealing with enlargement questions, EMS design, and industrial adjustment.

Cold War trade remained constrained by political division, technology controls, and planning systems. The Soviet Union and Eastern Europe maintained trade relationships inside Comecon and with Western suppliers for selected technology, grain, and industrial goods, but statistics are less comparable than for market economies. China’s reform turn at year-end opened the prospect of deeper trade and technology relationships, although the full effect came later.

Regional integration advanced in Europe and remained limited elsewhere. ASEAN existed but was not yet the deeply integrated economic bloc it later became. The EMS agreement was the most important regional monetary development of the year.

Indonesia devalued the rupiah on 15 November from Rp415 to Rp625 per US dollar, a 50.60 percent rise in rupiah per dollar and a 33.60 percent fall in the dollar value of the rupiah, to support labor-intensive non-oil exports and import substitution. Turkey cut its import volume by about 25 percent. Western bank credit to Eastern Europe, with Poland the largest taker at about $2.7 billion, showed that Cold War separation did not imply financial autarky.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil was the central strategic commodity. OPEC remained influential, but 1978 did not yet show the full price shock. The key change was political and supply-side: Iranian unrest began affecting production late in the year. EIA and Federal Reserve History sources identify the revolution-related Iranian production collapse as a key trigger of the 1978 to 1979 oil shock.

Metals and minerals followed cyclical and exchange-rate forces. The BIS notes that primary commodity prices fell in late spring and early summer and then rose strongly through April 1979. Countries most exposed included Canada, Australia, Brazil, South Africa, Chile, Zambia, and oil-exporting Middle Eastern economies. Banco de México reports New York copper down 1.1 percent but London copper up 5.2 percent in 1978; the BIS Economist non-oil commodity index rose more than 20 percent from mid-1978 to April 1979, a window that crosses years. ### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor markets in the advanced economies remained weak by postwar standards. The IMF highlighted high unemployment in industrial countries even while output was expanding. BIS December unemployment fell from 6.3 to 5.9 percent in the United States but rose from 2.1 to 2.3 percent in Japan and from 5.2 to 5.8 percent in France; Belgium’s rate uses insured persons rather than the civilian labor force as its denominator. This was a structural break from the early postwar period, when full employment was the default policy expectation in Western Europe and Japan. Youth unemployment and industrial unemployment were central concerns in Europe.

Demographically, the world population was about 4.29 billion (4.283 billion in the WDI aggregate). China and India together accounted for a very large share of humanity, but their per capita output remained low. Urbanization, migration to manufacturing centers, and the expansion of education shaped labor supply in East Asia and Latin America. In the United States, the baby boom cohort was moving through early adult labor markets, contributing to labor-force growth and housing demand.

Wage growth and productivity were a central conflict. In several countries, nominal wage bargaining attempted to protect workers from inflation, while productivity growth was weaker than in the 1950s and 1960s. This worsened unit labor cost pressure and pushed governments toward incomes policies, wage guidelines, or tighter money. BIS earnings rose 9.3 percent nominally and 0.3 percent in real terms in the United States, while Canadian earnings rose 6.8 percent nominally but fell 1.5 percent in real terms.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States combined strong real growth with intensifying inflation and exchange-rate stress. Demand was supported by employment growth, consumer spending, housing, and business investment, but the policy environment was shifting. The late-year dollar-support program and discount-rate increase signaled that the Federal Reserve was no longer willing to treat dollar weakness as a side issue. The Humphrey-Hawkins Act also reinforced the formal policy language around full employment and price stability, even though the tension between those goals was acute.

Maddison growth was 5.70 percent and current WDI growth 5.54 percent, while the Federal Reserve annual report described real GNP growth of about 4.5 percent over the four quarters of 1978, a different measure. The discount rate rose in seven approved steps from 6 percent to 9.5 percent at year-end, the November increase from 8.5 to 9.5 percent being approved on 1 November; December requests by some Reserve Banks for 9.75 and 10 percent were rejected. Annual CPI inflation was 7.6 percent and BIS December unemployment fell from 6.3 to 5.9 percent.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Microeconomic change was important. The Airline Deregulation Act of 1978 began a long transition from route and fare regulation toward competition in passenger air transport. The banking system had recovered from the 1974 to 1975 recession, although problem banks remained a supervisory concern. Technology was still small in macro share, but Intel’s 8086 introduction in June 1978 became a key foundation for the later x86 personal-computing ecosystem. Autos, housing, airlines, commercial banking, defense contractors, and semiconductors were key sectors to watch. The June money-market certificate, with a $10,000 minimum, a six-month term and a Treasury-bill-related ceiling, helped banks and thrifts compete for savings as market rates rose. Nationwide automated clearing-house net settlement was approved in April, and Regulation BB under the Community Reinvestment Act took effect on 6 November. BIS earnings rose 9.3 percent nominally and 0.3 percent in real terms.

**Economic narrative:**

The Soviet Union was one of the largest economies in the world, but 1978 measurement is highly uncertain. Output was organized through central planning, heavy industry, energy, defense, and state agriculture. Western estimates generally identify slowing productivity growth by the late 1970s, although the full scale of stagnation became clearer only retrospectively. ### 5C. Innovation & technology

The 8086 microprocessor, introduced on 8 June 1978, was the most visible technology event of the year. Its immediate 1978 economic effect was limited, but its architectural legacy became central to the IBM-compatible PC ecosystem and later computing history. This is a good example of a micro innovation whose macro importance became visible only in hindsight. Payments infrastructure also changed: in April the Federal Reserve approved nationwide automated clearing-house net settlement, despite concerns about projected costs and volumes.

Other innovation themes included industrial robotics in Japan, numerical control in machine tools, energy efficiency in autos and manufacturing, telecommunications switching, pharmaceuticals, and aerospace technology. R&D was concentrated in the United States, Japan, West Germany, France, the United Kingdom, and the Soviet military-industrial complex. Infrastructure megaprojects were important in energy and transport. Brazil continued large-scale infrastructure and energy development. Mexico expanded oil-related infrastructure. France continued nuclear-power investment. The UK’s North Sea oil infrastructure improved external prospects. China’s post-1978 reforms later unlocked major infrastructure growth, but that was not yet a 1978 macro outcome.

### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Labor markets reflected the end of the easy full-employment era. In the United States, unemployment was falling but inflation was rising: the BIS December rate fell from 6.3 to 5.9 percent, and earnings rose 9.3 percent nominally but only 0.3 percent in real terms. In Western Europe, unemployment remained high and politically salient; French December unemployment rose from 5.2 to 5.8 percent. In Canada, earnings rose 6.8 percent nominally but fell 1.5 percent in real terms. In Sweden, Belgium, the UK, Spain, and Italy, industrial restructuring made employment policy central. Japan had comparatively strong labor-market outcomes, but its system relied on enterprise employment practices and was not directly comparable to Western measures.

Sectoral employment shifted gradually away from agriculture in middle-income and advanced economies and toward services and manufacturing. China and India still had enormous rural labor forces. Brazil, Mexico, Spain, and Türkiye were urbanizing and industrializing, but informal employment complicated measurement. Union power remained significant in the United States, United Kingdom, Italy, France, Sweden, and West Germany, though institutional structures differed widely.

Education and skills were increasingly important for manufacturing competitiveness. Japan and West Germany benefited from strong vocational and firm-based training systems. The United States led in university-based science, computing, and business innovation. China’s reform period began with a major need to rebuild human capital after the disruptions of the Cultural Revolution.

### 6C. Currencies

The dollar weakened materially against the Deutsche mark, yen, and Swiss franc. The BIS reports that from late May to end-October the dollar fell about 23 percent against the yen, 25.5 percent against the Swiss franc and 18.5 percent against the Deutsche mark. On an annual-average basis the rate fell 21.6 percent to 210.44 yen per dollar and 13.5 percent to 2.0086 marks per dollar, appreciations of both currencies. The U.S. policy response in November made exchange-rate defense a central monetary-policy issue. The Federal Reserve and Treasury mobilized up to $30 billion of foreign-currency resources through swaps, IMF drawings, SDR sales and intended foreign-currency securities, raised the discount rate, and raised reserve ratios.

European currencies moved toward greater coordination. The EMS agreement in December created the framework for a European exchange-rate mechanism, which began operating on 13 March 1979. The ECU was designed as a denominator for exchange-rate and settlement functions. This was one of 1978’s most important institutional financial developments. Indonesia devalued the rupiah from Rp415 to Rp625 per dollar on 15 November.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The largest active risk was the transition from a stable-looking oil market to the second oil shock. In 1978 itself, the shock was not fully priced, but Iranian production disruption began late in the year. Hindsight shows that the oil shock was not only a supply event; fear, inventory behavior, and inflation expectations amplified it. The BIS discussion of Iranian output falling from over six million barrels per day to below one million spans the upheaval and early 1979, so it does not describe a calendar-1978 annual average.

The dollar crisis was a second major risk. A disorderly fall in the dollar threatened imported inflation for the United States, destabilized trade relationships, and complicated reserve management for foreign central banks. The November dollar-support program helped stabilize conditions, but it also showed that floating exchange rates did not eliminate the need for policy coordination.

A third risk was developing-country external debt. In 1978, bank lending to sovereign and quasi-sovereign borrowers was still treated as a growth-enabling capital flow. Non-oil developing borrowers’ new bank credit rose from $11.1 billion to $24.7 billion, although broader 1978 reporting coverage exaggerates the comparison; Brazil took more than half of the $14.9 billion lent to non-OPEC Latin America, and Poland took about $2.7 billion. Hindsight shows that rising dollar rates, oil-price changes, and refinancing needs created the early-1980s debt crisis. Brazil, Mexico, Argentina, Türkiye, and several smaller economies were exposed. For firms and households, imported-input costs, funding conditions and real earnings linked these macro risks to concrete decisions.

European industrial unemployment was a fourth vulnerability. Steel, shipbuilding, coal, and older manufacturing sectors faced overcapacity and social pressure. Governments used subsidies, public ownership, and labor-market programs to manage adjustment. This delayed some restructuring and increased fiscal burdens.

Finally, data opacity itself was a systemic problem. Non-market economies, many developing countries, and high-inflation economies had statistics that were either not comparable, not timely, or not market-convertible. Policymakers and investors were often operating with partial information.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1978 vs. 1977)

The main year-over-year change was that recovery became more visibly constrained by inflation and currency instability. Industrial-country output growth stayed around 4 percent, but confidence in a smooth post-1973 recovery weakened. World real growth rose from 3.94 to 4.14 percent and world nominal GDP from $7.434 trillion to $8.804 trillion, an increase that includes prices and exchange conversion. The United States accelerated in real terms, but this intensified inflation and dollar pressure.

The monetary regime shifted from passive floating toward active exchange-rate management. The U.S. year-end discount rate rose from 6 to 9.5 percent, Japan’s fell from 4.25 to 3.5 percent, the Bundesbank discount rate stayed at 3 percent, and Britain’s Minimum Lending Rate rose from 7 to 12.5 percent. The dollar’s weakness in 1978 forced the United States to defend the currency more explicitly. Europe moved from discussion toward institutional design for the EMS. Compared with 1977, 1978 had a clearer search for anchors.

Trade and finance expanded together. BIS nominal merchandise exports rose 15.21 percent while its differently covered export-volume index rose 4.33 percent, and net international bank lending rose from $75 billion to $110 billion. Mexico’s preliminary real GDP rose 7.0 percent, against 8.96 percent in current WDI, while its current deficit widened from $1,623.1 million to $2,611.3 million.

Energy moved from oversupply or stability toward looming disruption. Oil prices in 1978 were not yet the 1979 shock prices, but Iran changed the risk distribution. This is a classic case where the decisive macro event began before it appeared fully in annual price data.

China’s policy direction changed dramatically. In 1977, China was still emerging from the Cultural Revolution and Mao-era political succession. By December 1978, the leadership had formally shifted toward economic construction and reform and opening. This was not yet visible in world trade statistics, but it was one of the most important structural breaks of the year.

Around the top-20 cutoff, Argentina and Türkiye appeared large in nominal GDP terms but were structurally fragile. Iran fell under revolutionary disruption. Saudi Arabia remained close to the cutoff, with oil-sector volatility and source differences affecting rank. Poland and the German Democratic Republic illustrate the ranking problem for centrally planned economies: depending on whether one uses nominal GDP, World Bank Atlas GNP proxies, or Maddison-style PPP estimates, their apparent positions change materially.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

Data reliability is highest for the United States, Japan, West Germany, France, the United Kingdom, Italy, Canada, Australia, the Netherlands, Sweden, Switzerland, Belgium, and Spain. Their national accounts were produced inside established statistical systems, and World Bank or OECD-linked sources cross-reference them. Even for these countries, unemployment definitions, policy-rate series, public-debt ratios, and current-account ratios vary by source; Sweden, Switzerland and Belgium have no comparable 1978 macro indicators beyond GDP growth.

Data confidence is medium for Brazil, Mexico, India, Türkiye, Argentina, and China. These countries have official statistical series, but exchange controls, inflation, informality, administrative prices, and revisions reduce comparability. China’s 1978 data are especially sensitive because the economy was still planned and the reform transition had just begun. WDI exchange-rate series for Brazil, Mexico, Argentina and Türkiye are expressed in later redenominated currency units and are not shown.

Data confidence is low for the USSR and other Soviet-bloc economies. The World Bank Atlas explicitly derived centrally planned economy figures from official net material product data and warned about comparability. UNdata provides current-dollar GDP per capita for Former USSR, but official exchange rates and accounting concepts remain problematic. Maddison Project and OWID-Maddison series are better for real PPP-style historical comparisons, but those values are not directly convertible into nominal current U.S. dollar rankings.

Several growth figures conflict across sources. Italy’s 9.6 percent growth in a countryeconomy table conflicts with current WDI growth of 3.24 percent and Maddison growth of 3.73 percent, so the WDI figure is used. Australian growth is 0.90 percent in current WDI, 2.91 percent in Maddison and 4.1 percent in other national-accounts tables, reflecting fiscal-year and revision differences. Mexico’s contemporary Banco de México estimate of 7.0 percent differs from current WDI (8.96 percent), Maddison (8.83 percent) and the 8.2 percent in other national-accounts tables; Argentina’s central bank recorded a 4.2 percent fall against 4.51 percent in WDI. West Germany’s growth is shown from the WDI Germany series; Maddison’s 2.80 percent and 78.1 million population refer to the combined German territory, while the Bundesbank rates, public debt and current-account figures in the West Germany block refer to the Federal Republic. The record of December 1978 discount-rate requests at 9.75 and 10 percent shows they were rejected, so the U.S. year-end rate is 9.5 percent.

The weakest data concern: commodity price levels for agricultural staples, a full-year London gold average, country-specific CPI inflation and unemployment for the Soviet bloc, policy-rate levels outside the United States, Japan, West Germany and the United Kingdom, government debt ratios for several emerging economies, stock-index returns for most non-U.S. exchanges, credit spreads, household balance sheets, healthcare and defense sector output, and Soviet-bloc nominal ranking near the cutoff.

Data not available include: a complete 1978 top-20 GDP ranking from a single primary source that includes both market and non-market economies; a full global corporate revenue or market-cap ranking; comprehensive country-by-country sector shares; and comparable unemployment definitions for the full sample.

The most important source conflict is the treatment of centrally planned economies. World Bank Atlas GNP proxies can put Poland and the German Democratic Republic higher than market-economy nominal GDP sources suggest. The primary ranking therefore uses cross-checked current-dollar GDP where available and includes only the USSR among Soviet-bloc economies because its scale is clear across proxy methods. The Maddison PPP ranking described in section 4 produces a different order, adding Indonesia, Poland and Iran and dropping Sweden, Switzerland and Belgium.
