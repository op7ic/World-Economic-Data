# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1944

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1944 was the high-water mark of total-war economic organization and the year in which the postwar monetary order was designed. The dominant economic fact was not a normal business cycle but the allocation of labor, materials, finance, shipping, food, fuel, and industrial capacity under military and state direction. The United States reached extraordinary output levels and became the central supplier, financier, and reserve-currency anchor of the Allied economy. Europe and much of Asia, by contrast, were fragmented by occupation, liberation, transport disruption, rationing, and the destruction or diversion of productive capacity.

The most durable institutional event was the July 1944 Bretton Woods conference. Delegates from 44 countries agreed on the architecture that would become the International Monetary Fund and the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development. The agreement did not make 1944 a normal open-trade year. Capital controls, exchange controls, rationing, and blocked trade still dominated. Its importance was forward-looking: the dollar-gold anchor, fixed but adjustable exchange rates, and multilateral reconstruction finance created the framework within which postwar recovery, European reconstruction, and the early development agenda would operate.

The microeconomic structure of the world economy was just as distinctive as the macro picture. Defense-linked manufacturing, shipping, rail logistics, petroleum, metals, chemicals, public procurement, food processing, and communications were privileged sectors. Civilian automobiles, private housing, many consumer durables, normal retail choice, and private international finance were suppressed. Price controls and rationing meant that measured inflation understated shortages and quality decline in many economies, while black markets and monetary disorder were severe in several occupied or wartime economies.

The country ranking uses historical GDP estimates rather than modern rankings. The primary numerical top-20 table is based on the Maddison legacy 2010 annual GDP levels, expressed in million 1990 international Geary-Khamis dollars, cross-checked against the Maddison Project Database 2023 documentation and Our World in Data's Maddison-based GDP presentation. This is not a perfect global ranking. China has no annual 1944 GDP value in the Maddison annual series, although its 1938 and 1950 benchmarks show that it was almost certainly a major economy. Soviet and occupied-economy values are rough reconstructions and should be treated as high-uncertainty estimates. Independent indicators confirm the divergence: the War Production Board put U.S. munitions output at USD 61.3 billion, while the Federal Reserve's contemporary factory-and-mine index fell slightly and civilian consumption changed little; Italy's reconstructed consumer prices rose 344%, and Belgium blocked most of its money stock in October.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1944

**1. Total war replaced the market as the main allocation mechanism.** In the largest belligerents, production was not primarily guided by consumer demand, market prices, or profit-maximizing investment. It was guided by procurement orders, materials boards, labor direction, rationing, shipping priorities, and state finance. The United States War Production Board, for example, shifted from shortages of steel, copper, and aluminum toward bottlenecks in manpower and common components. The countries most affected were the United States, United Kingdom, Germany, Soviet Union, Japan, Canada, Australia, and occupied European economies. The forward linkage was reconversion: 1945 to 1947 would be dominated by the shift from state procurement to civilian consumption, housing, and private investment.

**2. Bretton Woods made the dollar the central postwar monetary anchor.** The conference at Bretton Woods in July 1944 agreed on the IMF and World Bank framework and on fixed but adjustable exchange rates centered on the U.S. dollar, with the dollar linked to gold at $35 per ounce. This did not instantly create convertibility or free capital markets. It created a planned postwar system designed to prevent competitive devaluations, trade collapse, and beggar-thy-neighbor policies. The most affected countries were the United States and United Kingdom, but the framework shaped the monetary choices of almost every postwar economy.

**3. Trade was logistical, political, and military rather than commercial.** Normal private trade was heavily displaced by lend-lease, convoy shipping, occupation systems, blocked ports, rationing, and strategic supply chains. Allied flows moved food, fuel, aircraft, vehicles, raw materials, and industrial goods. Axis trade was increasingly constrained by fuel, shipping, bombing, and territorial loss. Neutral countries such as Sweden, Switzerland, Spain, Argentina, and Turkey faced diplomatic pressure and supply restrictions. The postwar linkage was the desire to rebuild an open trading system, later formalized through GATT in 1947.

**4. Inflation was suppressed, displaced, or hidden.** Many official CPI measures were held down by price ceilings, rationing, subsidies, and quantity controls. This does not mean households faced normal purchasing power. Shortages, waiting lists, black markets, quality deterioration, and forced saving were widespread. The United States had relatively low measured CPI inflation in 1944, but that reflected controls. China, occupied Europe, and several disrupted economies experienced far more severe monetary and price instability, though comparable national CPI data are not available.

**5. Labor scarcity became a binding constraint in advanced war economies.** In the United States and Australia, unemployment was close to wartime lows. Women entered manufacturing, clerical, logistics, and public-service work at higher rates. Military service and war production competed for labor. In Germany and occupied territories, labor allocation included coercive systems that make ordinary labor-market statistics ethically and analytically non-comparable. The forward linkage was postwar demobilization, veteran employment, gender-role reversal pressures, and labor-union bargaining.

**6. Axis economies faced resource and logistics exhaustion.** Germany and Japan still had substantial measured output in 1944, but the limiting factors shifted toward fuel, transport, skilled labor, imported materials, and component shortages. This is why GDP alone is misleading. A large measured economy could be strategically brittle if it could not move inputs, fuel transport networks, or sustain household food supply. The postwar consequences were severe reconstruction needs and major institutional reforms in Germany and Japan.

**7. Development and decolonization pressures intensified.** India, China, Brazil, Mexico, Turkey, and other non-Western economies were not side notes. Wartime procurement, inflation, food stress, import substitution, labor migration, and state planning debates reshaped future development strategies. In India, wartime strain fed the political economy of independence. In Latin America, import constraints and wartime demand encouraged domestic industry and state-led development models.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

World GDP for 1944: Data not available as a complete aggregate. The Maddison Project and Our World in Data provide long-run historical GDP series and 1944 map presentations, but the Maddison 2010 legacy table lacks a world total for 1944, and the Maddison Project Database 2023 has no 1944 observations for China or the Soviet Union. World growth rate: Data not available. Global trade volume: Data not available in a comparable single annual series for 1944; qualitative evidence shows that trade was dominated by official logistics, lend-lease, military supply, rationed shipping, and blocked or occupied routes.

Reserve currency dynamics were in transition. The pound sterling still mattered through the sterling area, but the U.S. dollar became the designed anchor of the new system. The Bretton Woods agreement made the dollar-gold link central, while convertibility and normal private capital mobility remained future objectives rather than 1944 realities. Global inflation trends were uneven: open inflation was suppressed in the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, and several neutral economies, while monetary disorder and black-market prices were much more severe in China, occupied territories, and late-war Axis economies.

Commodity prices were largely administrative. Gold was fixed at $35 per troy ounce under the dollar-gold framework. A common historical crude-oil price series gives U.S. crude around $1.21 per barrel in 1944, but this is a posted-price historical series rather than a free global spot-market benchmark. Copper and many other metals were subject to wartime ceilings and allocation, so a single global market price is not meaningful. Agricultural staples were shaped by procurement, rationing, shipping constraints, and harvest conditions; comparable global price data are not available.

International capital flows were official and political. The principal directions were U.S. war finance, lend-lease, Allied official transfers, blocked sterling balances, domestic war-bond finance, and constrained neutral-country finance. Normal private long-term international investment was largely suspended or controlled.

World population in the Gapminder version 8 reconstruction was 2,358,978,018 in 1944 against 2,341,257,167 in 1943, an increase of 17,720,851 or 0.76%; this is a smoothed historical estimate, not a census count. Contemporary U.S. national accounts reported by the BIS put gross national product at USD 198.7 billion (1943: USD 186.5 billion), of which federal war expenditure was USD 86.4 billion, private consumption USD 97.5 billion and private capital formation only USD 1.8 billion. The BEA's current real GDP series rises 7.95%, the Maddison Project Database 2023 7.20% and the Maddison 2010 legacy series 8.4%, so the U.S. growth rate depends on the vintage.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The U.S. interest-rate environment was explicitly pegged. Treasury bills were held around three-eighths percent, long-term Treasury bonds around 2.5 percent, and Federal Reserve discount rates at 1 percent. The purpose was war finance, not countercyclical monetary policy. In the United Kingdom, Bank Rate remained at 2 percent. German and Japanese financial conditions were command-finance systems with administrative credit and non-convertible exchange arrangements. Japan's pre-1945 statistics contain missing intervals in official historical tables, so many central-bank indicators must be marked Data not available. The League of Nations discount-rate table shows the Reichsbank at 3.50%, the Bank of Japan at 3.29%, the Reserve Bank of India at 3.00% and the Bank of France at 1.75% through the first three quarters. The Bank of Canada cut its rate from 2.50% to 1.50% effective 8 February, and the Bank of Italy rate is reported at 4.50% in the third quarter with a cut to 4.00% dated 11 September.

The exchange-rate regime was transitional. The Bretton Woods system was agreed in 1944 but became operational only after the institutions formally came into existence after the war. Most countries operated exchange controls, trade controls, and capital controls. The major FX movement of historical significance was not a market swing in 1944 but the political decision to build a dollar-centered fixed exchange-rate architecture.

Credit conditions differed by regime. In Allied economies, governments absorbed savings through war bonds and directed bank balance sheets toward public debt. In Axis and occupied economies, credit was administrative and often tied to requisition, occupation finance, or monetary expansion. Stock markets existed in some countries, but their signals were weakened by controls, closures, thin trading, and the wartime split between official prices and real scarcity. The U.S. Dow Jones Industrial Average rose in 1944, with common historical tables showing a 12.09 percent annual price return and a year-end close near 152.32.

Money expanded faster than spendable output. U.S. adjusted deposits plus currency rose from USD 123 billion to USD 151 billion (+22.76%), with government deposits rising from USD 10 billion to USD 21 billion. Belgium's decree of 6 October withdrew large-denomination notes and blocked most balances, leaving free circulation of 57.4 billion francs at year-end. In Italy, decree no. 226 of 14 September transferred credit supervision to the Treasury and delegated it to the Bank of Italy from 5 October. Greece's 11 November conversion exchanged 50 billion old drachmas for one new drachma, the end point of a wartime hyperinflation.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

There was no peacetime trade regime in 1944. Lend-lease, blockades, occupation systems, rationing, convoy priorities, military shipping, and administrative import controls shaped flows. The United States and United Kingdom had already linked lend-lease principles to future trade liberalization discussions, and Bretton Woods institutionalized part of that postwar thinking. GATT did not exist until 1947, the EEC did not exist, and ASEAN did not exist.

Sanctions, embargoes, and blockades were not marginal. They shaped fuel availability, shipping, metals, and food. Neutral economies were pressured to limit strategic trade with Axis powers. Bilateral economic tensions were inseparable from the war, including U.S.-British disputes over postwar trade, sterling balances, imperial preference, and the design of international institutions. The Cold War had not yet formally begun, but the future U.S.-Soviet economic divide was visible in the different assumptions behind market multilateralism and state planning.

The IMF Articles of Agreement were adopted on 22 July 1944 but entered into force only on 27 December 1945, so the Fund did not operate in 1944. U.S. exports reached USD 14.2 billion, up USD 1.5 billion, chiefly as Lend-Lease shipments rather than commercial sales. Argentina suspended trade and financial relations with the Axis on 27 January, and its exports rose to 2,286 million pesos from 2,045 million. Turkey introduced dollar export and import premia of 40% and 48% on 15 November, and from 1 March Switzerland restricted acceptance of dollars earned by watch exports.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil was a strategic commodity, not merely an energy price. U.S. production and refining capacity were central to Allied logistics. Germany and Japan faced acute fuel constraints. OPEC did not exist and therefore has no 1944 context. Petroleum prices were administratively shaped, with U.S. posted-price historical series around $1.21 per barrel, but this should not be treated as a free global oil price.

Metals such as steel, aluminum, and copper were allocated by state priority systems. In the United States, the War Production Board's 1944 discussion shows that major metals were no longer always the primary binding constraint by mid-1944; manpower and common components had become central bottlenecks. Agricultural commodities were also administered. Wheat, rice, meat, sugar, oils, and feed grains were affected by harvests, shipping, military procurement, and rationing. Food stress was particularly severe in parts of Asia and occupied Europe, but comparable global staple-price statistics are not available.

U.S. steel output was 89.0 million net tons against 88.2 million in 1943 (+0.91%), and synthetic rubber output reached 753,000 long tons. Wheat carryover stocks in the United States, Canada, Argentina and Australia fell from 42.0 million to 26.6 million metric tons between 1 August 1943 and 1 August 1944 (-36.67%), a stock measure rather than a harvest or trade figure. Wheat freight averaged CHF 21.40 per 100 kg in the BIS series.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

There is no comparable global unemployment rate for 1944. The United States had extremely low unemployment, around 1.3 percent in the NBER/FRED quarterly series. Australia also had extreme labor tightness, with RBA historical material noting unemployment below 1 percent in 1943 and strong manufacturing employment growth into 1944. In the United Kingdom and Canada, mobilization absorbed labor; the Macrohistory Database gives annual unemployment of 0.4% and 1.47%. The Federal Reserve reported a December U.S. labor force of 63.2 million including the armed services, armed forces of more than 11.9 million, and late-year unemployment below 700,000 persons.

Migration and labor allocation had large economic significance. Military service moved millions out of normal labor markets. War industries drew women and rural workers into factories and logistics. The Bracero program linked Mexican labor to U.S. agricultural needs; about 120,000 Mexican farm and railway workers were in the United States late in 1944, and remittances reached 127 million pesos. Forced and coerced labor in Axis-controlled Europe makes unemployment, wages, and productivity data non-comparable. Demographically, wartime disruption suppressed normal household formation and housing construction in many countries, setting up postwar housing shortages and baby-boom dynamics.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States was the dominant industrial and financial economy of 1944. Real output was still rising as federal procurement, logistics, shipbuilding, aircraft, vehicles, electronics, chemicals, and food processing operated at extraordinary scale. Civilian consumption was constrained by rationing, price ceilings, and shortages, so measured GDP growth did not translate into normal household choice. The main macro story was not demand weakness but allocation: the state directed credit, labor, materials, and factory capacity toward wartime production while households accumulated deposits and war bonds.

Monetary policy was subordinated to Treasury finance. The Federal Reserve maintained low rates and an interest-rate peg, with bills around three-eighths percent and long bonds capped near 2.5 percent. The New York discount rate stood at 1 percent. The fiscal position was heavily in deficit and gross federal debt rose to about 90.9 percent of GDP in the FRED/OMB annual series. The major forward linkage was reconversion: by late 1944, firms and policymakers were already preparing the shift from military procurement to civilian housing, automobiles, appliances, and private capital formation.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Growth drivers: federal procurement, shipbuilding, aircraft, vehicles, steel, aluminum, chemicals, petroleum, rail, ports, finance, and food. Contracting or suppressed sectors: civilian automobiles, civilian housing, many consumer durables. Corporate landscape: General Motors, Ford, Chrysler, U.S. Steel, DuPont, Standard Oil companies, AT&T, GE, Westinghouse, Boeing, Douglas, Lockheed, and IBM were central suppliers or technology firms, but revenue rankings for 1944 are not available in a comparable global series. Banking was stable under deposit growth and bond finance. Household balance sheets strengthened through forced saving and bond purchases, while consumer choice was narrow. The War Production Board recorded munitions output of USD 61.3 billion, 96,359 aircraft (16,048 heavy bombers), 17,565 tanks and 595,330 Army Service Forces trucks; steel output was 89.0 million net tons against 88.2 million in 1943 (+0.91%), and synthetic rubber reached 753,000 long tons. The contemporary Federal Reserve factory-and-mine index fell from 239 to 235 (1935-1939=100, -1.67%), while the current FRED industrial production index rises 7.50% in annual average. Individual saving was about USD 40 billion and liquid assets about USD 130 billion at year-end; adjusted deposits plus currency rose from USD 123 billion to USD 151 billion (+22.76%), including government balances. In the Macrohistory Database, total bank loans rose 2.60%, real consumption per person rose 1.70% and the nominal house-price index rose 16.58%. Chicago's hedonic asking-price index rose 15.43% while its asking-rent index fell 15.00% (late-May advertised samples, not completed transactions or rents paid). Penicillin output was about eighty times the 1943 level according to the War Production Board; Pfizer's deep-tank plant opened on 1 March and civilian distribution began on 1 May.

**Economic narrative:**

Germany remained one of the world's largest measured economies in the Maddison legacy series, but the 1944 number is especially hard to interpret. It represented a command economy under intense military pressure, occupied-territory extraction, forced labor, price controls, material shortages, and late-year transport disruption. Market prices, household welfare, and output accounting were not comparable to peacetime national accounts. The measured GDP estimate can therefore overstate the sustainable economic capacity available to consumers and firms.

The key economic story was resource exhaustion. Fuel, transport, skilled labor, and industrial components became binding constraints even where factories could still produce. Financial conditions were administratively controlled; interest rates, exchange rates, credit, and investment were political instruments rather than market prices. By the end of 1944 the postwar problem was already visible in hindsight: the country would face physical reconstruction, currency reform, demobilization, and a redefinition of property and industrial control.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Growth drivers: armaments, chemicals, machine tools, rail-linked heavy industry, and state-directed production. Contracting areas: civilian consumption, housing, normal trade, consumer finance, and nonessential services. Corporate and sector notes: heavy industrial groups such as IG Farben, Krupp, Siemens, Daimler-Benz, and major banks remained important, but ownership, procurement, and labor conditions were shaped by the wartime state. Banking-sector health cannot be assessed by normal profitability or credit-quality measures. Volkswagen's own historical record shows a workforce of 17,365 on 30 April 1944, of whom 11,334 were foreign persons, and an underground project at Tiercelet allocated in March and abandoned in September before any V1 production there. Real consumption per person fell 8.44% in the Macrohistory Database, and the Berlin thirty-stock reconstruction gave a price return of only +0.12% (+3.17% total return).

**Economic narrative:**

The United Kingdom was a fully mobilized war economy in 1944, but its output growth had peaked earlier. Maddison's legacy GDP estimate shows a decline from 1943, consistent with the strain of mobilization, the narrowing of production toward military and logistics needs, and the limited availability of labor and imported supplies. The external position depended heavily on official finance, lend-lease, shipping control, and sterling-area arrangements rather than normal private capital flows.

The policy regime combined cheap money, capital controls, rationing, wage and price controls, and centralized allocation. Bank Rate remained at 2 percent, part of the long cheap-money regime. The big structural issue was not whether Britain could sustain total mobilization for another year, but how postwar fiscal pressure, debt, export weakness, and imperial finance would be managed after victory. The Beveridge-style welfare agenda and reconstruction planning already pointed toward a larger postwar state.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Growth drivers: military supply, ship repair, aircraft, coal, public administration, and Atlantic logistics. Contracting or constrained sectors: civilian consumer goods, private construction, and export industries outside priority needs. Household dynamics: rationing and utility clothing limited consumption, while savings rose. Corporate landscape: ICI, Vickers, Rolls-Royce, shipbuilders, banks, insurers, rail and port operators were central. ### 5C. Innovation & technology

The innovation frontier in 1944 was military-linked and state-funded. Radar and radio electronics advanced rapidly. The economic implication was not immediate consumer technology but capability creation. ### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Sectoral employment shifted from agriculture and peacetime services toward manufacturing, logistics, public administration, and military-related work. Women's employment rose in several Allied economies, although postwar labor-market norms would pressure many women out of wartime roles. Skilled labor scarcity became a binding constraint in the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, Germany, Japan, and the Soviet Union.

Wage dynamics are difficult to compare because controls, rationing, overtime, in-kind benefits, military service, and coercive labor systems distorted normal wage measurement. Union movements gained leverage in some Allied economies through tight labor markets and postwar political bargaining, while authoritarian and occupied economies suppressed independent bargaining. ### 6C. Currencies

The U.S. dollar became the planned center of the postwar system through Bretton Woods. The dollar's link to gold at $35 per ounce was the anchor. Sterling remained important through the sterling area but was subject to controls and external pressure. Most currencies were not freely convertible. Germany's mark, Japan's yen, and several occupied currencies were administrative instruments rather than freely traded claims. China's wartime currency situation was unstable, but comparable 1944 exchange-rate and inflation data are not available in a form suitable for this ranking; in Chungking the League of Nations cost-of-living index rose from 4,483 to 15,299 between June 1943 and June 1944 (1939=100, +241%), a local measure rather than a national CPI.

Liberation brought new administered rates rather than market prices. Allied forces converted at 50 francs per USD in France (later 49.625), at 43.8275 Belgian francs per USD, and at a cross-rate of 2.652 guilders per USD (10.691 per pound) in the liberated Netherlands, while Allied military conversion in occupied German areas from September used RM 10 per USD against a Berlin quotation of about RM 2.5. Argentina's free-market dollar ended the year at 4.05 pesos under capital-entry controls. Turkey's central bank raised gold holdings from 143.4 to 196.5 tonnes (+37.03%) while its foreign exchange excluding clearing fell from 89.3 million to 51.7 million lira.

Gold was officially fixed at $35 per troy ounce under the dollar-gold framework. Private gold markets and ownership rules varied by country, so a single global investor price is not meaningful. The qualitative picture is clear: private housing construction was suppressed in many belligerents, urban damage and rent controls distorted values in Europe, and forced saving created deferred demand that would matter after 1945.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The active crises of 1944 were not financial-market surprises in the modern sense. They were war, occupation, liberation disruption, food stress, fuel shortages, transport bottlenecks, fiscal strain, and monetary instability. The biggest systemic vulnerability was reconversion: economies had become optimized for state procurement, and the transition back to civilian production could have produced mass unemployment, inflation, debt stress, or industrial dislocation.

Another risk was suppressed inflation. Price ceilings and rationing reduced measured CPI but created hidden purchasing power. Once controls were relaxed, prices could rise sharply. This risk materialized in several countries after the war, including the United States in 1946, and in more severe form in economies with weaker monetary institutions or physical shortages.

A third risk was institutional failure. Bretton Woods was agreed, but not yet tested. It depended on U.S. financial strength, British acceptance of a reduced monetary role, and broad participation by countries with very different political economies. Hindsight shows the system was successful enough to structure early postwar recovery, but its gold-dollar design also contained long-run tensions that became visible much later.

A fourth risk was data blindness. Policymakers and firms did not have modern national accounts, real-time logistics dashboards, inflation expectations surveys, or comparable international labor statistics. In many countries, output data were secret, political, fragmented, or simply impossible to collect accurately. That uncertainty matters when interpreting all 1944 macro claims.

Realized humanitarian and monetary crises were severe. The Famine Inquiry Commission recorded 981,228 registered deaths in Bengal in January-June 1944, 422,371 above the 1938-1942 average for those months, and a rough total for the famine and its epidemics of about 1.5 million deaths. Greece's 11 November currency conversion at 50 billion old drachmas per new drachma marked the collapse of its wartime currency. Monetary overhangs took different forms: Belgian blocked balances, Italian cashier's checks, Indian sterling claims and U.S. household liquid assets of about USD 130 billion had different conditions of use, which shaped postwar inflation risks.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1944 vs. 1943)

The most important structural change from 1943 to 1944 was the shift from expansion of war production to management of bottlenecks and preparation for postwar order. In the United States, Maddison's reconstructed GDP rose again, but the constraint shifted from basic materials toward manpower and common components. In the United Kingdom, Maddison shows output declining from 1943, consistent with peak mobilization and strain. In the Soviet Union, the Maddison proxy is flat, but that masks territorial recovery and continuing hardship.

Europe's economic geography changed sharply. Italy and France show large Maddison declines as military and political fragmentation disrupted national output. The Netherlands fell sharply. Belgium's available Maddison estimate rose, but the interpretation is uncertain because the year included occupation and liberation. Germany still shows a higher Maddison GDP in 1944 than 1943, but this should not be mistaken for sustainable health; logistics and fuel bottlenecks were worsening.

The international policy regime changed more than the data tables reveal. In 1943 there was no agreed postwar monetary architecture. In 1944, Bretton Woods created one. This was a regime shift even though implementation came later. It changed expectations about exchange rates, capital controls, reconstruction lending, reserve assets, and the role of the United States.

The price picture diverged sharply. Consumer-price inflation fell in the United States (6.1% to 1.7%), Canada, Spain, Switzerland and Sweden, but accelerated in Japan (6.04% to 35.29%) and Italy (67.70% to 344.39%). Public debt ratios rose further: the U.K. ratio in the Macrohistory Database rose from 173.99% to 200.63% of GDP, Japan's from 133.36% to 203.95% and Canada's from 113.60% to 131.71%. Policy rates were broadly unchanged; the main change was Canada's cut to 1.50% in February. RBI sterling securities rose 45.88% between June 1943 and June 1944, and U.S. money plus deposits rose 22.76%, showing how war finance accumulated claims that would shape postwar adjustment.

The risk map also changed. In 1943 the central question was whether Allied production could overpower Axis production. In 1944 the answer was increasingly clear, and the new questions were how to reconstruct Europe and Asia, how to demobilize labor, how to prevent postwar depression or inflation, how to handle debt, and how to rebuild trade without repeating interwar failures.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

High-confidence data: United States real GDP, unemployment, discount-rate environment, federal debt/GDP, and budget deficit/GDP have relatively strong official or institutional series. U.S. financial-market data are also relatively strong. Bank of England Bank Rate is high confidence. The broad fact of Bretton Woods is high confidence because official IMF, World Bank, Federal Reserve, and State Department sources agree on the conference, institutions, and dollar-gold fixed-rate architecture.

Moderate-confidence data: Maddison legacy GDP and population estimates for the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, Sweden, Switzerland, Argentina, Brazil, Mexico, Spain, Turkey, and some Western European countries are useful but should not be treated as modern official national accounts. They are historical reconstructions using changing source quality. Inflation, unemployment, current accounts, and fiscal balances for Argentina, Brazil, Mexico and Turkey are Data not available in comparable annual form; for the other countries they come mainly from the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database reconstruction rather than contemporaneous official releases.

Low-confidence data: Germany, Japan, Italy, France, Belgium, Netherlands, Austria, and the Soviet Union require special caution because war, occupation, territorial control, secrecy, coercive labor, command finance, and disrupted statistics make normal macro comparison weak. The Soviet Union's Maddison "Total Former USSR" figure is especially rough; the flat 1941 to 1945 annual values in the legacy table should be read as a data limitation, not as a precise output path.

Major gaps: China has no annual 1944 GDP figure in either Maddison vintage, despite being a major economy. Indonesia, Egypt, South Africa, Romania, Poland, Iran, and several other plausible near-top-20 or regionally significant economies lack annual 1944 GDP in the Maddison 2010 series. World GDP, world trade volume, global current account balances, global unemployment, global commodity prices outside gold and selected posted-price series, corporate revenue rankings, M&A, IPOs, bankruptcy rankings, city real-estate prices outside Zurich and Chicago, and sectoral employment by country are Data not available. In the country tables, the Soviet Union has no indicator beyond the repeated GDP level; unemployment is missing for Germany, the Soviet Union, India, Japan, Italy, France, Argentina, Brazil, Mexico, Belgium, Austria and Turkey; consumer-price inflation for the Soviet Union, India, Argentina, Brazil, Mexico, Belgium, Austria and Turkey; a policy rate for the Soviet Union, Italy, Brazil, Spain, Australia and Turkey; debt ratios for Germany, the Soviet Union, India, France, Argentina, Brazil, Mexico, Belgium, Austria, Turkey and the Netherlands; current accounts for Germany, the Soviet Union, India, Japan, Italy, France, Argentina, Brazil, Mexico, Belgium, Austria, Switzerland, Turkey and the Netherlands; exchange rates for the Soviet Union, India, Italy, France, Argentina, Brazil, Australia, Mexico, Belgium and Turkey; and budget ratios for Germany, the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union, India, Japan, Argentina, Brazil, Mexico, Austria, Turkey and the Netherlands.

Weakest claims: any exact ranking near the cutoff, any comparison involving occupied Europe or non-market economies, any qualitative statement about household welfare based only on GDP, and any broad claim about global inflation. The figures are best read as a map of established data and explicit gaps, not a complete modern statistical yearbook.

Vintage and source conflicts: the Maddison 2010 legacy growth rates in the ranking differ from the Maddison Project Database 2023, most sharply for Japan (-4.3% against +1.34%, a difference of sign) and for the United States (8.4% against 7.20%, with BEA real GDP at 7.95%). The contemporary Federal Reserve factory-and-mine index fell from 239 to 235 (-1.67%), while the current FRED industrial production index rises 7.50% in annual average; the difference reflects revisions and coverage and is not resolved. U.S. consumer-price inflation is the BLS annual average of 1.7%; the December-to-December change was 2.3%. U.S. unemployment (about 1.3%, NBER/FRED quarterly), federal debt (90.9% of GDP, OMB) and the budget balance (-21.2%, OMB fiscal year) keep their established U.S. sources, while the 1943 comparators in the Macrohistory Database basis (unemployment 1.9%, debt 67.30%) and the Macrohistory Database 1944 values (1.2%, 89.49%, -21.19%) are shown in the Notes rather than mixed. The Bank of Italy discount-rate change in 1944 is reported inconsistently (4.50% in the third-quarter table, 4.00% from 11 September in a footnote). Japanese consumer prices are a historical reconstruction, and Belgian consumer prices for 1940-1945 are implied from share-price indices, so the latter are not used.

Comparability with 1943: the 1943 assessment leaves budget balances blank for Italy, France, Canada, Spain, Australia, Sweden, Belgium and Switzerland because fiscal years were not matched to calendar-year GDP; the 1944 values for these countries are Macrohistory Database revenue less expenditure over nominal GDP, whose fiscal and calendar alignment varies, so their year-on-year change is marked not comparable and the same-source 1943 value is given in the Notes. Exchange rates for France, Belgium, the Netherlands and Germany in 1944 include liberation or military conversion rates that are not annual averages and are not comparable with occupation-period rates. Confidence grades follow the groups above: High for the United States; Medium for the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, Sweden, Switzerland, Argentina, Brazil, Mexico, Spain and Turkey; Low for Germany, Japan, Italy, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Austria and the Soviet Union; and Low for India, whose wartime and famine-year statistics are weak.
