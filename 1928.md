# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1928

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1928 was the last full year of apparent global prosperity before the crisis sequence that began in 1929. It was not a uniformly booming year, but it was a year in which the late-1920s international system still looked workable: the gold-exchange standard had been restored across most major economies, global trade was active, US equity prices surged, France completed legal stabilization of the franc, Germany still benefited from Dawes-era capital inflows, and Japan was preparing for a return to gold. The surface picture was orderly. The underlying structure was fragile.

The defining macroeconomic force was the interaction of gold-standard discipline, creditor-country monetary choices, and increasingly speculative US financial markets. The United States remained the dominant economy and financial center, and the Federal Reserve tightened in 1928, lifting the New York Reserve Bank discount rate from 3.50 percent early in the year to 5.00 percent on 13 July, in steps on 3 February, 18 May and 13 July. France, after stabilizing the franc at a low gold value, attracted gold and became a major gold accumulator. These forces did not yet produce a global collapse in 1928, but they shifted liquidity away from several debtor and trade-dependent economies.

The year also contained major structural transitions. The Soviet Union began the First Five-Year Plan on October 1, 1928, marking the decisive move away from the New Economic Policy toward coercive central planning and forced extraction from agriculture. China entered the Nanjing decade after nominal Nationalist consolidation, still on a silver monetary base rather than gold. Japan recovered from the 1927 banking crisis but remained financially fragile. Germany’s recovery remained dependent on short-term foreign capital. Britain’s older export industries continued to suffer under a strong sterling parity.

Growth was uneven across the large economies. In the Maddison Project Database 2023, US real output rose only 0.52%, while Canada (+8.88%), Czechoslovakia (+8.78%), Japan (+7.80%) and France (+6.96%) expanded much faster and Spain contracted slightly (-0.33%). Industrial indicators were similarly mixed: the League of Nations production index rose in the United States, France and Canada, was flat in Germany and slipped in Britain, and world steel output rose 8.14% while German and British steel output fell. World trade expanded, by 2.78% in the League of Nations quantum index and by 4.56% in the Federico-Tena reconstruction.

At the microeconomic level, 1928 was a year of mass-production capitalism, commodity-export dependence, and sectoral divergence. Automobiles, radios, electrical equipment, chemicals, ports, banking, and construction drove modern growth, while agriculture and older heavy industries were more vulnerable. Corporate giants such as General Motors, Ford, AT&T, Standard Oil successors, Royal Dutch Shell, IG Farben, Siemens, Mitsubishi, Mitsui, and major banks were central mechanisms through which macro trends became investment, employment, credit, and household behavior.

Data confidence is uneven. The United States, United Kingdom, France, Germany, Canada, Australia, and several Western European countries have relatively better monetary, trade, and financial documentation. China, India, Indonesia, Brazil, Mexico, the Former USSR, and several colonial or semi-colonial economies have useful historical reconstructions but far weaker contemporaneous statistical coverage. ## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1928

**1. The restored gold-exchange standard looked stable but was becoming deflationary.** By late 1928, most major economies had returned to some version of gold or gold-exchange discipline. France legally stabilized the franc in June 1928 at a gold content far below the prewar parity, which helped French competitiveness and attracted gold. The monetary law of 25 June defined the franc as 65.5 milligrams of gold nine-tenths fine (58.95 milligrams of fine gold), with a 35% gold cover on notes and other demand liabilities. The United States was already a major gold-standard center, and Britain remained on gold at prewar sterling parity. The significance is that the system appeared to anchor confidence while also transmitting tight money internationally. Countries most affected included the United States, France, Britain, Germany, Belgium, the Netherlands, Canada, and Australia. The forward linkage is direct: the gold standard became a major propagation mechanism of the Great Depression.

**2. US financial-market exuberance and monetary tightening converged.** US equity prices rose sharply, with the Dow Jones Industrial Average posting an annual gain near 49 percent in widely used historical series; the broader rate-of-return reconstruction shows a price gain of 32.59% and a total return of 37.46%. At the same time, Federal Reserve policy tightened against speculation, and the New York Fed discount rate reached 5.00 percent on 13 July 1928. Between mid-1925 and mid-1928, member-bank securities loans rose from $6.718 billion to $9.068 billion. The historical significance is not that 1928 already contained a crash, but that it showed the contradiction between asset-price inflation, gold-standard reserve constraints, and limited central-bank tools. The countries most affected by the forward linkage were the United States, Germany, Canada, and Latin American borrowers dependent on New York credit.

**3. Germany’s recovery was strong but externally fragile.** Germany’s Maddison-based real GDP grew about 4.4 percent in 1928. Heavy industry, chemicals, machinery, and construction were active, and the Dawes Plan continued to frame reparations and foreign lending. Industrial indicators were mixed: the League of Nations industrial index was unchanged at 120 and steel output fell from 16.167 to 14.517 million metric tonnes, while electrical-equipment exports rose from $103 million to $126 million. The key vulnerability was maturity and funding risk: German prosperity depended on external borrowing, much of it short-term and dollar-linked. The forward linkage was the sudden stop after 1929 and the banking crisis of 1931.

**4. The Soviet economic regime changed category.** The First Five-Year Plan began in October 1928. The grain procurement crisis pushed the state toward coercive procurement and away from the mixed incentives of the New Economic Policy. This is one of the most important economic regime shifts of the twentieth century. It affected the Former USSR directly and changed global comparisons by creating a major non-market industrialization model. The forward linkage was forced collectivization, rapid heavy-industrial growth, and severe social and agricultural disruption.

**5. Commodity exporters were prosperous but exposed.** Canada, Argentina, Australia, Brazil, Indonesia, India, and parts of Latin America and colonial Asia depended heavily on export commodities: wheat, wool, beef, coffee, rubber, sugar, oil, tin, tea, jute, and cotton. In 1928 world trade and finance still supported this model. Commodity markets moved in different directions: the League of Nations estimated world rubber production about 36,000 tonnes below 1927 while shipments rose about 6-7% and British year-end stocks fell from 67,300 to 22,900 tonnes, and London quotations for Rio coffee and Middling American cotton rose while Newcastle coal fell. The significance is that commodity-export economies often looked healthy just before the price declines and trade contraction of the early 1930s. Brazil’s coffee economy and Argentina’s grain-meat model are key examples.

**6. Industrial modernity was sector-specific, not universal.** Automobiles, electricity, radio, chemicals, aviation, telephones, ports, and mechanized manufacturing represented modern growth. World motor-vehicle output rose from 4.159 million to 5.203 million, partly from a depressed US 1927 base. At the same time, coal mining, older textiles, small agriculture, and informal labor markets lagged or contracted in many places. This sectoral unevenness mattered inside countries: Britain’s London-centered services differed from coal districts; Japan’s zaibatsu and urban factories differed from silk villages; India’s mills differed from rural agriculture.

**7. Political economy mattered more than simple market indicators.** Fascist Italy pursued currency prestige and corporatist control. China’s Nationalist government sought fiscal and tariff consolidation under incomplete political unity; the US-China tariff treaty was signed on 25 July 1928, but ratifications were exchanged only on 20 February 1929. Mexico was still navigating post-revolutionary political risk. Spain used public works and protection under Primo de Rivera, and a decree-law of 6 August 1928 authorized the Banco Exterior de España and export-credit insurance. These political-economic settings shaped firm decisions, labor markets, and external confidence.

Germany: The Reichsbank operated under the Dawes-era stabilization and gold-exchange setting, with an official discount rate of 7.00% at the end of 1928. The main condition was not just the level of interest rates, but dependence on foreign credit. October deposits at six Berlin banks were 8,923 million reichsmarks, against 6,872 million a year earlier; a March 1928 reclassification affects bills and acceptances.

Japan: Bank of Japan historical materials and later analyses report the official discount rate around 5.48 percent through 1928-1929, and it was 5.48% at the end of 1928. Japan had not yet returned to gold. The financial system was still digesting the 1927 banking crisis and policy was influenced by the planned gold resumption of 1930. Bank of Japan total assets fell from ¥2,482.5 million on 17 December 1927 to ¥2,352.9 million on 15 December 1928.

Exchange-rate regime: Bretton Woods did not exist. The international regime was the interwar gold-exchange standard, with fixed parities or gold-exchange arrangements in many countries, sterling and dollar reserve roles, and important exceptions such as China on silver, Japan off gold, Spain outside full gold restoration, and the Former USSR outside convertibility.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

Trade policy in 1928 was still less restrictive than in the early 1930s, but the liberal pre-1914 world had not been fully restored. Tariffs, imperial preferences, colonial trade structures, reparations, and exchange controls in some countries shaped trade flows. The 1928 League of Nations statistical convention was part of a broader effort to standardize economic data, including trade statistics. The League of Nations reports the ending of British rubber-export restrictions in November 1928.

Major geopolitical-economic issues included German reparations under the Dawes framework, Soviet industrialization and grain procurement, China’s Nationalist consolidation, colonial commodity systems in India and Indonesia, and the role of sterling and dollar finance in global capital movements. The Cold War did not exist. ASEAN, the EEC, and Bretton Woods institutions did not exist.

Key bilateral tensions were economic rather than purely military: German reparations and Allied debts; British concerns over sterling competitiveness; US concern over speculative credit; Japanese policy debate over gold; and Chinese efforts to renegotiate fiscal and tariff authority after nominal unification. The US-China treaty of 25 July 1928 provided for tariff autonomy from 1 January 1929 only if ratifications had been exchanged by then; the exchange took place on 20 February 1929, so the treaty was signed but not implemented in 1928.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil was abundant and cheap by later standards. The US first-purchase crude price of $1.17 per barrel in 1928 reflects a market far removed from the OPEC era, which would not begin until 1960. Major producers included the United States, Mexico, Venezuela, the Soviet Union, the Dutch East Indies, and Persia. Oil mattered for autos, shipping, mechanization, and electricity, but coal remained central to many industrial economies.

The most important labor-market fact is non-comparability: unemployment in Britain’s insured trades, US historical estimates, colonial underemployment, Soviet plan allocation, and agrarian household labor are not measuring the same thing. Narrowly defined series illustrate the divergence: Japan's private-factory employment index fell from 91.8 to 90.1 between December 1927 and December 1928 while earnings per working day rose from 106.2 to 109.5; Italian registered unemployment at year-end fell from 414,283 to 363,551; and the Macrohistory Database unemployment rate rose in the United States (3.3% to 4.2%), the United Kingdom (6.8% to 7.5%) and Australia (4.2% to 6.2%).

Demographically, high-population low-income economies such as China and India ranked near the top by aggregate GDP but low by GDP per capita. Urbanization was rising in the United States, Japan, Germany, Argentina, Brazil, and parts of Europe, but agriculture still employed very large shares of the workforce in India, China, Indonesia, Brazil, Mexico, the Former USSR, and Spain.

Migration still mattered economically, but the open migration regime of the pre-1914 world had narrowed. US immigration restrictions limited inflows. Settler economies such as Canada, Argentina, and Australia still reflected earlier migration and capital inflow patterns. Internal migration from countryside to city was increasingly important in Japan, the United States, Brazil, and Soviet industrialization.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States remained the largest economy in the Maddison-based ranking by a very large margin. Real output growth in the PPP reconstruction was modest because 1928 came late in a long expansion, but industrial production, consumer durables, electric utilities, radio, road transport, and securities markets still carried the public image of prosperity. Federal Reserve reporting for 1928 described a recovery from late-1927 weakness and a return of industrial activity to high levels, while agriculture remained much less buoyant than urban industry. The League of Nations industrial index rose from 102 to 107, steel output from 45.656 to 52.371 million metric tonnes, and motor-vehicle output from 3.401 million to 4.359 million, partly against a 1927 base depressed by a major plant shutdown.

The year was defined by monetary tightening against speculative credit. The New York Reserve Bank discount rate moved from 3.50 percent early in the year to 5.00 percent on 13 July. This was not a recessionary stance in immediate 1928 output data, but it signaled that the central bank was worried about securities credit, call loans, and bank balance sheets. Between mid-1925 and mid-1928, member-bank securities investments rose from $8.863 billion to $10.758 billion and securities loans from $6.718 billion to $9.068 billion; this is a three-year comparison, not 1928 growth. With hindsight, this policy mix mattered because the United States was both the largest economy and a central gold-standard creditor.

Microeconomically, the consumer durables boom was visible in automobiles, radios, household electrification, advertising, branded consumer goods, and installment credit. Ford was shifting from the Model T to the Model A, while General Motors benefited from a more segmented model strategy, and Chrysler acquired Dodge in 1928. Banking consolidation, chain banking, and bank mergers also drew Federal Reserve attention. The weakest sector was still agriculture, where debt burdens and commodity-price volatility limited household purchasing power in farm regions.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Autos, electrical equipment, oil, steel, radio, advertising, retailing, and securities brokerage were the leading sectors; US electrical-equipment exports rose from $99 million to $107 million. Bank suspensions fell to 491 from 662, with suspended deposits of $138.642 million against $193.891 million, and 39 suspended banks reopened against 95 in 1927. Real consumption per head in the Barro-Ursúa reconstruction rose 2.60%, total bank loans in the Macrohistory Database rose 6.01% and mortgage lending 7.46%. The Macrohistory urban house-price index rose 2.86%, while newspaper asking-price indices rose 10.54% in New York City and fell 2.75% in Boston. Farm regions were more fragile, and rising call-money and broker-loan exposure increased financial-system vulnerability.

**Economic narrative:**

China is included because the Maddison Project has adjacent observations in 1913 and 1929 and because excluding China would produce a historically implausible top-20 list. ### 5C. Innovation & technology

Technological change in 1928 was concentrated in electrification, radio, telephones, aviation, chemicals, automobiles, road infrastructure, agricultural mechanization, and industrial process engineering. This was a high-impact technology environment even though it predated computers, digital networks, container shipping, jet aviation, and antibiotics at modern scale. Alexander Fleming's discovery of penicillin in 1928 was published in 1929; purification and mass production came much later, so it had no measurable 1928 economic effect.

Electrification increased demand for copper, machinery, appliances, and utility finance. The League of Nations grouped electricity growth between 1925 and 1928 at roughly 21-30% for the United States and Britain, 31-40% for France, Italy and Germany, 41-50% for Australia and the Netherlands, and 51-60% for Switzerland and Canada. Radio changed advertising, entertainment, and consumer culture. Automobiles changed oil demand, road spending, steel, rubber, glass, insurance, and consumer credit. Chemical firms such as IG Farben and DuPont linked research laboratories to industrial scale. Aviation was still small economically but strategically important.

### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Labor markets were segmented by sector, gender, skill, and political regime. Industrial workers in US auto plants, German chemical firms, Japanese textile mills, British coal mines, Indian jute mills, Soviet state industry, and Argentine ports faced fundamentally different wage-setting systems. A single global unemployment figure would be misleading; Data not available is the honest standardized answer.

Sectoral shifts were clear. High-income economies were moving labor toward manufacturing, construction, distribution, transport, and services. Lower-income economies still had large agrarian labor forces. The Soviet Union began a state-directed shift from agriculture to heavy industry. Japan continued rural-to-urban movement tied to textiles, heavy industry, and services; its private-factory employment index fell 1.85% between December observations while contractual wage rates fell 0.90% and earnings per working day rose 3.11%. Brazil and Argentina had urbanizing labor markets tied to export wealth and domestic industry.

Union and labor-movement dynamics varied sharply. Britain had high unemployment and post-1926 labor tensions. Italy suppressed independent labor under fascist corporatism. The Soviet Union subordinated labor allocation to plan priorities. The United States had productivity gains but uneven union strength. Colonial economies combined formal industrial labor with very large informal and rural labor pools. Cotton-mill short-time in Austria and Spain fell in the half-year ending January 1928 and rose again in the half-year ending January 1929.

### 6C. Currencies

The dominant currency regime was the interwar gold-exchange standard. The US dollar was fixed to gold at $20.67 per fine troy ounce. Sterling was on gold at about $4.8665 per pound; the New York annual mean quotation was 486.6223 cents and the Bank of England annual mean $4.8655. The French franc returned legally to gold in June 1928 with a dollar parity of about 3.9179 cents per franc, roughly 25.5 francs per dollar; the New York annual mean was 3.921 cents. The Dutch guilder and several other currencies operated within gold-standard contexts. The exchange rates in section 4 are New York annual mean quotations in US cents per foreign unit; their reciprocals are reciprocals of a mean, not means of daily reciprocals.

China was on silver, which made it an important exception; the yuan dollar's New York quotation rose 5.00%. Japan had not yet restored gold convertibility and would do so only in January 1930; the yen's dollar value fell 2.11%, and Spain's peseta, outside the full restored gold bloc, fell 2.73%. The Soviet Union’s currency was not convertible in the sense required for normal market comparison. Capital controls varied by country and are not standardized here.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

Active risks in 1928 included US stock-market speculation, gold-standard liquidity strain, German dependence on foreign capital, Britain’s overvalued sterling and old-industry unemployment, Japan’s unresolved banking fragility, Soviet grain procurement conflict, China’s incomplete fiscal and monetary consolidation, and commodity-export concentration in Canada, Argentina, Australia, Brazil, Indonesia, and India.

Active crises included the Soviet grain procurement crisis and the policy break into the First Five-Year Plan. Japan was not in a 1928 crisis on the scale of 1927 or 1930-1931, but it was still living with the consequences of the 1927 banking crisis; Tokyo bank loans fell while deposits rose, and Bank of Japan assets contracted between mid-December observations. Germany was not yet in collapse, but it was exposed to a sudden stop. Britain’s old industrial unemployment was chronic rather than a one-year shock. US bank suspensions persisted at 491 despite the improvement on 1927.

Hindsight-labeled vulnerabilities: The biggest visible-in-retrospect mistake was treating the restored gold-exchange standard as a source of stability while ignoring how gold hoarding, reserve losses, and central-bank tightening could transmit deflation. The second was underestimating speculative leverage and securities-credit risk in the United States. The third was underestimating how quickly global capital flows could reverse, especially for Germany and commodity exporters.

Tail risks that did not yet materialize in 1928 included a US stock-market crash, German banking collapse, sovereign defaults, a collapse in world trade, mass unemployment on early-1930s scale, and Japan’s deflation after gold resumption. The fact that these did not materialize inside 1928 is precisely why the year is historically important: the system looked resilient until it did not.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1928 vs. 1927)

The operational Maddison-based available-country sum rose by about 3.2 percent from 1927 to 1928, using the same China interpolation method, and the 53 annual MPD records rose 3.46% in total; both are mechanical changes in summed records, not certified world growth. This suggests continued late-cycle expansion rather than stagnation, but with wide divergence: Germany's growth slowed from 10.00% to 4.37% and Spain's from 9.57% to -0.33%, while France moved from -2.08% to 6.96% and Japan from 1.20% to 7.80%. The top-20 composition did not change under this method. World trade volume rose 2.78% in the League of Nations index and 4.56% in the Federico-Tena series, and world steel output 8.14%.

The largest structural change was the Soviet break from NEP toward central planning. This was not a normal year-over-year macro fluctuation; it was a regime change. The First Five-Year Plan began in October 1928, and coercive grain procurement changed the relationship between agriculture, state finance, and industry.

France moved from de facto stabilization to legal gold-standard stabilization of the franc in June 1928. That strengthened confidence in France but also contributed to international gold-flow asymmetries. Compared with 1927, France became a more important monetary force, and its official discount rate was lowered from 4% to 3.5% in January.

The United States shifted from easier late-1927 conditions toward clear 1928 tightening. The New York Fed discount rate moved from 3.50 percent at the start of 1928 to 5.00 percent on 13 July, a year-end rise of 1.5 percentage points. This was a policy pivot with global implications because New York credit mattered to Germany and other borrowers. Italy cut its discount rate from 7% to 5.5% during the year, while Britain held Bank Rate at 4.5%.

Japan moved closer to gold resumption, though the actual return came in 1930. Compared with 1927, the immediate banking panic had passed, but the banking system and policy debate remained fragile.

Germany’s recovery continued, but the forward risk increased as US money tightened. Britain’s structural problems remained unresolved, and its reconstructed unemployment rate rose. Commodity exporters still looked healthy, but concentration risk grew because their prosperity depended on trade and credit conditions that would deteriorate after 1929.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

Best-documented figures: the dated official discount-rate decisions from the Federal Reserve annual report and Bulletin; US bank-suspension counts; the French monetary-law gold content; the US crude oil first purchase price from EIA; the US producer copper price from USGS; League of Nations physical-output tables for steel, pig iron and vehicles; and the Bank of Italy's register reconciliation. Maddison Project GDP per capita and population for countries with direct 1928 observations are the most suitable long-run cross-country framework, but they are reconstructions, and the ranking table therefore grades them Medium at best.

Medium confidence: The direct MPD observations and country narratives for the United States, United Kingdom, France, Germany, Japan, Canada, Australia, Netherlands, Belgium, Italy, Spain and Austria, and the Macrohistory Database price, unemployment, debt and current-account series, are supported by official or high-quality secondary sources, but many specific macro indicators are not standardized. Equity-market information is strongest for the United States and weaker elsewhere; the non-US returns are portfolio reconstructions rather than exchange indices.

Low confidence or high uncertainty: China’s 1928 GDP is interpolated. The Former USSR GDP is a non-market-economy reconstruction and should be interpreted with a wide error band; its MPD growth record is not shown because the source territory behind its GDP and population joins is unmatched, so its growth is Data not available; the raw source-series change of 7.15% is given in the Notes. Indonesia is a colonial economy estimate and should be read in the Dutch East Indies context, and Czechoslovakia is a later-border successor-state record; both are graded Low. India, Brazil, Mexico and Argentina have useful reconstructed GDP data but many missing standardized macro indicators and are graded Low or Medium-low.

Corrections and conflicts: The New York discount-rate increase to 5% took effect on 13 July 1928, according to the Federal Reserve annual report, after increases to 4% on 3 February and 4.5% on 18 May. UK indicators are shown from the Macrohistory Database, and the Bank of England millennium dataset differs: unemployment 7.43% against 7.5%, public debt 169.40% against 175.78% of GDP on a public-sector par-value basis, and current account 2.67% against 2.70%; for the budget, Macrohistory fiscal-year revenue minus expenditure gives +1.34% of GDP while Bank of England calendar public-sector net lending gives -0.13%, so no UK budget value is shown. World trade growth differs between the League of Nations quantum index (+2.78%) and the Federico-Tena series (+4.56%), whose methods, coverage and revisions differ while their customs-data ancestry overlaps. US unemployment is shown from the Macrohistory Database (4.2%) for 1928, which may differ from the Lebergott vintage. In London, the Campbell-Grossman-Turner all-share index (+6.88%) and the rate-of-return blue-chip reconstruction (+12.10%) measure different constituent universes, as do the Bank of Japan share index (+1.89%) and the Japanese rate-of-return reconstruction (+3.36%). The Federal Reserve's December-average US gold-stock comparison (-$274 million) and its calendar-year change (-$240 million) refer to different periods. Exchange rates in this report are New York annual mean quotations in US cents per foreign unit, a different basis from the Macrohistory Database annual levels in local currency per dollar; China's figure is the yuan-dollar quotation and Argentina's the gold peso, and Australia, the Former USSR and the United States have no quotation shown.

Metrics with major gaps: Budget balances are not shown as values for any economy, because fiscal-year accounts and calendar-year GDP are not matched; the Macrohistory Database arithmetic is given in the Notes. Policy-rate year-over-year changes are not shown for India, Belgium and Austria, because their prior-year official rates are not reconciled. Inflation, unemployment, government debt/GDP and current account/GDP are Data not available for China, India, the Former USSR, Indonesia, Argentina, Brazil, Mexico, Czechoslovakia and Austria in comparable form, along with corporate revenues, market capitalization, IPOs, M&A, bankruptcies, wages, household debt, savings rates, exchange-level index data outside the listed markets, and sectoral employment. The Australian, Belgian and German loan series include categories such as interbank and mortgage lending that are not a household-debt aggregate, and the large French loan change is not reported because its coverage is not documented.

Historical data conflicts: GDP ranking can differ depending on whether one uses Maddison PPP reconstructions, nominal current-dollar estimates, colonial administrative data, or later national accounts. This document uses Maddison PPP for ranking because it is the most suitable long-run cross-country framework, but it should not be confused with market-exchange-rate GDP. The ranking includes interpolated China; a ranking restricted to direct MPD observations would move every economy below China up one place and add Switzerland at rank 20.

Weakest claims: Any statement about global inflation, cross-country unemployment, non-US equity returns, Soviet output quality, China’s aggregate GDP, colonial household consumption, and global corporate rankings should be treated cautiously.
