# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1935

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1935 was a recovery year, but not a return to the pre-Depression world economy. Output rose in many major economies, equity markets improved, commodity stocks fell, and unemployment indicators moved down from extreme Depression levels. Yet world trade remained far below 1929, exchange controls were widespread, international capital flows were weak, and the economy was increasingly split among the sterling area, the dollar area, the remaining gold bloc, exchange-control economies, and planned economies.

The dominant macro divide was between countries that had escaped gold-standard deflation and those still defending gold parities. The United States, United Kingdom, Japan, Sweden, Canada, and Australia benefited from easier money, currency flexibility, public works, or cheap credit. France and the Netherlands remained under gold-bloc pressure. Belgium left gold in 1935 and recovered more strongly, foreshadowing the broader 1936 gold-bloc break.

State direction was the second defining force. In the United States, the Second New Deal turned recovery policy into permanent institutions for banking, labor, social insurance, and work relief. In Germany, Japan, Italy, and the USSR, growth was tied more directly to state allocation, public procurement, heavy industry, and strategic sectors. In colonial and developing economies, exchange controls, commodity schemes, and trade preferences mattered more than open-market adjustment.

The top-20 GDP ranking was led by the United States, former USSR, China, Germany, United Kingdom, India, France, Japan, and Italy. Large colonial or agrarian economies ranked high because of population. Financial markets improved, but recovery remained fragile and fragmented.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1935

**1. Uneven recovery from the Great Depression.** League of Nations indices with 1929 equal to 100 put world industrial production in 1935 at 95 including the USSR and 84 excluding it. The quantum of world trade was only 79, showing that production recovered faster than trade. The same survey gives a second set of figures on another page (industry 92 and trade 82), so these indices carry vintage uncertainty. Stronger recovery economies included the United States, Germany, Japan, USSR, Britain, Canada, Australia, Sweden, and Belgium.

**2. The gold bloc weakened.** France and the Netherlands still defended gold, while Belgium left gold and devalued. This mattered because gold defense transmitted external pressure into domestic deflation. The forward link was the broader 1936 break by France and the Netherlands.

**3. State-led demand replaced older laissez-faire assumptions.** The United States expanded institutions, while Germany, Italy, Japan, and the USSR used more direct state allocation. Britain relied more on cheap money and housing. Across systems, public policy became a growth mechanism.

**4. Trade recovery was slow and managed.** Tariffs, quotas, clearing agreements, exchange controls, and colonial preferences shaped flows. Germany's clearing system, sterling-area preferences, China's currency reform, and Italy's sanctions crisis show how trade became more political and administrative.

**5. Cheap money supported recovery, but finance was not normal.** The U.S. prime loan rate was recorded at 1.5 percent, date uncertain, and the UK Bank Rate was 2 percent. Low rates helped housing, debt service, and equities, but international lending and capital mobility remained impaired.

**6. Micro recovery ran through construction, durable goods, heavy industry, and import substitution.** Housing in Britain, autos and durables in the United States, metals and engineering in Germany and Japan, and import substitution in Latin America were major transmission channels.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The main monetary divide was flexible-currency recovery versus gold defense. The United States operated after the 1934 gold revaluation. Britain held Bank Rate at 2 percent. Germany used exchange controls and bilateral clearing. Japan benefited from earlier yen depreciation. France and the Netherlands remained under gold-bloc pressure. Equity markets rose where recovery and reflation were credible; the Dow Jones Industrial Average ended 1935 at 144.13, up 38.53 percent. Reconstructed national returns for other markets appear in section 6A. Official discount rates at the end of 1935 were 1.5% in New York, 2% in London, 4% in Germany, 6% in France, 3.65% in Japan, 5% in Italy, 5% in Spain, 5% in Poland, 3.5% in the Netherlands, 2% in Belgium, 3.5% in Czechoslovakia, 2.5% in Sweden, 3% at the Reserve Bank of India, 4% at the Java Bank and 2.5% at the new Bank of Canada. The United States Banking Act of 23 August reorganized the Federal Reserve Board and the Federal Open Market Committee, with effect in 1936.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

Trade remained fragmented. Clearing agreements, quotas, tariffs, import licensing, exchange controls, and imperial preferences mattered more than pre-1914-style open flows. Italy's late-1935 sanctions crisis affected raw-material and energy expectations; League sanctions operated from 18 November. China's currency reform of 3-4 November changed trade finance. Belgium cut the gold content of its currency by 28% from 1 April after exchange controls began on 18 March. There was no Bretton Woods framework and no postwar-style regional integration. The relevant blocs were sterling, dollar, gold, German exchange-control, Soviet planning, and colonial trade systems.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor markets improved from the Depression trough, but unemployment remained high and definitions were not comparable. Migration was constrained by restrictions and weak labor markets. World population in the Federico-Tena reconstruction on 1991 borders rose 1.33%, by 28.6 million, to 2,175.5 million. Urbanization continued in the USSR, Japan, Latin America, and colonial Asia, but household welfare depended on rural prices, food costs, and employment recovery.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States was the clear number one economy and had one of the strongest recoveries of 1935. The Second New Deal changed the institutional setting through the Banking Act, Social Security Act, National Labor Relations Act, and Works Progress Administration.

The recovery remained incomplete because unemployment and private investment were still weak by later standards. Hindsight: 1935 looked like a strong rebound, but it did not remove the policy fragility visible in the 1937 to 1938 recession.

Official series confirm the strength of the year. BEA real GDP rose 8.90% to $1,058.836 billion in chained 2017 dollars and nominal GDP reached $74.241 billion, while Federal Reserve industrial production rose 15.82% on annual means. Consumer prices rose 2.2% on average and 3.0% from December to December. The National Labor Relations Act was signed on 5 July and the Social Security Act on 14 August, with payroll taxes scheduled from 1937 and monthly old-age benefits from 1942. The Banking Act of 23 August reorganized the Federal Reserve, with the reconstituted Board taking office in February 1936 and the new Federal Open Market Committee in March 1936.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:**

Autos, steel, construction materials, utilities, finance, and consumer durables led. Banks were healthier after earlier reforms, while securities, utilities, and labor relations faced new federal rules. Residential building contracts in 37 states (floor area, provisional) rose from 13 to 27 (1925-1929=100); the Macrohistory nominal house-price index rose 11.62%, and newspaper asking prices for housing rose 3.14% in the Boston market area and 1.46% in the Chicago market area.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The biggest active risks were monetary fragmentation, geopolitical sanctions, incomplete labor recovery, and fragile trade. Gold-bloc countries risked deflation and capital flight. Exchange-control countries risked scarcity and misallocation. Commodity exporters risked renewed price declines. Banking was safer than in 1931 to 1933, but still depended on confidence and public backstops.

Italy's sanctions crisis showed how political conflict could reshape trade. China's currency reform created implementation and confidence risk. Germany's recovery hid foreign-exchange bottlenecks behind controls. Soviet growth was difficult to compare with market economies. Hindsight: 1935's recovery was achieved through national systems that reduced international integration and increased late-1930s instability.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1935 vs. 1934)

The main change was acceleration. MPD-covered economies grew by about 6.54 percent in aggregate. The United States, China, Germany, Canada, Belgium, Australia, Italy, Sweden, and Argentina all posted strong or moderate gains, and Soviet official industrial output rose from 352 to 431 in the League of Nations index. Industrial output, employment, and commodity indicators improved.

Rank changes were concrete. The former USSR overtook China for second place. Germany overtook the United Kingdom for fourth. Australia overtook Czechoslovakia. No country entered or exited the top 20. Policy changes were also large: the United States institutionalized the Second New Deal, Canada and India began central-bank operations, Argentina created its central bank, Belgium devalued, China reformed its currency, and Germany and Italy deepened controlled-economy policies.

Risks also changed. Immediate deflation and banking-collapse risks receded in many economies, but geopolitical, exchange-control, and trade-fragmentation risks increased. Recovery was broader than in 1934, but less internationally liberal.

Matched measures show the shift. MPD growth accelerated from -1.83% to +3.73% in the Netherlands, from -0.83% to +6.17% in Belgium and from -0.26% to +5.40% in Italy, but slowed from +10.63% to +8.08% in Canada and from +8.23% to +2.81% in Brazil. United States consumer inflation eased from 3.1% to 2.2%, while French consumer prices fell 8.54% after 3.53%. Official discount rates rose by 3.5 pp in France, 1 pp in Italy and the Netherlands and 0.5 pp at the Java Bank, and fell by 1 pp in Spain and 0.5 pp in Belgium. The League of Nations index of world mining and manufacturing rose 11.84%, from 94.6 to 105.8 (1925-1929=100), and its trade quantum 4.05%, from 86.5 to 90.0.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

Highest confidence: MPD 2023 GDP per capita, population, computed total GDP, and ranking calculations. These are still historical estimates, not official 1935 national accounts. Confidence is highest for the United States, United Kingdom, France, Germany, Canada, Australia, Sweden, Netherlands, and Belgium. Confidence is lower for China, India, Indonesia, Brazil, Poland, and Argentina. Confidence is lowest for the former USSR because market GDP concepts must be reconstructed from planned-economy data, and its estimate carries substantial, unquantified uncertainty.

Country-level unemployment, CPI inflation, current-account balances, debt-to-GDP ratios, budget balances, and policy rates are not comparable without national-source reconciliation. Many countries lacked modern CPI definitions, labor-force surveys, fiscal reporting, or market-based policy rates. Data not available is shown wherever no matched source exists.

**Indicator sources and definitions.** Policy rates are official discount rates in force at the end of the year as published in the Federal Reserve Bulletin of January 1936, not annual averages; Argentina's new central bank, Australia, Brazil and China have no official rate shown, and the Soviet State Bank rate is administered. CPI, unemployment, debt, current-account, budget and exchange-rate entries for most advanced economies come from the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database (Release 6), and equity returns, long-term yields and national house prices from it and its rate-of-return extension; these data are used under the CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 licence. Exchange rates from the Macrohistory Database are shown as local currency per US dollar, French rates converted to francs of the time and Australian rates to Australian pounds. United Kingdom indicators use the Bank of England millennium dataset and United States CPI the Bureau of Labor Statistics series. Wholesale-price changes for China, India, the Netherlands Indies, Argentina, Poland and Czechoslovakia are not consumer-price indices and are not used as CPI. Budget balances are Data not available for most economies because central-government fiscal years and coverage are not reconciled with calendar-year nominal GDP; the arithmetic ratios are given as context. League of Nations industrial-production indices are national series on their own bases (mostly 1925-1929=100, but 1928=100 for Italy and Spain, a 1924 and 1927-1929 base for the United Kingdom, the Swedish industrial federation index for Sweden and official gross output for the USSR), so their changes, not their levels, are comparable across countries. The Zurich rent index is taken from Historical Statistics of Switzerland Online under the CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 France licence.

**Source conflicts.** Our World in Data, the GDP Histomap and the Maddison 2010 vintage share the Maddison lineage, so they do not provide an independent check on the MPD 2023 ranking. The World Economic Survey gives world industry rising from 85 to 95 and trade from 77 to 79 on one page but 85 to 92 and 78.5 to 82 on another. United States real GDP rose 12.47% in MPD 2023 but 8.90% in the BEA chained-dollar series, and United Kingdom real GDP 3.86% in MPD 2023 but 3.70% in the Bank of England series. United Kingdom unemployment is 10.87% in the Bank of England dataset against 11.00% in the Macrohistory Database, public debt 171.07% against 178.09% of GDP, and the public-sector balance -0.32% against +1.20% in the Macrohistory receipts-minus-expenditure arithmetic. The Swedish current account is slightly negative in the Macrohistory Database, whereas the Bank for International Settlements describes surpluses since 1932. The Macrohistory short rate for Japan (5.33%) does not reconcile with the official 3.65% discount rate. Some 1934 values in the League of Nations industrial series differ from earlier vintages: Germany is 90 in the quarterly series against 93 in the annual series, Belgium 80 against 78, Japan 150 against 151 and the USSR 352 against 354. The Macrohistory unemployment entries for Italy and Spain are not certified rates, French unemployment covers only industrial wage and salary earners, and the Belgian external balance is a merchandise trade balance. The Zurich three-room rent index differs from the two-to-four-room index used for earlier years.

**Territorial definitions.** GDP growth for the former USSR is withheld because the national-income source behind the MPD record has not been matched to the territory of its population series; the raw record change is +15.12%. India corresponds to undivided India, and Indonesia to the MPD Indonesia series, which covers the Dutch East Indies and includes Timor. Levels and growth for 1934 and 1935 come from the same MPD 2023 vintage.

Data conflict in three main ways: Maddison versions differ by benchmark and PPP choices; exchange rates may be official, market, gold-parity, black-market, or clearing rates; and unemployment may mean insured unemployment, union unemployment, relief rolls, or something else entirely. Weakest evidence areas are non-U.S. equity returns, global corporate rankings, exact commodity prices, fiscal ratios, and country-level current accounts.
