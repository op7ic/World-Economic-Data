# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1930

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1930 was the year the 1929 financial shock became a worldwide depression in output, trade, prices, credit, and employment. The Maddison Project Database 2023 records with both 1929 and 1930 observations, 55 entities, sum to approximately 5,458.1 billion 2011 international dollars in 1930 against 5,636.7 billion in 1929, a mechanical change of -3.17 percent in the summed records. This is a reconstructed PPP total, not an official world national-account release, and it includes the Former USSR record, whose growth is not shown in this report. Real GDP fell 9.49 percent in the United States, 9.53 percent in Australia and 7.65 percent in Japan. The League of Nations industrial-production index for twelve countries excluding the USSR fell from 111.8 to 96.9.

The central macro force was deflation under the interwar gold standard. Countries defending gold parity often tried to adjust through wages, prices, budgets, and credit contraction rather than currency depreciation. This made debt heavier in real terms and pushed distress through banks, exporters, farmers, construction, and durable-goods producers. Trade values fell more than quantities: the Federico-Tena world trade volume index declined 7.20 percent, while nominal exports fell 19.78 percent. Quotations for coffee, copper, cotton, jute, rubber and wheat fell by 18 to 43 percent.

Policy choices intensified the downturn. The United States passed Smoot-Hawley in June, Japan returned to gold in January, Germany moved toward deflationary austerity under reparations pressure, and most governments still treated balanced budgets and currency defense as priorities. Central-bank discount rates generally declined, with New York cutting to 2 percent and London to 3 percent, but the Reichsbank raised its rate again in October and U.S. bank suspensions more than doubled to 1,345. The Bank for International Settlements opened in 1930, but international monetary cooperation was weakening as the depression deepened.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1930

**1. Gold-standard deflation.** The gold-exchange standard transmitted monetary tightness across countries. The United States, Britain, Germany, France, Japan, the Netherlands, Belgium, Sweden, and many linked economies were affected. U.S. consumer prices fell 2.3 percent, Japanese consumer prices about 10 percent and Australian about 8 percent, while the French index rose 1.0 percent. The forward link was clear: countries leaving gold in 1931 or soon after gained more monetary flexibility.

**2. Trade protection and retaliation.** Smoot-Hawley did not cause the whole depression, but it worsened confidence and encouraged retaliation. Export-dependent economies such as Canada, Argentina, Australia, Germany, Japan, Brazil, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Czechoslovakia were especially exposed. The Senate passed the tariff on 13 June and the President signed it on 17 June.

**3. Commodity deflation.** Wheat, wool, coffee, rubber, sugar, silk, cotton, metals, and oil all mattered as transmission channels. Primary producers faced lower cash income, weaker collateral, lower fiscal revenue, and reduced import capacity. The Rio coffee quotation fell 43.42 percent and crude rubber 38.27 percent. Currencies of several primary producers lost dollar value: the Chinese yuan dollar fell 28.60 percent, the peseta 20.54 percent, the Argentine gold peso 12.22 percent and the milreis 9.27 percent.

**4. Financial fragility.** Asset-price declines turned into credit stress. U.S. bank failures intensified late in 1930, while Germany and other debtor economies carried refinancing risks that became more dangerous in 1931. Equity total returns in the Macrohistory and rate-of-return reconstructions were negative in every top-20 market with data except Spain.

**5. Orthodoxy before Keynesian policy.** Most governments had not yet accepted large countercyclical fiscal stimulus. Austerity, wage adjustment, and currency defense dominated policy thinking in Germany, Britain, Japan, Australia, Italy, Poland, and many gold-linked economies.

**6. Political stress and regime change.** Brazil's revolution, Spain's regime transition, Germany's political radicalization, Japan's policy backlash, and Soviet forced planning show that 1930 was also a political-economy crisis.

**7. Durables led the industrial decline.** World motor-vehicle output fell from about 6.31 million to 4.14 million (-34.43 percent), U.S. output from 5.36 million to 3.36 million and Canadian from 263,000 to 154,000. Output held up better in Britain (239,000 to 237,000) and rose in Czechoslovakia and the USSR. French industry was approximately flat and Soviet industry expanded, so the contraction was broad but not uniform.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

**World GDP and growth rate.** MPD 2023 records with both 1929 and 1930 observations, 55 entities, sum to about 5,458.1 billion 2011 international dollars in 1930 against 5,636.7 billion in 1929, a mechanical change of -3.17 percent in the summed records. The sum is a calculation over source-defined territorial records, not a certified world total or world growth rate: matching record names does not establish matching territories, coverage is incomplete, and the sum includes the Former USSR record whose growth is not shown. World population in the Federico-Tena reconstruction, on 1991 country borders, was 2,043.9 million, up 27.1 million or 1.34 percent; it should not be combined with the incomplete GDP sum to derive world income per head.

**Global trade volume.** The Federico-Tena world trade volume index (1913=100) fell from 132.83 to 123.28 (-7.20 percent), and nominal exports at current borders fell from USD 32,965.7 million to USD 26,443.9 million (-19.78 percent). The League of Nations rounded world trade quantum index fell from 110 to 101, about -8.2 percent; its 1930 continental method changed and some figures were provisional, so the two measures are not averaged. Trade contracted sharply as incomes, prices, credit, and policy cooperation weakened.

**Reserve currency dynamics.** Bretton Woods did not exist. Gold was the anchor, while sterling and the U.S. dollar were major reserve and settlement currencies. China remained a major silver-standard exception. Central banks' foreign-currency reserve deposits at the BIS were 804 million Swiss francs at par on 31 December 1930, 51 percent of its resources; this is not a measure of world reserves, and no 1930 reserve-currency composition with a documented definition is available.

**Inflation and commodities.** The dominant trend was deflation. U.S. crude oil first-purchase price was about $1.19 per barrel, and official U.S. gold remained $20.67 per fine troy ounce. British quotations in the Federico-Tena commodity series fell for coal, coffee, copper, cotton, jute, rubber and wheat; the crude-petroleum import-price series rose (see 3D). No world inflation rate with a documented definition is available for 1930.

**Capital flows.** Capital retrenched. U.S. foreign lending weakened, debtor economies faced refinancing pressure, and gold/liquid reserve centers became more attractive than commodity-exporting borrowers. No quantified 1930 total of international capital flows with a documented definition is available.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

Official discount rates fell in most gold-standard countries during 1930. At year-end the Federal Reserve Bank of New York rate was 2 percent (4.5 percent at end-1929), Bank Rate 3 percent (5 percent), the Banque de France rate 2.5 percent (3.5 percent), the Netherlands Bank 3 percent (4.5 percent), the Riksbank 3.5 percent (5 percent), the Banca d'Italia 5.5 percent (7 percent) and the Bank of Japan commercial-bill rate 5.11 percent (5.48 percent). The Reichsbank eased to 4 percent but raised its rate to 5 percent on 9 October (7 percent at end-1929). The broad stance was constrained easing: nominal rates could fall, but gold defense and deflation kept real credit conditions tight. Japan's January return to gold, Germany's deflationary stance, France's gold accumulation, and U.S. banking stress were the most important monetary-financial facts.

U.S. bank suspensions rose from 642 in 1929 to 1,345 in 1930, with deposits in suspended banks rising from USD 234.5 million to USD 864.7 million. Suspensions include institutions that later reopened and are not a count of permanent failures. The Dow Jones Industrial Average closed 1930 at 164.58, down 33.77 percent for the year; the Standard Statistics all-common-stock index, an annual average of weekly observations (1926=100), fell from 190.3 to 149.8. ### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

Smoot-Hawley, signed on June 17, 1930, was the central trade-policy event. It amplified an income-driven trade collapse and encouraged retaliation; no causal share of the 1930 trade decline is attributed to the statute here. There was no Cold War, EEC, or ASEAN. The relevant geopolitical frame was interwar reparations, imperial trade, colonial dependency, and unresolved post-World War I financial commitments. The BIS began operations on 17 May 1930 as the settlement agent for the Young Plan reparations arrangements. The London Naval Conference set limits on auxiliary naval vessels among the major naval powers; treaty ceilings are not realized 1930 military spending. Brazil's change of government in October and November added political uncertainty to an adverse coffee-price environment.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

OPEC did not exist. Oil, coal, metals, grains, wool, silk, coffee, sugar, and rubber were depressed by weaker industrial demand, weaker transport demand, and lower purchasing power. Gold was a monetary anchor, not a freely floating alternative asset inside gold-standard systems.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Unemployment rose sharply in industrial economies, but definitions are not comparable across countries. U.S. unemployment (Lebergott) rose from 3.2 to 8.9 percent, the British reconstruction from 7.18 to 11.10 percent, the Canadian estimate from 3.05 to 9.56 percent and the Australian from 6.7 to 9.8 percent, while the French 2.0 percent covers only workers in manufacturing, mining and construction. Rural distress in China, India, Indonesia, Brazil, and Eastern Europe often appeared as underemployment, debt stress, and lower farm income rather than measured unemployment. Migration and labor mobility were increasingly restricted as jobs disappeared. World population grew 1.34 percent in the Federico-Tena reconstruction.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States was the largest economy by a wide margin and the main source of the global demand shock. MPD-derived real GDP fell 9.49 percent, while BEA chained-dollar data point to a similarly severe contraction. The League of Nations industrial index fell from 108 to 88 and motor-vehicle production from 5.358 million to 3.356 million vehicles. Lebergott's unemployment estimate rose from 3.2 to 8.9 percent and consumer prices fell 2.3 percent.

Monetary conditions eased in nominal terms, but deflation raised real debt burdens. The New York discount rate fell from 4.5 percent to 2 percent, the last cut taking effect on 24 December, so the year was not without monetary easing even though the adequacy of the response remains disputed. Bank suspensions rose from 642 to 1,345, with USD 864.7 million of deposits in suspended banks; a cluster of failures began in November and a large New York commercial bank closed on 11 December. Suspension included temporary closure and does not by itself measure final losses.

Federal debt rose to 17.55 percent of GDP in the Macrohistory Database and the current-account surplus was 0.77 percent of GDP. Hoover signed the Smoot-Hawley tariff on 17 June.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Automobiles, steel, construction, railroads, consumer durables, and agriculture contracted. Banking stress intensified late in the year. The Standard Statistics common-stock index (1926=100, annual average) fell from 190.3 to 149.8, with industrials 189.4 to 140.6, railroads 147.3 to 124.9 and utilities 234.6 to 214.6. The League electrical-equipment new-orders index fell from 129 to 99. In the Macrohistory Database the nominal wage index fell 1.66 percent, real consumption per head 6.42 percent, total bank loans 1.98 percent and the nominal house-price index 12.12 percent. Quality-adjusted newspaper asking-price indices fell 7.80 percent in New York City and 8.84 percent in Boston, and asking rents fell 2.46 and 1.53 percent; these are mostly late-spring samples, not completed-sale prices.

**Economic narrative:**

China ranked second in MPD total GDP because of its large population, not because of high per-capita output. The Nanjing decade state was attempting currency, tariff, infrastructure, and industrial modernization, but political fragmentation, civil conflict, warlord power, and limited fiscal capacity sharply constrained policy reach. The reconstructed GDP increase is not evidence that China escaped the international contraction.

The economy remained overwhelmingly agrarian. As a silver-standard economy, China experienced the world fall in silver as currency depreciation: the New York quotation for the Chinese yuan dollar fell from 41.90 to 29.92 U.S. cents on an annual-average basis (-28.60 percent). This measures its dollar price, not domestic inflation.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Agriculture dominated employment and output. Shanghai and other treaty-port centers hosted textiles, finance, shipping, and light industry. The League of Nations identified cotton textiles as China's largest manufacturing industry and described expanding cotton activity, but its crop-year consumption figures do not establish calendar-1930 industrial growth. The Young Plan and the new Bank for International Settlements formalized reparations arrangements, but confidence was already deteriorating. ## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The active crisis was the Great Depression: output contraction, deflation, rising unemployment, trade collapse, bank stress, and commodity-price weakness. The main near-miss was the gold-standard and banking crisis that would erupt more fully in 1931. With hindsight, the most visible policy mistakes were defending gold too long, tightening fiscal policy into contraction, escalating tariffs, and underestimating banking fragility. Central-bank easing coexisted with distress, and Germany's October rate increase reversed part of its easing.

Tail risks that materialized included trade retaliation, U.S. banking stress, Brazil's political rupture, worsening German political risk, and commodity-exporter distress. Tail risks that had not yet fully materialized included Britain leaving gold, Japan's second gold suspension, the deepest U.S. bank failures, New Deal intervention, and Italy's later IRI rescue system.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1930 vs. 1929)

The shift from 1929 to 1930 was the shift from crash to depression. The summed MPD records for the same 55 entities fell 3.17 percent, a mechanical change rather than certified world growth. Among the top 20, the largest MPD contractions were Australia -9.53 percent, United States -9.49 percent, Japan -7.65 percent, Brazil -5.96 percent, Spain -5.05 percent, Poland -4.63 percent, Italy -4.52 percent, Argentina -4.13 percent, Canada -3.33 percent, and Czechoslovakia -3.26 percent. World trade volume fell 7.20 percent and nominal exports 19.78 percent; world motor-vehicle output fell 34.43 percent; U.S. unemployment rose from 3.2 to 8.9 percent and U.S. bank suspensions from 642 to 1,345.

Policy also changed. Smoot-Hawley made trade conflict explicit. Japan's gold return made deflation worse. Germany moved deeper into austerity. Year-end discount rates were lower than at end-1929 in New York (2 against 4.5 percent), London (3 against 5 percent), Paris, Amsterdam, Stockholm, Rome, Tokyo and Berlin, although the Reichsbank raised its rate in October. The BIS opened as a new institution, but the system it was meant to stabilize was already weakening. Rankings changed only slightly: Sweden entered the top 20 and Mexico exited.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

Higher-quality statistical records exist for the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, Sweden, the Netherlands, Belgium, France, Germany, and Japan, although modern-style national accounts were often reconstructed later; the ranking table grades the GDP estimates for the United States, United Kingdom, Canada and Australia High and those for Germany, France, Japan, the Netherlands, Belgium and Sweden Medium. Medium-confidence cases include Italy, Spain, Poland, Czechoslovakia, Argentina, and Brazil. Low-confidence cases include China, India, Indonesia, and the Former USSR.

GDP, GDP per capita, population, and growth figures in the top-20 table are MPD estimates. They are not official 1930 national accounts. Soviet and other non-market GDP levels should be treated as rough approximations with likely 20 to 30 percent uncertainty, and Former USSR growth is not shown because the national-income reconstruction and the population series have not been matched to one territory. The 1930 change in summed MPD GDP is -3.17 percent when the same 55 entities are compared in both years; the summed records are not a published world total.

Several indicators rest on conflicting or narrowly defined sources. U.S. unemployment is 8.9 percent in Lebergott's estimate and 8.7 percent in the Macrohistory Database. The British indicators come from the Bank of England millennium dataset, whose public-sector debt and budget perimeter differs from the Macrohistory Database; its annual sterling rate of $4.86175 differs slightly from the Federal Reserve Bulletin annual mean of 486.21 U.S. cents. The Bank of Japan December telegraphic-transfer selling mean was $49.375 per 100 yen against $49.0 in December 1929, a different instrument and period from the Federal Reserve Bulletin annual mean of 49.39 cents per yen; the Bank of Japan December share-price index (-21.09 percent) and the rate-of-return reconstruction (-32.49 percent) are differently constructed. The Dow Jones close of 164.58 (-33.77 percent), the Standard Statistics annual-average index and the Macrohistory U.S. portfolio return (-27.52 percent) are different constructions. The League of Nations world trade quantum (-8.2 percent) and the Federico-Tena volume index (-7.20 percent) are not reconciled. Canadian Dominion accounts for the fiscal year ended March 1930 report total disbursements about $5 million above the Macrohistory expenditure input, and that discrepancy is unresolved. The crude-petroleum import-price series rose while most commodity quotations fell, so the description of falling oil prices applies to producer prices rather than to every quotation.

Exchange rates are Federal Reserve Bulletin annual means in U.S. cents per unit, so a higher number means a stronger currency; the United States row is the dollar reference, the Former USSR quotation is indicative for a non-convertible currency, and no Australian pound quotation with a documented definition is available. Policy rates are those in force at the end of 1930; for India it is the Imperial Bank of India rate listed by the Federal Reserve Bulletin, since no central bank existed, and for Indonesia the rate of the colonial Javasche Bank. Budget ratios are not shown except for Britain because fiscal years and institutional perimeters are not reconciled to calendar-year GDP; the Italian and Canadian fiscal-year outturns are given in the notes instead. The Belgian current-account field in the Macrohistory Database is a merchandise trade balance and is not shown. The Australian consumption-per-head series rises for 1930 for reasons not yet reconciled.

The weakest evidence concerns inflation, unemployment, debt, current-account and budget balances outside the Macrohistory countries, non-U.S. stock index levels, and corporate rankings. Data not available means that no observation with the stated definition and territory is available for 1930, not that no historical record exists. China, the Former USSR, India, Indonesia, Poland, Czechoslovakia, Argentina and Brazil have no national CPI, unemployment, debt, current-account or budget ratio with a documented definition.
