# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1975

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1975 was the trough year of the first postwar global stagflation cycle, and also the year recovery began. The oil shock of 1973-1974 had already changed relative prices, but 1975 revealed its full macroeconomic cost: industrial economies passed through the sharpest synchronized recession since 1945, inflation remained high by postwar standards, and unemployment reached levels that many advanced-economy policy systems had not been designed to handle. The U.S. business-cycle trough is retrospectively dated to March, and recovery began while unemployment remained high. Contemporary IMF reporting placed world output growth at about -0.5 percent in 1975, with industrial countries at about -1.5 percent; the current World Bank WDI series records world real growth of 0.81 percent, down from 1.99 percent in 1974, and world nominal GDP of US$6.048 trillion. Developing countries and oil-exporting economies continued to grow, though under very different balance-of-payments conditions.

The dominant economic story was not simply recession. It was a regime break. The old postwar combination of fixed exchange rates, cheap energy, fast manufacturing growth, and confidence in demand management had been replaced by managed floating currencies, oil-cartel pricing power, wage-price conflict, and a deeper debate over inflation control. Central banks in the United States, Japan, Germany, and the United Kingdom generally eased from the severe monetary tightness of 1973-1974, but they did so with inflation still elevated, and the easing was uneven and sometimes reversed within the year. That produced a policy dilemma that would define the late 1970s.

The year also redistributed economic power. OPEC exporters, especially Iran and Saudi Arabia outside the top-20 PPP list used here, accumulated surpluses and placed them through international banks, although the BIS estimates that the OPEC current-account surplus fell from about US$65 billion in 1974 to about US$35 billion. Deficit oil importers, including Italy, Turkey, India, Brazil, and parts of southern Europe, faced external financing pressure; the non-oil developing countries' deficit widened from about US$15 billion to about US$25 billion. Commercial banks became a major channel for recycling oil surpluses into sovereign and quasi-sovereign borrowing. With hindsight, this was an early phase of the debt cycle that culminated in the Latin American debt crisis of the 1980s. Financial fragility was also visible in institutions: New York City lost access to short-term refinancing and received a federal seasonal-loan law on 9 December, Indonesia's Pertamina missed payments on short-funded investments, and Argentina suffered a severe price, wage and monetary disruption.

At the microeconomic level, households delayed purchases, especially autos and durable goods, because inflation, unemployment risk, and credit conditions damaged confidence. Heavy industry, autos, construction, shipping, and energy-intensive manufacturing were under stress. Oil, gas, petrochemicals, and parts of mining retained strategic importance. Technology was still small in macro terms, but 1975 marked a seed year for personal computing: the Altair 8800 entered public attention, and Microsoft was founded to supply software for that new microcomputer market. Sony launched Betamax while its profits fell, illustrating innovation amid weak demand.

For non-market economies, especially the former USSR, China, and Poland, figures are historical estimates rather than national-account measurements directly comparable to OECD data. ## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1975

**Theme 1: Stagflation became the defining advanced-economy problem.** 1975 joined recession and inflation in a way that challenged postwar Keynesian policy assumptions. In many industrial economies, output contracted while consumer prices rose rapidly. IMF and BIS evidence from the period describes a deep downturn, record postwar unemployment, and inflation that eased from 1974 peaks but remained historically high. The United Kingdom and Italy were among the most visibly affected, with annual CPI inflation of 24.2 and 17.0 percent, while Germany and Japan had lower inflation but still faced real output weakness. Annual averages and December rates told different stories: Japan's annual CPI inflation of 11.7 percent coexisted with a December rate of 7.6 percent. The forward link was direct: the late 1970s became a struggle between incomes policies, monetary restraint, fiscal stimulus, and later disinflation.

**Theme 2: Floating exchange rates replaced Bretton Woods as the operating reality.** By 1975, the Bretton Woods fixed-rate system was no longer the anchor of major-currency policy. The dollar, Deutsche mark, yen, sterling, lira, and franc moved in a volatile managed-float environment. This changed how external adjustment worked. Countries could no longer assume fixed parities would absorb trade and capital shocks. Currency weakness intensified inflation in import-dependent countries, while stronger currencies and tighter monetary credibility helped Germany and, later, Japan contain inflation. The forward linkage was the development of modern foreign-exchange intervention, reserve management, and exchange-rate surveillance.

**Theme 3: Oil-price redistribution reshaped current accounts and banking flows.** The oil shock did not end in 1974. In 1975 the macro adjustment continued. Oil exporters accumulated surpluses while oil importers either compressed demand or borrowed, but the exporters' estimated investible surplus fell from US$56.9 billion to US$32.1 billion as their imports grew. International banks became intermediaries in petrodollar recycling. Energy importers such as Italy, Turkey, India, and Brazil faced a difficult mix of higher import bills, inflation, and growth objectives. Oil exporters such as Iran used revenue to expand state spending and industrial projects, often creating domestic bottlenecks. With hindsight, the same recycling mechanism helped build sovereign-debt vulnerabilities in Latin America and parts of the developing world.

**Theme 4: The industrial cycle was synchronized, but recovery timing differed.** The United States and Japan began to recover earlier in the second half of the year, while much of Europe lagged. Inventory liquidation, weak capital expenditure, and depressed consumer confidence defined the trough. Germany cut its discount rate in five steps from 6 percent to 3.5 percent, Japan reduced the discount rate in four steps from April, and the United States saw the federal funds rate fall from its 1974 highs, with a tightening interlude between June and October. The forward link was a 1976 cyclical rebound, but the rebound did not restore the pre-1973 growth regime.

**Theme 5: Developing economies split between commodity winners and oil-importing borrowers.** Oil exporters and some primary producers had more favorable external positions, while many oil-importing developing economies relied on debt, import controls, or domestic austerity. Brazil and Mexico maintained high headline growth through state-led investment and external finance; Brazil's current-account deficit reached 5.4 percent of GDP. India benefited from a rebound and agricultural improvements, but remained heavily controlled and import-constrained. Indonesia, although an oil exporter, faced the Pertamina debt crisis, proving that commodity revenue did not eliminate financial fragility.

**Theme 6: Microeconomic adjustment appeared before macro indicators normalized.** Consumers cut or postponed durable-goods spending; firms reduced inventories; construction slowed; and banking systems faced new credit-risk patterns. Autos, steel, shipbuilding, and energy-intensive industry were particularly exposed. The household sector became more cautious, while labor conflict intensified where real wages were squeezed by inflation; Argentine average real wages fell 2.7 percent. These micro adjustments were not secondary details. They transmitted the oil shock and monetary tightening into output, jobs, corporate profits, and public budgets.

**Theme 7: Technology was economically small but historically decisive.** The large 1975 economy was still oil, autos, steel, agriculture, finance, utilities, and construction. Yet the microcomputer appeared as a commercially meaningful object. The Altair 8800 and the founding of Microsoft did not change 1975 GDP, but they signaled a future shift from hardware-centered mainframe computing toward software, personal computing, and platform economics. Microsoft's licensing agreement with MITS was dated 22 July, and its year-end sales were US$16,005. This was a weak short-run macro event but a high-significance structural event.

The global economy was in recession, but not all regions moved together. Industrial economies bore the main output contraction. Developing economies as a group still grew, but the average hid sharp differences between oil exporters, commodity producers, and oil-importing borrowers. Trade was a major transmission channel: the IMF described the volume of world trade as falling about 4 to 5 percent from 1974 to 1975. Industrial import compression damaged suppliers, while oil-exporter and planned-economy purchases provided partial support.

Key commodity prices were unusually important. International oil marker prices remained far above pre-1973 levels, while U.S. domestic crude first-purchase prices averaged about $7.67 per barrel under domestic pricing arrangements, up 11.6 percent from $6.87. Gold remained far above the Bretton Woods price of $35 but below its 1974 peak; the BIS records a market low of $128.75 per troy ounce on 23 September, and the USGS gold unit value of $5.19 million per tonne is a separate measure rather than a London annual average. Copper and other industrial commodities weakened with manufacturing demand: the USGS copper unit value fell 17.1 percent, from $1,700 to $1,410 per tonne.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The broad pattern was easing from the very tight conditions of 1974, but not a return to easy money in the pre-oil-shock sense. The U.S. effective federal funds rate averaged about 5.82 percent in 1975, falling from 7.13 percent in January to 5.20 percent in December, with a tightening episode between June and October; the Federal Reserve discount rate fell from 7.75 percent entering the year to 6 percent by mid-May. The Bank of Japan cut its discount rate in four steps from 9.0 percent to 6.5 percent by 24 October. The Bundesbank discount rate fell from 6 percent entering the year to 3.5 percent from 12 September, while the separate Lombard rate fell from 8.0 percent to 4.5 percent. The Bank of England moved in a more complicated path, with Minimum Lending Rate at 11.25 percent in January, 9.75 percent in April, 12.0 percent in October, and 11.25 percent at year-end. Italy cut its discount rate from 8 percent to 6 percent in May and September after removing its import deposit in March.

Equity markets rebounded in the United States as investors anticipated recovery; secondary market-return tables place the S&P 500 1975 price return at roughly 31.6 percent. This rebound should not be confused with macro health. Corporate profits, employment, and capital spending remained under pressure in much of heavy industry. Government bond markets reflected recession, inflation uncertainty, and shifting expectations about policy credibility; the BIS long-rate chart mixes U.S. Aaa corporate bonds, German public-authority bonds, UK twenty-year gilts and other instruments, so comparable 10-year yields for every top-20 country are Data not available.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

1975 sat inside the Cold War, decolonization aftershocks, and the first oil-shock adjustment. Trade disputes were less defined by modern tariff wars than by balance-of-payments measures, capital controls, import deposits, exchange controls, and energy policy. The European Economic Community continued to matter as a regional trade and policy framework, but European economies were divided by exchange-rate pressures and different inflation outcomes. The Lomé Convention between the EEC and African, Caribbean and Pacific states was signed on 28 February, although it entered into force only on 1 April 1976. ASEAN remained young and economically smaller but strategically important in Southeast Asia. The United States remained the central reserve and trading economy, while Japan continued to grow as an export and manufacturing power despite the oil shock.

The Helsinki Final Act, signed on 1 August by 35 participating states, symbolized détente, but economic integration across the Cold War divide remained limited; its economic-cooperation commitments were political rather than a customs arrangement. Soviet-bloc trade was still heavily planned, with significant intra-bloc flows and selected hard-currency trade for energy, grain, machinery, and technology. The BIS identifies poor harvests and technology purchases as reasons for higher imports by centrally planned economies; the USSR imported grain when harvest conditions and planning outcomes required it, and energy exports were increasingly important for hard-currency capacity. In Argentina, major devaluations in March and June were followed by more frequent adjustments from August.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil dominated the economic meaning of commodities. OPEC's new pricing power forced oil importers to reallocate income abroad or borrow. The energy shock changed sector profitability, public investment priorities, and industrial competitiveness. Countries with domestic energy resources, including the USSR, Iran, Indonesia, Canada, the Netherlands through gas, and Australia through resource exports, had strategic advantages, though not always macro stability.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor markets deteriorated most visibly in industrial economies. Unemployment definitions differed widely, so this document does not present a single cross-country unemployment ranking. The key comparative fact is strongly supported by contemporary IMF and BIS descriptions: postwar unemployment highs and weak consumer confidence appeared across many advanced economies. National estimates put annual unemployment at 8.5 percent in the United States, 6.9 percent in Canada, 4.0 percent in the United Kingdom and 1.9 percent in Japan; BIS half-year seasonally adjusted rates were 8.4 and 8.5 percent for the United States and 3.4 and 4.6 percent for the United Kingdom.

Demographics still supported labor-force expansion in many developing economies, including India, Indonesia, Mexico, Brazil, Iran, and Turkey. World population grew about 1.9 percent to 4.06 billion. Urbanization continued to shift employment from agriculture toward industry and services, but in many countries the formal labor market could not absorb the full demographic increase. **Economic narrative:**

The United States was the largest economy by both Maddison PPP-style GDP and World Bank current-dollar GDP. Its 1975 story was recession trough and early recovery: the NBER dates the trough to March, after the November 1973 peak, and the New York Fed identified recovery beginning in the second quarter, so annual output could fall, by 0.28 percent in MPD and 0.21 percent in WDI, even as second-half production recovered. Consumer durables, autos, steel, construction, and inventories were weak, and unemployment rose to 8.5 percent. Inflation eased from 1974, with annual CPI inflation at 9.1 percent, but remained far above the postwar norm. Monetary policy was easier than in 1974, with the discount rate cut to 6 percent by mid-May and federal funds averaging 5.82 percent, and fiscal policy leaned countercyclical: central-government net borrowing widened to 2.4 percent of GDP. Policymakers still feared reigniting inflation, and a June-October tightening interrupted the easing.

New York City's loss of access to short-term refinancing exposed a distinct municipal maturity and budget problem, not a national sovereign default. State intervention through the Municipal Assistance Corporation, pension-fund and bank purchases, and a three-year moratorium on certain notes preceded the federal law of 9 December, which provided seasonal loans maturing within the city's fiscal year at one percentage point above matched Treasury yields, with a US$2.3 billion outstanding cap.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Auto producers faced weak demand and energy-efficiency pressure; oil and gas companies retained pricing power and strategic influence; banks adapted to a larger role in international dollar intermediation, while real-estate investment trusts and bank loan losses remained concerns. The personal-computer sector was tiny, but the Altair 8800 and the founding of Microsoft marked a long-run shift toward software and microcomputer platforms. The JST equity reconstruction shows a nominal total return of 37.7 percent, and San Francisco's asking-price index rose 10.6 percent.

The later term Dutch disease is often associated with the macro effects of gas revenues, though using that term for 1975 should be understood as hindsight.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Gas, ports, logistics, finance, food processing, and chemicals were major sectors. Wage bargaining and welfare-state costs were central to policy. Rotterdam's logistics role remained strategically important. Equities rebounded, with a JST nominal total return of 47.5 percent.

### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Labor markets diverged. In advanced economies, unemployment rose and union conflict increased; annual unemployment reached 8.5 percent in the United States and 6.9 percent in Canada. Wage bargaining was central because inflation reduced real purchasing power. Countries with formal wage indexation or strong collective bargaining, including Italy and parts of Western Europe, found disinflation more difficult. In Canada, wage-price controls became a major policy experiment. In Argentina, average real wages fell 2.7 percent despite large nominal awards, while deposit holders also faced negative real returns.

In developing economies, the deeper issue was labor absorption. India, Indonesia, Brazil, Mexico, Iran, and Turkey had large and growing workforces. Education and urbanization were improving, but formal-sector job creation often lagged population growth; in India, agricultural input availability and irrigation constrained productive capacity as much as labor numbers. ## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The active crisis was global stagflation. It combined recession, inflation, unemployment, and external imbalances. The direct shock came from oil and earlier monetary tightening, but the persistence came from wage-price dynamics, indexation, weak confidence, and currency instability.

The second crisis was balance-of-payments fragility. Oil importers needed adjustment or financing. Petrodollar recycling made financing available, but it also shifted risk into banks and sovereign balance sheets. With hindsight, the borrowing of the mid-1970s was an early warning for the 1980s debt crisis, especially in Latin America.

The third crisis was political economy. Argentina's Rodrigazo, Canada's wage-price controls, Britain's inflation and labor conflict, Spain's transition, and Indonesia's Pertamina crisis all showed that macro shocks became institutional stress tests. New York City combined a structural budget gap with an inability to roll short-term debt; the Municipal Assistance Corporation exchanged financing instruments and mobilized state, bank and pension support, and a three-year moratorium on certain notes meant that not every contractual payment was made on schedule. The 9 December federal law supplied conditional seasonal liquidity rather than an assumption of city liabilities. In planned economies, the visible risk was different: weak consumer supply, hidden inefficiency, and unreliable price signals.

The main near-miss was that the 1975 recession did not become a depression. Policy easing, automatic stabilizers, and the inventory cycle helped generate recovery in 1976. The unresolved vulnerability was that recovery occurred before inflation had been structurally defeated.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1975 vs. 1974)

1974 was the year when the oil shock and inflation surge hit with full force; 1975 was the recession trough and the first phase of policy response. The major delta was from inflation acceleration to output contraction with still-high inflation. In industrial economies, the question shifted from how to contain overheating to how to restart growth without validating inflation.

World trade moved from expansion to outright decline. The IMF described global trade volume as down roughly 4 to 5 percent from 1974 to 1975, and the BIS measure fell about 6 percent. Industrial production and durable-goods consumption were weaker than in 1974, while inventories were liquidated. This made 1975 more severe for manufacturing and logistics than the first oil-shock year alone.

Monetary policy pivoted toward easing in the United States, Japan, and Germany. The U.S. federal funds rate fell over the year, with a mid-year tightening interlude; the Bank of Japan cut the discount rate in four steps; the Bundesbank lowered its discount rate from 6 percent to 3.5 percent. The United Kingdom had less room because inflation and sterling pressure remained severe.

Economic power shifted further toward energy producers and surplus holders, although their surpluses shrank as imports grew. The policy risks that emerged in 1975 were not all immediate: bank recycling of oil surpluses, sovereign borrowing, and state-enterprise contingent liabilities became more important. In hindsight, Brazil's continued debt-financed growth, Mexico's external imbalance before 1976, and Indonesia's Pertamina bailout were all warning signs. The MPD top twenty had no entries or exits.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

Highest-confidence data in this document include the Maddison Project Database country GDP, population, and GDP per capita estimates as a consistent historical framework; IMF and BIS descriptions of the 1975 global recession; official central-bank policy-rate series for the United States, United Kingdom, Japan, and Germany; and World Bank CPI inflation values where the series reports a 1975 observation.

Medium-confidence data include top-20 aggregate GDP comparisons, because ranking by 2011 international dollars is a defensible historical method but not identical to current-dollar national accounts. World Bank current-dollar GDP cross-checks confirm the relative scale of major market economies, but they cannot directly solve the USSR, China, Poland, and other non-market comparability problem. The top-20 sum of about 21.35 trillion 2011 international dollars is a sum of country estimates, not an official world aggregate.

Lowest-confidence areas are Soviet-bloc and planned-economy GDP levels, China-specific macro indicators, unemployment comparability, government debt/GDP, current-account balances, budget balances, and exchange rates for controlled or non-market economies. For the USSR, China, and Poland, a practical error band of plus or minus 20 to 30 percent should be assumed for aggregate GDP levels. For unemployment, definitions vary so much that this document does not rank countries.

Several sources conflict. Contemporary IMF reporting of about -0.5 percent world growth differs from the current WDI figure of 0.81 percent, and IMF and BIS trade-volume estimates differ in coverage. Some published annual price figures for 1975, such as 7.2 percent for Japan, 25.8 percent for the United Kingdom, 33.9 percent for Brazil, -1.1 percent for China and 197.7 percent for Argentina, do not match the WDI annual-average CPI series used here; Brazil and China have no WDI CPI observation, and Argentina is shown on the central bank's December-to-December basis. The German discount rate fell from 6 percent to 3.5 percent; the 8 to 4.5 percent path describes the Lombard rate. Growth reconstructions diverge widely for Spain, Indonesia and Iran, and Argentina's contemporary -2 percent estimate differs from both MPD and WDI.
