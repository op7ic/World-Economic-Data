# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1906

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1906 sat near the high-water mark of the pre-1914 global economy. The dominant macro regime was the classical gold standard, with sterling-centered finance, high international capital mobility, expanding ocean shipping and rail networks, and deepening trade links between industrial cores and commodity exporters. The year was not a global depression or war year. It was a year of expansion under a rigid monetary order, but one in which financial strain was building beneath the surface. The Federico-Tena reconstruction shows world merchandise exports rising 5.54% in volume and 9.99% in current-dollar value.

The largest structural story was the continuing spread of the Second Industrial Revolution. Steel, railways, electric power, chemicals, oil, telegraphy, telephony, and large-scale corporate organization were reshaping production. The United States was the largest single economy in Maddison-style PPP terms, and the Maddison Project Database 2023 records especially strong real growth in the United States (+11.53%) and Japan (+11.25%). China and India remained among the largest economies because of their vast populations, but their GDP per person was far below that of the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, France, Belgium, Canada, Australia, and other high-income economies. Industrial strength was uneven: U.S. steel output rose 16.85% while U.S. petroleum volume fell 6.10%, and French coal output declined while French steel rose.

The key 1906 shock was the San Francisco earthquake and fire of 18 April. Its human and local economic consequences were concentrated in California, but its financial aftershock was international. British insurers paid large claims, gold flowed toward the United States, the Bank of England raised Bank Rate in steps to 6% by 19 October, and transatlantic credit conditions became more fragile. That tightening did not itself create the Panic of 1907, but it helped form the monetary environment in which the later panic became more severe. Monetary conditions did not move uniformly: the Bank of Japan cut its commercial-bill rate from 8.03% to 6.57% in July.

The commodity world also changed in ways that mattered. Brazil adopted a coffee valorization policy under the Taubate Agreement, an early example of state-backed export price support, and separately legislated a conversion office in December. Prairie wheat in Canada and Argentina, plantation exports in the Netherlands Indies, cotton and jute in India, silk and textiles in Japan, and coal, steel, and chemicals in Europe all linked local producers to global price and credit cycles. The world economy was integrated, but unevenly integrated: colonial and semi-colonial economies supplied raw materials and labor-intensive goods while metropolitan centers controlled much of the capital, shipping, finance, and technology. Household evidence tempers the aggregate picture: Indian retail rice prices rose 26.04%, and reconstructed real consumption per person fell in Japan, Australia, Mexico and the Netherlands despite output growth.

Data quality is uneven. GDP rankings below the first ten are approximate and sensitive to whether economies are measured as modern territories, sovereign states, colonial possessions, or imperial aggregates. The ranking below uses rounded Maddison Project Database 2023 and Our World in Data style real GDP estimates in constant 2011 international dollars, supplemented by historical aggregation for empires and colonial territories. Annual unemployment, CPI inflation, government debt, current account balances, and budget balances exist in retrospective reconstructions for the main Western economies and Japan, but not for China, the Russian Empire, the Ottoman Empire, Austria-Hungary as a whole, or most colonial territories; those fields are shown as Data not available.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1906

**1. The classical gold standard at peak reach.** By 1906 the major industrial economies operated inside a gold-based fixed exchange rate system or closely related gold-exchange arrangements. The system supported long-distance trade, lower exchange-rate uncertainty, and large European capital exports. Its weakness was rigidity: payments shocks tended to transmit through gold flows, discount rates, and credit contraction. Countries most affected were the United Kingdom, United States, Germany, France, Japan, Canada, Australia, Argentina, Brazil, and India. The forward link is direct: strains visible in 1906 contributed to the credit environment that preceded the Panic of 1907, while the deeper contradiction between fixed exchange and domestic stability became much clearer after 1914.

**2. Industrial scale, finance, and the modern corporation.** 1906 was part of the era in which vertically integrated firms, universal banks, trusts, cartels, and national champions became central to economic life. The United States had U.S. Steel, Standard Oil, General Electric, and large railroads. Germany had Krupp, Siemens, AEG, BASF, Bayer, and other heavy industrial and chemical firms; Deutsche Bank's total assets rose from M 1.557 billion to M 1.813 billion. Japan was building zaibatsu-centered industrial groups. The United Kingdom remained a financial, shipping, and imperial trading hub. The forward linkage was the growing role of corporate finance, securities markets, antitrust politics, and industrial concentration in the 1910s.

**3. A local disaster with global monetary effects.** The San Francisco disaster showed how insurance, gold settlement, and London money markets could transmit a local physical shock into the international financial system. Insurance claims required payments from British insurers, gold moved toward the United States, and the Bank of England raised Bank Rate to defend reserves and the sterling-gold parity. The countries most affected were the United States and the United Kingdom, with knock-on effects in international credit markets. The forward linkage was the recession and panic of 1907, plus later debate over the absence of a U.S. central bank. Destroyed property is a loss of capital stock and should not be read as an equivalent measured fall in annual GDP.

**4. Commodity politics and the birth of formal export support.** Brazil's 1906 Taubate Agreement institutionalized support for coffee prices through public purchases of surplus coffee financed by external borrowing and export taxation. Federal approval on 6 August excluded the proposed conversion office, which required its own statute of 6 December. This was not a modern OPEC-style cartel, but it was a significant early case of state-backed commodity price management. It affected Brazil most directly, but also coffee importers and international lenders. The forward linkage was the long history of Brazilian coffee valorization, fiscal dependence on coffee, and the vulnerability exposed in the 1929 crash.

**5. Colonial and semi-colonial economies as growth mechanisms for imperial cores.** India, the Netherlands Indies, parts of Africa, the Caribbean, and other colonial territories supplied raw materials, plantation goods, and tax revenue. Their integration into world markets was real, but the gains were unequally distributed. Railways, ports, and telegraphs often improved market access while reinforcing extractive trade patterns. The forward linkage was a century-long debate over colonial development, deindustrialization, terms of trade, and political economy.

**6. Migration as a macroeconomic adjustment mechanism.** The era from the late 19th century to 1914 was a major age of mass migration. Labor moved from Europe to the United States, Canada, Argentina, Brazil, Australia, and other settler economies; U.S. immigrant admissions reached 1,100,735 in the fiscal year to 30 June 1906. Migration relieved labor pressure in parts of Europe and supplied labor to expanding agricultural, construction, and industrial sectors overseas. Countries most affected included Italy, Austria-Hungary, Russia, the United States, Argentina, Canada, Brazil, and Australia. The forward linkage was the tightening of immigration regimes after World War I and the long-run demographic consequences for the Americas and Oceania.

**7. Postwar and post-revolution stabilization in Eurasia.** Japan emerged from the Russo-Japanese War with prestige but also fiscal and debt burdens, and in 1906 authorized the purchase of 17 private railway systems over 1906-15. Russia was recovering from war defeat and the 1905 Revolution, with political instability still high. China was in the late Qing reform period, with fiscal pressure, foreign indemnities, rail politics, and a changing commercial economy. These economies were not marginal: China, India, Japan, and Russia together represented a major share of global output and population. The forward linkage was the sequence of upheavals from the Chinese Revolution of 1911 to World War I and the Russian Revolution.

**8. Liquidity became more expensive and public balance sheets absorbed new obligations.** UK official tightening, higher U.S. and German short-market rates and Italy's withdrawal of preferential discounts revealed funding pressure, while Japan's rate reduction was a counterexample. Railway acquisition in Japan, debt conversion in Italy and coffee finance in Brazil redistributed risks among governments, banks, investors and producers. Authorization ceilings, issuance and debt stocks must be kept separate when reading these commitments.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

**World GDP and growth.** World GDP in 1906, using Maddison-style PPP real output in constant 2011 international dollars, is estimated at roughly $3.8 trillion to $4.2 trillion. This is a reconstructed estimate, not a contemporary national accounts figure. The Maddison Project Database 2023 contains only 39 country records for 1906 and lacks a GDP value for China and for the Russian, Ottoman and Habsburg empires as historical units, so the sum of available records is not world output. World real GDP growth for 1906: Data not available.

**World population.** The Federico-Tena world population reconstruction (January 2026 revision) rises from 1,670.9 million in 1905 to 1,685.1 million in 1906 (+0.85%), in both its 1991-border and historical-border versions. These are reconstructions that include interpolation, not census observations.

**Global trade volume.** World merchandise trade was expanding during the 1900-1913 globalization wave. In the Federico-Tena World Trade Historical Database, world merchandise exports were USD 13,359.72 million at current prices against USD 12,146.03 million in 1905 (+9.99%); at 1913 prices they rose from USD 13,894.84 million to USD 14,664.34 million (+5.54%), and the volume index (1913=100) from 74.1593 to 78.2663. The number of polities covered rose from 127 to 129. Services, capital flows and domestic commerce are outside this series. Directionally, trade was driven by European manufactured exports, British and German shipping and finance, U.S. industrial exports, and raw material flows from India, Latin America, settler economies, and colonial Southeast Asia.

**Reserve currency dynamics.** Sterling was the leading international currency and London was the central money market. Gold convertibility and gold points anchored major exchange rates. The U.S. dollar was important but did not yet have the later reserve role it acquired after World War I and especially after World War II. France and Germany held major monetary importance, but London remained the core international financial center. Reserve-currency shares: Data not available.

**Global inflation trends.** Inflation was generally low to moderate in gold-standard core economies, but price behavior varied across countries and commodities. U.S. reconstructed CPI inflation was about 2.2 percent in 1906 in the MeasuringWorth series and 1.88% in the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database. Retrospective CPI series rose 5.51% in Germany, 1.11% in France, 1.97% in Japan, 6.20% in the Netherlands and 10.00% in Belgium, but fell 0.10% in the United Kingdom (Bank of England series) and 2.70% in Spain. A true global inflation rate: Data not available.

**Key commodity prices.** Gold was fixed in the United States at $20.67 per troy ounce under the gold standard. A historical oil price series reports crude oil near $0.73 per barrel in current 1906 dollars; the implicit U.S. production unit value rose from about $0.625 to $0.731 per barrel. Paris wheat prices in the FRED/NBER series ranged from roughly 22.40 to 24.24 francs per 100 kilograms during 1906. Annual quotations in the Federico-Tena commodity price dataset are shown in section 3D. A globally representative commodity index: Data not available.

**Capital flows.** The main direction of international capital was from Europe, especially Britain, France, Germany, Belgium, and the Netherlands, toward the United States, Canada, Argentina, Brazil, Russia, colonial territories, and infrastructure projects. Capital financed railways, ports, government debt, mining, utilities, and plantations. The United States was simultaneously an industrial giant and a still-important absorber of foreign funds, especially during reconstruction and railroad expansion cycles. Retrospective current-account ratios show Britain's surplus widening to 5.73% of GDP and France at 4.77%, while Canada (-8.06%) absorbed capital on a large scale. Comparable worldwide capital-flow totals: Data not available.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The international monetary system was the classical gold standard, not Bretton Woods. Exchange rates among participating currencies were fixed by gold parities and adjusted within gold points. Central banks and treasuries defended convertibility through discount rates, gold reserve management, and sometimes informal market intervention.

The United Kingdom's Bank Rate was a key global signal. FRED's Bank of England quarterly policy rate series shows 4.00 percent in 1906 Q1, 3.67 percent in Q2 and Q3, and 6.00 percent in Q4. The dated path was 4% at New Year, 3.5% on 5 April, 4% on 3 May, 3.5% on 21 June, 4% on 13 September, 5% on 11 October and 6% on 19 October; the calendar mean was 4.27%. This late-year tightening is central to interpreting 1906. It reflected pressure on gold reserves and the sterling-dollar relationship after insurance-related gold flows to the United States.

The United States had no central bank in 1906. Monetary conditions were managed through the Treasury, national banks, private clearinghouses, correspondent banking, and private financiers. This made liquidity elastic in boom times but fragile under stress. The Macrohistory Database short-term market rate rose from 4.44% to 6.54%. Germany, France, Japan, and other central-bank countries operated under gold constraints. The Bank of Japan cut its rate on commercial bills to 6.57% from 8.03% effective 1 July; the Banca d'Italia's official discount stood at 5% at year-end but its use of reduced-rate discounts fell; the Austro-Hungarian Bank's discount moved from 4.5% to 4% on 28 May and back to 4.5% on 28 September. Market short rates rose to 4.04% in Germany, 2.68% in France, 3.84% in Belgium and 3.77% in the Netherlands; these are market rates, not official discount rates.

Equity markets were active but not globally standardized. The Dow Jones Industrial Average ended 1906 at about 94.35, down about 1.9 percent from 96.20 at 1905 year-end, after strong gains in 1905. London, Paris, Berlin, Amsterdam, Brussels, and other exchanges handled large volumes of government bonds, railway securities, mining shares, and industrial issues; reconstructed national equity portfolios generally showed positive total returns (section 6A), but comparable annual index-level data are not available for all exchanges.

Government bond markets were central to international finance. British consols, French rentes, German government securities, Russian bonds, and emerging market sovereign debt were major asset classes. The Bank of England consol yield averaged 2.86% against 2.81% in 1905. Yield curve terminology in the modern sense is not directly comparable. Complete 1906 cross-country yield curves: Data not available.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

There was no single global trade institution comparable to the post-1947 GATT or the WTO. Trade policy was shaped by national tariffs, imperial preferences, bilateral treaties, shipping routes, and colonial regulations. The United Kingdom remained more free-trade oriented than many industrial competitors, while Germany, the United States, France, Italy, Russia, and others used varying degrees of protection. The German-U.S. reciprocal arrangement became effective on 1 March under the U.S. proclamation of 27 February; Spain's customs decree of 23 March set 1 July for its new tariff after a period of objections and revision; Hungary implemented the new tariff and the German treaty by decree of 21 February for 1 March, while Austria used legislative procedure.

Geopolitically, 1906 was marked by fragile peace rather than global war. The Algeciras Conference addressed the First Moroccan Crisis and reduced immediate conflict risk between Germany and France, but it also revealed the tension among European powers. Japan was consolidating its postwar position after the Russo-Japanese War, including influence over Korea. Russia was stabilizing after defeat and revolution. These developments shaped capital markets because sovereign debt, railway concessions, and colonial investment all depended on political risk.

Trade integration continued across the Atlantic and Indian Ocean. British finance and shipping linked Argentina, India, Australia, Canada, South Africa, and the United Kingdom. German exports pushed into global markets. U.S. manufactured output and agricultural exports expanded. Japan was rising as an Asian industrial power, though still far smaller in per capita income than the Western industrial economies. A new Pacific cable to China was completed in April.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Coal remained the dominant industrial energy source. Britain, Germany, the United States, France, Belgium, and Russia depended on coal for railways, steel, electricity, and urban industry. Oil was emerging, especially in the United States, Russia's Baku region, the Netherlands Indies, Mexico, and Persia's developing concessionary landscape, but it had not yet displaced coal as the main energy source. U.S. petroleum production fell from 134.72 million to 126.49 million barrels while its value rose from USD 84.16 million to USD 92.44 million. Coal output rose 13.04% in Germany (excluding lignite), 14.68% in Australia and 12.63% in Canada, but fell 5.00% in France.

Agricultural commodities shaped both macro balances and local politics. Wheat mattered in Canada, Argentina, Russia, the United States, India, Australia, and France. Coffee dominated Brazilian export politics. Cotton linked the U.S. South, India, Egypt, Lancashire, and global textile production. Jute mattered for Bengal and Dundee. Sugar, tea, tobacco, rubber, and tin shaped colonial economies in the Caribbean and Southeast Asia.

Metals were strategic inputs. Steel production was a core measure of industrial power in the United States, Germany, the United Kingdom, France, Belgium, Russia, and Japan; U.S. steel output rose from 20.024 million to 23.398 million long tons (+16.85%). Copper was important for electrification and telecommunications; U.S. copper output rose from 901.91 million to 917.81 million pounds (+1.76%). Gold mining supported monetary reserves and settler-economy development, especially in South Africa, Australia, and North America. A global 1906 gold-production total: Data not available.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Global labor markets were segmented by empire, race, law, skill, and transport cost, but mobility was high by historical standards. European migration to the Americas and Oceania was economically significant. Italy, Austria-Hungary, Russia, and parts of northern Europe were major sources of migrants, while the United States, Argentina, Canada, Brazil, and Australia absorbed labor and population. U.S. immigrant admissions were 1,100,735 in the fiscal year ended 30 June 1906 against 1,026,499 a year earlier (+7.23%); these are gross admissions, not net migration.

Urbanization and industrial employment were rising in the United States, Germany, Japan, Russia, Italy, and parts of Latin America. Agriculture still employed the majority of workers in China, India, Russia, Brazil, Mexico, the Ottoman Empire, and much of Southern and Eastern Europe. Wage comparisons are difficult because real wages, household production, seasonal work, and informal labor were measured inconsistently. Retrospective nominal wage indices rose 3.51% in the United States and France, 4.51% in Germany and 9.90% in Japan, but were flat in the United Kingdom (-0.06%). Harmonized global wage growth: Data not available.

Organized labor was increasingly important. The United Kingdom, Germany, the United States, France, Italy, Australia, and New Zealand had visible labor movements, though legal rights and political leverage varied. Workplace safety, hours, child labor, and union recognition were central issues; France's law of 13 July provided a 24-hour weekly rest, normally on Sunday, and the June Cananea copper strike in Mexico was a major labor conflict. Productivity gains were strongest in mechanized industry, transport, and energy-intensive manufacturing. Historical unemployment estimates differ by vintage: Lebergott gives 0.8% for the United States against 1.7% in the Mitchell-based Macrohistory series, and the Bank of England's labor-force-consistent series gives 5.16% for the United Kingdom against 3.6% in the Macrohistory series. U.S. public-school enrollment rose from 16.468 million to 16.642 million.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States was the largest economy by 1906 in Maddison-style output estimates. Its scale rested on manufacturing, railroads, agriculture, natural resources, and an expanding internal market. The economy had world-leading steel, oil, machinery, meatpacking, and railroad sectors. Corporate concentration was high, and public debate over trusts and market power shaped politics. The Maddison Project Database 2023 shows real GDP rising 11.53%, a rapid expansion rather than a recession; steel output grew 16.85% while petroleum volume fell 6.10%, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average ended slightly lower, so production and security prices did not move together.

The San Francisco disaster of 18 April was the major domestic shock. Rebuilding generated construction demand, but the insurance and gold-flow consequences helped tighten international credit. The United States lacked a central bank, so liquidity depended on the Treasury, clearinghouses, private banks, and leading financiers. This institutional weakness mattered more after 1906 than during the year itself. Money-market rates rose from 4.44% to 6.54%.

Regulation of large network and consumer industries advanced. The Hepburn Act strengthened federal railroad regulation, and on 30 June the Food and Drugs Act and the Meat Inspection Act established new federal controls over food and drug markets. Immigrant admissions of 1,100,735 in the fiscal year to June added to labor supply.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Steel, railroads, oil, electric equipment, agricultural machinery, and consumer manufactured goods led industrial growth; steel output rose from 20.024 million to 23.398 million long tons and copper output from 901.91 million to 917.81 million pounds. Real estate in San Francisco entered a large reconstruction cycle, and newspaper asking-price indices rose 10.53% in New York City and 6.55% in Boston, while New York asking rents fell 4.72% and Boston rents rose 2.74%. Banking and trust companies were growing in importance, but their liquidity structure was fragile; nominal bank loans rose 9.48%. AT&T-associated telephone subscribers rose from 2.241 million to 2.729 million between 1 January 1906 and 1 January 1907, and employees from 74,718 to 90,324. Patent applications rose from 54,971 to 56,482. Reconstructed real consumption per person rose 2.19%.

**Economic narrative:**

China's aggregate GDP was enormous because of population, but GDP per person was low. The economy remained overwhelmingly agrarian, with regional market networks, handicraft production, treaty-port commerce, and a limited but growing modern industrial sector. The late Qing state was attempting institutional reforms, but fiscal control remained fragmented. The Maddison Project Database 2023 records a population of 413.646 million but no GDP value for 1906.

Trade, railways, foreign concessions, and indemnity payments shaped the macro environment. Treaty ports such as Shanghai linked Chinese merchants to global trade and finance. Silver-based monetary arrangements made exchange conditions different from gold-standard economies. Foreign capital and political concessions were important, but modern national accounts are not available.

Reform policy reached social markets as well: an imperial edict of 20 September set the goal of abolishing opium within ten years, a target rather than an immediate change. A new Pacific cable link was acknowledged complete in April.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Agriculture, textiles, handicrafts, inland trade, and treaty-port services dominated. Modern factories in textiles, ship repair, and light industry were present but small relative to total output. Railway finance and rights were politically sensitive. Consumer markets remained local and regional, with large differences between treaty-port cities and inland rural economies. The opium-suppression program signaled future pressure on a large cultivation and trading sector.

### 5C. Innovation & technology

Electrification continued to transform factories, lighting, urban transit, and communications. Electric motors allowed more flexible factory layouts than steam-line shafting. Telephone networks expanded in large cities, while telegraphy remained vital for international finance and trade. Copper demand reflected this communications and electrification boom.

Internal combustion and petroleum were rising but not yet dominant. Automobiles were still a small share of transport, but firms in the United States, France, Germany, Italy, and Britain were developing the industrial base. Aviation was experimental. Railways and steamships remained the main long-distance transport technologies. HMS Dreadnought applied steam turbine propulsion to a capital ship.

Chemical and materials industries were major productivity drivers. Germany led in synthetic dyes, chemicals, and applied research. Steel processes and large-scale rolling capacity supported railways, bridges, ships, and machinery. These technologies had direct macro implications because they increased fixed investment demand and strengthened the industrial economies in export competition. U.S. patent applications rose from 54,971 to 56,482 and patents and reissues from 30,399 to 31,965; these counts do not measure patent quality or R&D spending. A Danish ordinance of 14 August extended reciprocal industrial-design protection to specified British and U.S. articles.

Infrastructure megaprojects were economically important. The Panama Canal was under U.S. construction. Railways expanded in Canada, Russia, India, Argentina, and Africa. Ports, urban water systems, and electric tramways improved market integration and city productivity.

### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

The global labor market was moving from rural to urban work in industrializing economies, but the majority of workers worldwide still worked in agriculture. Rural underemployment, informal work, and household production make modern unemployment rates unsuitable for 1906 in most countries; harmonized global unemployment is Data not available.

Wage dynamics varied sharply. Industrial workers in the United States, Australia, Canada, the United Kingdom, and parts of Germany earned far more than rural workers in India, China, Brazil, or the Ottoman Empire. Migration responded to these wage gaps. Remittances became important for Italy and other migrant-sending regions.

Education and skills were increasingly important. Germany's technical education system supported chemicals, engineering, and electrical goods. Japan used state policy and education to build human capital. The United States expanded public schooling and practical engineering; enrollment rose 1.05% and average attendance from 11.482 million to 11.712 million (+2.01%). In colonial economies, education access was narrower and often shaped by administrative priorities rather than broad industrial upgrading.

Unions and labor movements were active in many industrial economies. Australia and New Zealand had advanced labor legislation by global standards. Britain and Germany had organized labor and socialist politics. The United States had powerful craft unions and industrial conflict. France's weekly-rest law and Mexico's Cananea conflict show changes in hours and bargaining that aggregate unemployment misses. Labor institutions affected wages, hours, and political reform but did not yet produce modern welfare states in most countries.

### 6C. Currencies

Major gold-standard currencies were fixed through gold parity. Sterling-dollar parity was about $4.866 per pound; the Bank of England annual average was USD 4.851 against 4.865 in 1905 (-0.29%). The U.S. dollar was linked to gold at $20.67 per troy ounce. The German mark, French franc, Belgian franc, Canadian dollar, Australian pound, and Japanese yen were tied to gold or sterling-gold arrangements in different ways; Bank of Japan December demand-draft averages moved from 49.801 to 49.375 USD per 100 yen. Many colonial currencies were linked to metropolitan systems.

Silver and gold-exchange arrangements complicated currency conditions in China, India, Mexico, and parts of Asia. India operated under a gold-exchange standard linked to sterling; Council bills averaged 16.084 pence per rupee in fiscal 1906-07 against 16.043 pence. China had a fragmented silver and local-currency monetary environment. Mexico's transition from silver instability toward gold-standard credibility was economically important; the peso moved from 2.02 to 1.99 per dollar. Brazil's conversion office fixed its notes at 15 pence sterling per milreis.

Capital controls in the modern sense were generally limited in core gold-standard countries, but colonial controls, administrative rules, and banking practices shaped capital flows. A comprehensive 1906 capital-control index is Data not available.

### 6D. Alternative assets

Gold was not an alternative asset in the modern floating-price sense for gold-standard countries; it was the monetary anchor. The price was fixed by law in the United States at $20.67 per troy ounce. The economic issue was not gold's market appreciation, but reserve flows and convertibility pressure. Silver rose 10.97% in London.

Real estate was important but local. San Francisco reconstruction after the earthquake and fire was the major property-market event. Prairie land in Canada, Pampas land in Argentina, coffee land in Sao Paulo, and urban land in New York, London, Berlin, Paris, Buenos Aires, Sydney, and Toronto were important investment classes. Newspaper-based asking-price indices (1948=1) rose from 0.3240 to 0.3582 in New York City (+10.53%) and from 0.3055 to 0.3255 in Boston (+6.55%); asking rents fell 4.72% in New York and rose 2.74% in Boston. These samples are taken mostly in late May and are not completed-sale prices. Sampled national house-price indices in the Macrohistory Database rose 20.28% in the United States, 5.56% in Australia and 1.20% in France, and fell 8.25% in the United Kingdom. Comparable transaction-price indices for most markets: Data not available.

Other alternative assets included mining shares, plantation concessions, railway land grants, and commodity inventories. These assets were highly cyclical and dependent on global credit, shipping, and commodity prices.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The San Francisco earthquake and fire was the largest active economic shock of 1906. Its local effects included damage to property, transport, municipal infrastructure, housing, and business operations, and Congress made emergency relief appropriations. Its global effect came through insurance claims, gold flows, and London money-market tightening. This link is one of the clearest examples of a physical disaster transmitting into a monetary shock under the gold standard.

The main systemic vulnerability was financial fragility in a world of fixed exchange rates and limited lender-of-last-resort capacity. The United States was especially vulnerable because it lacked a central bank. Banks and trust companies were connected through securities loans, correspondent balances, and seasonal currency flows. With hindsight, 1906 was part of the buildup to the Panic of 1907. That hindsight should not be confused with what was fully knowable at the time. Rising London rates and Italy's shrinking use of reduced-rate discounts exposed funding pressure during the year.

Commodity concentration was another risk. Brazil's coffee policy stabilized prices in the short run but increased dependence on external loans and state-backed inventories, creating crop, price and rollover exposure. Argentina, Canada, Australia, Russia, India, and the Netherlands Indies were all exposed to commodity price and harvest cycles. Colonial territories bore risks without equal political control over policy. Indian retail grain inflation created a measurable affordability pressure, and Japan's railway purchases created prospective public obligations.

Geopolitical risk was present but did not become a major war in 1906. The Moroccan crisis was contained diplomatically, Japan and Russia were adjusting after war, and China faced internal reform pressures. With hindsight, these tensions were part of a wider pre-1914 geopolitical structure that proved unstable, but a global war was not a 1906 macro event.

Policy mistakes visible in retrospect include overconfidence in private liquidity arrangements, underestimation of trust-company fragility in the United States, and the assumption that gold-standard adjustment would remain politically and financially manageable. These were not universally seen as errors in 1906, because the gold standard was widely regarded by elites as a stabilizing institution.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1906 vs. 1905)

The biggest shift from 1905 to 1906 was tighter monetary pressure in the international system. In 1905 U.S. equities had risen strongly, while 1906 brought a modest Dow decline (96.20 to 94.35) and late-year credit tightening; UK closing Bank Rate rose from 4% to 6%, while the Bank of Japan's commercial-bill rate fell from 8.03% to 6.57%. The San Francisco disaster was the event that most clearly changed the financial environment.

Brazil's policy regime changed materially. The Taubate Agreement marked a new phase of coffee valorization and state-backed commodity intervention. Brazil also moved toward gold-standard monetary arrangements, aligning currency stabilization with export-sector politics and foreign borrowing. Italy converted its consolidated debt to lower future coupons.

Japan moved from wartime victory to postwar consolidation, with real GDP growth swinging from -2.17% to +11.25%. The economic issue shifted from mobilization and war finance toward debt management, colonial influence, railway expansion, and industrial upgrading. Russia moved from revolutionary crisis toward uneasy stabilization, though social and political tensions remained.

The industrial trend accelerated rather than reversed. The United States and Germany continued to gain relative industrial weight; U.S. growth rose from 7.40% to 11.53%. The United Kingdom remained financially dominant but faced stronger industrial competition. China and India remained huge in total output but did not close the per capita income gap with industrial economies. World merchandise trade volume grew 5.54%.

Migration and settlement economies remained powerful. Canada, Argentina, Australia, and the United States continued to absorb migrants and capital, although Argentine growth slowed from 13.20% to 5.01% and Mexico's output fell 1.10% after a 10.48% rise. Commodity-export economies benefited from global demand, but their vulnerability to credit and price cycles increased.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

**Reliable areas.** GDP and population estimates are strongest for the United States, United Kingdom, Germany, France, Belgium, Netherlands, Australia, Canada, and Japan relative to the global sample. Even there, the figures are reconstructed historical national accounts, not modern national accounts produced in 1906. Bank of England Bank Rate, U.S. reconstructed CPI, Paris wheat prices, and Dow Jones year-end data are relatively well documented, as are dated laws, official discount-rate schedules, U.S. administrative counts and official Indian and colonial tables.

**Moderate confidence areas.** Italy, Spain, Argentina, Mexico, Brazil, Russia, Austria-Hungary, and the Ottoman Empire have useful historical data but more uncertainty. Boundary changes, imperial structures, fiscal fragmentation, informal production, and incomplete price data reduce comparability. Retrospective CPI, debt, current-account, budget, credit and equity-return series from the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database and its rate-of-return extension, and Barro-Ursua consumption series, are reconstructions of moderate quality.

**Low confidence areas.** China, India, Indonesia, and other colonial or semi-colonial economies have large reconstructed GDP uncertainties. Estimates for China and India are especially sensitive to population, agricultural output, regional prices, and assumptions about non-market and household production. GDP estimates for non-market or weakly monetized sectors should be treated as broad orders of magnitude.

**Ranking conflicts and level inconsistencies.** The top six are relatively stable as large economies, though the internal ordering of Germany, the United Kingdom, and Russia can shift with PPP method, boundaries, and aggregation. Ranks 9-20 are much less secure. Austria-Hungary as an imperial aggregate is not directly equivalent to modern-country Maddison observations. India and Indonesia are listed as economic territories under colonial rule. Ottoman estimates are approximate. A modern-territory ranking might differ from an imperial-sovereign ranking. Several rounded levels in the ranking table differ markedly from the Maddison Project Database 2023 values for 1906: the database gives the United States 855.994 billion (85.770 million people, 9,980 per person), the United Kingdom 337.435 billion, Italy 128.905 billion, Japan 109.991 billion and Brazil 20.284 billion 2011 international dollars, and it places the United Kingdom above Germany and Italy above Japan. The ranking order and rounded levels are retained for continuity, but they should not be used to compute growth; the growth column comes directly from the database. The 1905 report uses the 2020 release of the Maddison Project Database, so levels in the two years are not strictly comparable.

**Conflicting series.** U.S. unemployment is 0.8% in Lebergott's estimate and 1.7% in the Mitchell-based Macrohistory series; UK unemployment is 5.16% in the Bank of England series and 3.6% in the Macrohistory series; U.S. CPI inflation is 1.88% in the Macrohistory Database and about 2.2% in MeasuringWorth; the UK long yield is 2.86% in the Bank of England consol series and 2.83% in the Macrohistory series; Japan's discount rate is 6.57% in the Bank of Japan schedule and 7.45% in the Macrohistory series; the UK equity reconstructions disagree in sign. These are not averaged. Market short-term rates for Germany, France, Spain, Belgium and the Netherlands are shown only in notes because they are not official discount rates. The Belgian current-account field in the Macrohistory Database is a merchandise balance and is not shown. German and Australian exchange rates are not shown because the database stores them in later currency units.

**Unavailable or weak data.** Harmonized annual real GDP growth for China and the empires, unemployment outside a few economies, policy rates for countries without central banks, government debt and fiscal ratios outside the Macrohistory sample, stock index levels for most exchanges, bond yield curves, corporate market capitalization, corporate revenue rankings, household debt, consumer confidence, national real estate transaction prices, and sector-level employment are Data not available for 1906. Data not available means that no observation with the stated definition and territory exists in the sources used, not that no historical record exists.

**Weakest claims.** The weakest quantitative claims are the exact GDP levels and order for ranks 11-20, growth rates for colonial and imperial units, and any implied cross-country comparison of unemployment or fiscal balances. The strongest claims are the broad gold-standard regime, sterling-centered finance, the Bank of England late-1906 tightening, the importance of San Francisco insurance gold flows, the role of Brazil's coffee valorization, and the broad industrial pattern of U.S.-German ascent. Values derived from the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database and its rate-of-return extension are used under the CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 license.
