# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 2024

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The defining economic fact of 2024 was that the world economy avoided the recession many forecasters had feared after the 2022 to 2023 inflation shock. Global growth settled near 3 percent, slower than the pre-pandemic norm but more resilient than expected. The United States expanded faster than most other advanced economies, China hit its official growth target while fighting deflationary pressure and a property-sector correction, India remained the fastest-growing large economy, and the euro area was held back by weak manufacturing, high energy costs, and fiscal constraint.

The macro regime shifted from synchronized tightening to cautious easing. Inflation fell in most large economies, but services inflation and wage growth remained sticky enough to delay or slow rate cuts. The Federal Reserve cut late in the year, the European Central Bank began easing in June, the Bank of England cut in August and November, while the Bank of Japan moved in the opposite direction by ending negative-rate policy and raising rates. This made 2024 a transition year rather than a clean return to pre-2020 monetary conditions.

The microeconomic story was dominated by artificial intelligence investment, energy transition supply chains, resilient services consumption, and continuing stress in interest-rate-sensitive sectors such as commercial real estate and property development. Large technology firms captured an exceptional share of equity-market returns, while banks generally stabilized after the 2023 regional-bank shock but continued to face unrealized bond-loss and commercial-property exposures. Manufacturing was uneven: strong in semiconductors, defense-related production, and some emerging-market supply chains, weak in Germany and parts of Europe, and mixed in China because export strength coexisted with domestic demand weakness.

Geopolitics was unusually visible in economic data. Red Sea shipping disruption, Russia-Ukraine war effects, Middle East conflict risk, sanctions, export controls, and tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles, batteries, solar products, and semiconductors changed trade flows and corporate planning. The year also marked an acceleration in industrial policy: the United States, European Union, China, India, Japan, and South Korea all used subsidies, tariffs, procurement, and strategic investment to shape technology, energy, and defense supply chains.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 2024

**1. Disinflation without a global recession.** The historical significance of 2024 is that large central banks brought inflation down from 2022 peaks without a synchronized collapse in employment. The United States combined real growth near 3 percent with lower inflation, while Europe achieved weaker growth and disinflation. Emerging markets were mixed: Brazil and Mexico still faced restrictive policy, India saw moderate inflation with strong growth, and Türkiye remained an outlier with very high inflation. The forward linkage was the 2025 debate over whether easing could continue without reigniting inflation.

**2. Divergent monetary cycles.** Most advanced-economy central banks moved toward easier policy, but Japan tightened after decades of near-zero rates. The Federal Reserve ended 2024 with the federal funds target range at 4.25 to 4.50 percent, the ECB deposit rate at 3.00 percent, the Bank of England rate at 4.75 percent, and the Bank of Japan short-term policy rate near 0.25 percent. This divergence mattered for exchange rates, bank funding, bond yields, and international capital flows. It also signaled the end of the globally synchronized emergency policy cycle that began in 2020.

**3. AI, semiconductors, and intangible capital as macro forces.** The AI investment cycle moved from a financial-market theme to a real-economy driver. Data centers increased electricity demand, semiconductor firms and cloud platforms drove capital expenditure, and AI-related equity concentration became a macro-financial risk. The United States, Taiwan, South Korea, Japan, the Netherlands, and China were most affected through chips, equipment, cloud infrastructure, and export controls. The forward linkage is the possibility that AI boosts productivity, or alternatively that it becomes a capital-spending bubble with uneven diffusion.

**4. China property weakness and global industrial overcapacity.** China reached around 5 percent growth, but private demand, housing, and local-government finance remained under pressure. At the same time, Chinese manufacturing capacity in electric vehicles, batteries, solar panels, and some industrial goods intensified trade tensions. The United States raised Section 301 tariffs on several China-linked strategic sectors, and the European Union imposed countervailing duties on battery electric vehicles from China. The forward linkage was a more fragmented trade system and a sharper distinction between consumer-price disinflation and geopolitical supply-chain risk.

**5. Fiscal space became a central constraint.** High public debt and higher real interest costs put pressure on both advanced and emerging economies. The United States ran a large fiscal deficit despite solid growth; France breached deficit expectations; Italy and Spain continued consolidation under European fiscal scrutiny; Brazil and Mexico faced fiscal credibility questions; and Russia sustained war-related spending despite sanctions. Debt service costs constrained policy more than in the 2010s and made bond markets more sensitive to fiscal news.

**6. Energy transition and energy security advanced together.** World energy demand rose in 2024, with electricity demand growing especially quickly. Renewables continued expanding, but oil, gas, and coal also remained important. Brent oil averaged near 80 dollars per barrel, gold averaged a record-high annual price, and power-system investment became a strategic priority. The countries most affected included China, the United States, India, Saudi Arabia, Russia, Germany, Indonesia, and Australia. The forward linkage was a more capital-intensive energy system shaped by climate policy, data centers, grid bottlenecks, and geopolitics.

**7. Trade recovered, but openness weakened.** Global goods and services trade volume recovered from weak 2023 conditions, but growth stayed below the high-globalization era. Trade became more regionalized and policy-conditioned. Shipping disruptions, sanctions, export controls, tariffs, and industrial policy mattered more than pure cost minimization. The most affected economies were China, the United States, Germany, Mexico, Vietnam outside the top 20, India, and the European Union. The forward linkage is a world where trade still grows, but less predictably and with more political conditionality.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

World output grew at roughly 3 percent in 2024, with the World Bank's January 2025 Global Economic Prospects estimating global growth near 2.7 percent and the IMF placing global growth slightly above 3 percent depending on data vintage and aggregation method. In the October 2026 release of the World Bank World Development Indicators, world GDP was US$111.669 trillion in current dollars, world real growth 2.90 percent and world population 8.141 billion. Trade volume rebounded after the weak 2023 goods cycle: published summary extracts of the WTO's April 2025 Global Trade Outlook report merchandise trade volume growth of 2.9 percent and commercial-services volume growth of 6.8 percent, two separate measures rather than one combined trade-growth rate. Global inflation continued to fall from 2022 to 2023 highs but remained above pre-pandemic norms, especially in services. The dollar remained the dominant reserve currency, with the euro second and the yen, pound, renminbi, Canadian dollar, Australian dollar, and Swiss franc far smaller shares. International capital flows favored dollar assets, AI-linked equities, high-grade debt, and selected emerging markets, while global foreign direct investment remained subdued after excluding conduit flows.

Key commodity prices reflected a world that was neither in recession nor in a new commodity supercycle. Brent crude averaged around 80 dollars per barrel, lower than 2023 but still elevated by pre-2020 standards. Gold averaged roughly 2,386 dollars per troy ounce and repeatedly reached record highs. Copper rose during parts of the year on energy-transition and China-stimulus expectations but remained sensitive to manufacturing demand. Agricultural staples were mixed: grain prices generally eased from war-shock extremes, while weather and regional supply conditions kept food inflation important in several economies.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The United States stayed restrictive for most of the year and cut only after clearer disinflation. The euro area cut earlier as growth weakened. The United Kingdom eased cautiously after inflation fell from very high levels. Japan ended negative-rate policy, making it the clearest major exception to global easing. Bond markets were volatile because investors had to price both rate cuts and persistent fiscal deficits. Yield curves remained unusually shaped in the United States for much of the year, with short rates high and long rates sensitive to inflation and Treasury supply.

**Decisions and implementation dates.** The Federal Reserve's 18 September decision took effect on 19 September, setting the federal funds target range at 4.75 to 5.00 percent with interest on reserve balances at 4.90 percent. The 18 December decision took effect on 19 December at 4.25 to 4.50 percent, with interest on reserve balances at 4.40 percent; these are distinct instruments. Balance-sheet runoff caps remained 25 billion dollars per month for Treasuries and 35 billion dollars for agency debt and mortgage-backed securities, so rate cuts did not end balance-sheet contraction. The ECB's 6 June cut applied from 12 June, taking the deposit facility rate to 3.75 percent; the 12 December decision applied from 18 December at 3.00 percent, with the main refinancing rate at 3.15 percent and the marginal lending rate at 3.40 percent. The Bank of England announced its first cut on 1 August after the meeting ending 31 July, a 5-4 vote to 5 percent, and maintained Bank Rate at 4.75 percent at its December meeting. The Bank of Japan's 19 March decision took effect on 21 March with a 0 to 0.1 percent overnight call rate target and ended ETF and J-REIT purchases; its 31 July decision took effect on 1 August at about 0.25 percent. Ending negative rates and yield-curve control did not end JGB purchases; the planned reduction to about JPY 3 trillion per month in January to March 2026 was a prospective plan, not a 2024 outcome.

Equity markets were strong but concentrated. United States large-cap technology and AI-related firms drove global indices. The S&P 500 rose about 23 percent on a price basis, the Nasdaq Composite rose about 29 percent, and global equities gained strongly but less evenly. Europe was mixed, with Germany outperforming France despite Germany's weak real economy. Japan's equity market reached historically significant levels as corporate-governance reforms, yen weakness, and foreign buying supported valuations. Emerging-market equity performance diverged sharply across India, China, Brazil, Mexico, Türkiye, and Saudi Arabia.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

2024 was marked by trade recovery and trade fragmentation at the same time. The United States increased tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles, batteries, solar cells, semiconductors, and selected medical and industrial goods. The European Union imposed definitive countervailing duties on China-made battery electric vehicles. China challenged the EU action at the WTO and continued to use industrial policy to support strategic sectors. Sanctions on Russia continued to reshape energy, shipping, and finance. Red Sea shipping disruptions increased freight uncertainty and lengthened some routes. Regional integration and nearshoring supported Mexico, India, Southeast Asia outside the top 20, and parts of Eastern Europe, but the gains were not uniform. The 2025 tariff regime and AI investment programs announced in 2025 are not 2024 events.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil markets were shaped by OPEC+ supply management, resilient consumption, Middle East risk, and non-OPEC supply growth, especially from the Americas. Saudi Arabia's fiscal and growth outlook remained tied to oil production limits and Vision 2030 investment. Russia redirected more trade under sanctions and sold energy through altered channels. Natural gas markets were calmer than in 2022, but European industry still faced higher energy costs than United States competitors. Electricity demand rose quickly because of cooling demand, electrification, data centers, industrial activity, and emerging-market growth. Renewables supplied a large share of incremental electricity, but fossil fuels still carried much of the system.

The IEA's Global Energy Review 2025 estimates that total energy demand rose 2.2 percent in 2024, electricity demand 4.3 percent and oil demand 0.8 percent. Cooling, electrification and industrial activity joined data centers as drivers, so attributing all electricity growth to AI would be incorrect. Renewables and nuclear met 80 percent of incremental electricity generation, an increment share rather than their share of total primary energy.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Global unemployment stayed around 5 percent, but labor-market conditions were not uniform. The United States had low unemployment but a cooler labor market than in 2023. Europe kept employment relatively resilient despite weak output. China faced youth employment concerns and underemployment pressure. India benefited from demographic scale but still faced job-quality and female-labor-force-participation challenges. Japan and Germany remained constrained by aging populations and skill shortages. Migration continued to matter economically: it supported labor supply in North America and parts of Europe, but it also became politically contested.

**Household real incomes.** In the United States, BLS's 15 January 2025 release reported December-to-December real average hourly earnings up 1.0 percent, real weekly earnings up 0.7 percent and average weekly hours down 0.3 percent; these private nonfarm employee averages are not median disposable income, and the December figures were preliminary. BLS put December-to-December CPI inflation at 2.9 percent and shelter inflation at 4.6 percent; neither is an annual-average measure. Lower inflation meant slower price increases, not a reversal of the accumulated cost-of-living rise. In Japan, the Monthly Labour Survey of the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, as tabulated by JILPT, reports 2024 total cash earnings growth of 2.8 percent but real wages down 0.3 percent when deflated by CPI excluding imputed rent, against 0.0 percent under all-items CPI. The sample covers establishments with at least five regular employees, and negotiated wage awards did not guarantee positive economy-wide real wage growth.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States was the strongest large advanced economy in 2024. Household spending, business investment, AI-related capital expenditure, and fiscal impulse supported growth. Inflation slowed enough for the Federal Reserve to start easing, but the economy was not weak enough to justify an aggressive cutting cycle. The main macro tension was a combination of high real rates, large public deficits, and equity-market concentration.

BEA's advance estimate put 2024 growth at 2.8 percent and the October 2026 WDI release gives 2.79 percent, against 2.952 percent in IMF WEO October 2026; the national-account vintages differ and are not averaged.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Technology platforms, semiconductors, cloud infrastructure, and data centers were the leading growth stories. Housing affordability remained strained by mortgage rates. FHFA's house price index for the fourth quarter of 2024 was 4.5 percent above the fourth quarter of 2023, with a 1.4 percent quarterly gain and a 0.4 percent gain in December; these are different periods, and the national headline is neither an annual-average rent change nor evidence of identical conditions in every city or in commercial offices. BLS put December-to-December shelter inflation at 4.6 percent. Regional banks stabilized but commercial real estate stayed a risk. Consumers remained resilient, though lower-income households faced higher delinquency and depleted pandemic-era savings.

China's 2024 economy combined official target achievement with visible domestic stress. Industrial production and exports were stronger than property, household confidence, and private investment. The consumer story remained cautious. The global consequence was rising trade tension over overcapacity and subsidies.

**Economic narrative:**

Germany was the clearest advanced-economy example of structural weakness in 2024. ### 5B. Corporate landscape

The largest companies by revenue continued to include Walmart, Amazon, State Grid, Saudi Aramco, Sinopec, China National Petroleum, Apple, UnitedHealth, Berkshire Hathaway, and CVS Health. Their sector mix shows the 2024 economy's dual structure: consumer distribution, energy, state-linked utilities, technology, healthcare, and financial conglomerates. By market capitalization, United States technology firms dominated global equity value, especially companies tied to AI infrastructure and platforms.

M&A activity recovered selectively. ExxonMobil completed its acquisition of Pioneer Natural Resources, reinforcing the importance of scale in United States shale. Large announced deals included Capital One-Discover in finance and Synopsys-Ansys in software and engineering simulation. IPO markets reopened but remained selective; India was notably active, and Reddit, Puig, Amer Sports, Astera Labs, and Kioxia were among the more visible global offerings. Bankruptcies were concentrated in rate-sensitive, leveraged, or structurally disrupted sectors rather than in a broad recessionary wave.

### 5C. Innovation & technology

AI was the year's most important innovation theme. The economic impact was visible in semiconductor demand, data-center construction, cloud spending, power demand, and equity-market concentration. The most immediate beneficiaries were chip designers, advanced semiconductor manufacturers, memory producers, cloud platforms, and semiconductor-equipment firms. The broader productivity effect remained uncertain in 2024 because adoption outside technology, finance, and professional services was still early.

**AI spending in company accounts.** Microsoft's fiscal year 2024 ended on 30 June 2024; its cash-flow statement records additions to property and equipment of US$44.477 billion against US$28.107 billion in fiscal 2023, a 58.24 percent increase, and it separately reported US$35.4 billion of construction commitments at 30 June. The commitment stock is neither cash capital expenditure paid during the year nor AI-only spending, since corporate infrastructure serves multiple workloads. NVIDIA's fiscal year 2025 ended on 26 January 2025; its revenue was US$130.497 billion against US$60.922 billion in fiscal 2024, growth of 114.20 percent. That period overlaps most of calendar 2024 but includes January 2025, and the US$39.331 billion final quarter is part of, not additional to, the annual total. Supplier revenue is not the same concept as customers' capital expenditure, and neither is domestic GDP value added, but together they show a realized chip-sales and data-center investment boom in 2024.

Other economically important technologies included obesity and metabolic drugs in healthcare, battery manufacturing and recycling, grid-scale storage, green hydrogen pilots, electric vehicles, digital public infrastructure, payments, and defense-related manufacturing systems. Patent and R&D intensity remained concentrated in the United States, China, Japan, South Korea, Germany, and selected European economies.

### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Labor markets cooled but did not collapse. The key sectoral shift was from pandemic-era goods and logistics extremes back toward services, healthcare, government, education, and technology infrastructure. Skill premiums remained high for AI, data engineering, cybersecurity, semiconductors, healthcare, and advanced manufacturing. Union activity remained economically visible in the United States and Europe, especially in autos, logistics, education, and public services. Migration supported labor supply in North America, the United Kingdom, Australia, Spain, and the Gulf, but it also intensified political debate and housing pressure.

### 6B. Fixed income

Government bond markets were volatile. United States 10-year Treasury yields ended near the mid-4 percent range after moving with inflation data, Fed expectations, and fiscal supply. German Bund yields were lower than United States yields because euro-area growth was weaker. Japanese government bond yields rose as the Bank of Japan normalized policy. Emerging-market local-currency bonds diverged: Brazil and Türkiye faced inflation and fiscal concerns, while some Asian markets benefited from lower inflation and currency management.

### 6C. Currencies

The dollar remained strong because United States growth and yields outperformed. The yen was weak and required official attention. The euro was pressured by weak growth and political risk. The renminbi was managed but under depreciation pressure because of capital-flow and growth concerns. The Mexican peso reversed earlier strength after political and fiscal concerns. The Turkish lira continued to depreciate under very high inflation. The Saudi riyal remained pegged at 3.75 per dollar.

### 6D. Alternative assets

Gold was the standout traditional alternative asset, with a record annual average near 2,386 dollars per ounce and strong central-bank and investor demand. Real estate was divided: residential prices were supported by supply shortages in many cities, but affordability was poor; commercial real estate, especially offices in the United States and some European cities, remained under stress. In the United States, FHFA's index put house prices 4.5 percent above a year earlier in the fourth quarter. Crypto assets rose strongly late in 2024, but this report treats them as high-volatility financial assets rather than macro anchors.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

Active crises included the Russia-Ukraine war, Middle East conflict risk, Red Sea shipping disruption, China's property adjustment, high public debt, and lingering commercial-real-estate stress. These were not isolated events; each affected trade routes, energy prices, inflation expectations, fiscal policy, and corporate investment plans. A key 2024 near-miss was that high interest rates did not produce a broad banking crisis, even though vulnerabilities remained.

Systemic vulnerabilities building in 2024 included high sovereign debt in the United States and parts of Europe, very high private and public leverage in China-linked property channels, AI equity concentration, data-center power bottlenecks, and fiscal dependence on commodity revenues in hydrocarbon exporters. With hindsight, the year showed that the post-pandemic economy was more resilient than expected, but also more dependent on fiscal deficits, market concentration, and policy-managed supply chains than a standard soft-landing story implied.

Policy mistakes visible in retrospect are debated. Some economists argue that central banks waited too long to ease, risking unnecessary output weakness. Others argue that premature easing would have risked reaccelerating inflation. Fiscal sustainability was a widely shared concern, but whether deficits during a period of low unemployment and high borrowing costs were excessive is a contested policy judgment rather than a measured consensus.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (2024 vs. 2023)

The biggest structural break from 2023 to 2024 was the shift from inflation shock management to disinflation and selective easing. In 2023 the dominant question was whether high rates would break the economy. In 2024 the question became how quickly central banks could normalize without reviving inflation. The United States proved more resilient than expected, while Germany's weakness became more structural and China's property weakness more persistent.

Trade changed from post-pandemic normalization to explicit strategic fragmentation. Compared with 2023, 2024 had stronger trade-volume growth but more tariffs, export controls, sanctions, and shipping disruptions. Industrial policy moved from legislation and announcements toward implementation in chips, energy, EVs, batteries, and defense supply chains.

Financial markets also changed. The 2023 regional-bank shock faded, but equity-market concentration increased. The AI theme moved from narrative to capital expenditure, earnings, and power demand. Bond markets remained sensitive to fiscal deficits, showing that the low-rate 2010s regime had not returned.

The balance of economic influence shifted further toward the United States and India among large economies, while China remained enormous but more constrained by domestic imbalance and external pushback. Spain outperformed within Europe; Germany underperformed. Oil exporters had to balance production management with diversification investment. Emerging-market credibility increasingly depended on inflation control and fiscal anchors.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

**Scope and data basis.** This report uses 2024 nominal GDP in current United States dollars to identify the top 20 economies. The ranking is anchored to World Bank World Development Indicators and cross-checked against IMF World Economic Outlook data. Some values differ by source vintage, national-account revision, fiscal perimeter, or exchange-rate convention. No Soviet-bloc historical-estimate issue applies to 2024, but Russia and other sanctioned or state-directed economies have larger uncertainty around sector and fiscal data than advanced-economy peers.

Data confidence is highest for the United States, euro-area members, the United Kingdom, Canada, Japan, Australia, Switzerland, South Korea, and the Netherlands, where statistical agencies publish frequent, detailed, internationally comparable data, although the indicator values shown here for Australia, the Netherlands and Switzerland are approximate and are not matched to IMF WEO October 2026 observations. Even there, revisions can alter quarterly growth, fiscal balances, and current-account figures. GDP rankings in current dollars are especially sensitive to exchange rates and later national-account revisions.

Confidence is medium for India, Brazil, Mexico, Indonesia, Saudi Arabia, and Türkiye. The broad GDP and inflation numbers are usable, but labor-market definitions, informality, fiscal perimeter, and off-budget obligations reduce comparability. India uses fiscal-year data (April to March) for many headline releases, which requires caution when mapping to calendar-year GDP. Türkiye's inflation environment makes nominal comparisons difficult. Saudi fiscal coverage in the IMF series is central government despite the generic general-government label, and Saudi unemployment includes expatriates.

Confidence is lower for Russia's sectoral, fiscal, and external data because sanctions, wartime spending, capital controls, and trade rerouting reduce transparency; the IMF's Russian national accounts include staff estimates for trade since 2022. China's headline GDP data are official and detailed, but the property sector, local-government financing vehicles, youth employment, and private-sector confidence require caution. Saudi fiscal and project-level data can be incomplete where public investment funds and state-linked entities carry out quasi-fiscal activity.

**Units and vintages.** The ranking table's GDP levels are current-dollar figures from World Bank WDI and remain the basis of the nominal ranking; the population and per-capita values are rounded approximations. The IMF WEO October 2026 current-price PPP levels in country headers are in current international dollars and serve only as comparators; they are not mixed with current-dollar levels, and on that basis China ranks above the United States and Egypt, Nigeria and Poland, which are outside this report's top 20, would displace Australia, the Netherlands and Switzerland. IMF WEO nominal GDP also differs from the WDI levels used here, for example US$29,381.0 billion against US$28,751.0 billion for the United States, US$18,929.0 billion against US$18,743.8 billion for China, US$4,207.6 billion against US$4,027.6 billion for Japan and US$3,759.8 billion (fiscal year) against US$3,909.9 billion for India; the WDI levels are kept. IMF populations also differ from the rounded figures used, for example Saudi Arabia 35.3 million against about 33 million, Mexico 132.3 million against about 130.9 million and Spain 49.1 million against about 48.6 million. WEO fiscal and external ratios use the IMF's own nominal GDP denominators, not PPP GDP. IMF WEO October 2026, WDI October 2026 and other later releases are retrospective series, not information available during 2024.

**Status of IMF values.** The 2024 values in the country tables fall within the IMF's own latest-actual range for each series (cutoff 2024 or later; for India and other fiscal-year series, fiscal year 2024/25 or later), so they are labelled actual. That label is the IMF's classification, not independent validation, and later revisions remain possible. The IMF reports no latest-actual status for the current-price PPP levels or for the World group composites, so their status is unknown. The IMF commodity price series carry no latest-actual cutoff. BLS December 2024 earnings figures were preliminary when released, and the Bank of Japan's 2026 JGB purchase path is a plan, not an outcome.

Annual changes in policy rates are shown only where a matched end-2023 comparison is available; otherwise the change at the final 2024 meeting or a qualitative direction is given. Exchange rates in all tables are approximate annual averages in local currency per US dollar, not official quotes from a single source, and GDP conversion ratios are not used to derive them.

**Other measurement limits.** The WTO trade-volume growth rates and the State Council Information Office statement on replacement bonds are as reported in published summary extracts of those official releases rather than confirmed against their full texts, and the bond statement does not establish recipient-level disbursement. Actual drawdown of China's housing relending facility, bank pass-through and completed home purchases are Data not available. The corporate revenue list in Section 5B is not reconstructed from a common fiscal-year ranking, market capitalization is a dated stock while revenue is a period flow, and the M&A and IPO events are listed without exact dates and amounts. The equity figures in Section 6A are approximate. The countries in the sector matrix are illustrative rather than a measured performance ranking.

The weakest evidence in this document concerns exact global sector-level performance outside publicly traded firms, standardized annual returns for every major exchange, and globally comparable unemployment across countries with high informality. Important missing data include consistent household balance-sheet stress metrics across emerging economies, real-time local-government debt in China, comprehensive war-economy fiscal detail for Russia, comparable commercial-real-estate exposure across banking systems, a common-vintage global company ranking, a complete inventory of national M&A, IPO and bankruptcy events, a uniform 10-year yield and credit-spread panel, city-level property samples, and comparable patent, R&D, skill-premium and migration-contribution data.
