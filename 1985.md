# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1985

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The world economy in 1985 was defined by disinflation, a maturing recovery from the early-1980s recession, a peaking U.S. dollar, and the approach of an oil-market break. Global nominal GDP was about $12.6 trillion in IMF current-dollar series, while a World Bank-sourced historical table places world real GDP growth near 3.1 percent. The recovery continued, but contemporary IMF and World Bank commentary described it as uneven: industrial-country growth slowed from 1984, European unemployment stayed high, trade growth weakened, and debtor developing countries remained constrained. Contemporary GATT estimates put merchandise-trade volume growth at 3%, following 9.5% in 1984, and modern IMF and World Bank reconstructions also show slower world output growth, although their weighting, coverage and vintages differ. Expansion therefore coexisted with weak commodity earnings and difficult adjustment in heavily indebted economies.

The dominant policy event was the Plaza Accord of September 22, 1985. The United States, Japan, West Germany, France, and the United Kingdom agreed that major non-dollar currencies should appreciate in an orderly way. This did not restore Bretton Woods; it was coordinated management inside a floating-rate system. The goal was to reduce the U.S. external deficit, ease Japanese and West German surplus pressures, and lower the risk of a protectionist trade spiral. The dollar's reversal did not begin at the Plaza meeting: Federal Reserve Bank of New York dealing records show substantial depreciation between the February peak and July, followed by renewed coordinated action after the G5 announcement. On 23 September the dollar fell before intervention began, a chronology that supports an expectations channel alongside actual official sales rather than a claim that intervention alone explains the year's exchange-rate movement.

Energy was the other regime-shift story. Oil prices did not collapse during most of 1985, but OPEC discipline weakened as Saudi Arabia moved from price defense toward market-share defense. This mattered for every top economy. Oil importers such as Japan, West Germany, South Korea, India, and much of Europe stood to gain from lower inflation and better terms of trade. Oil exporters such as Saudi Arabia, Mexico, Iran, and the Soviet Union faced revenue and foreign-exchange risks that became severe in 1986. The U.S. first-purchase crude price averaged $24.09 per barrel, down 6.92% from 1984.

At the micro level, 1985 was a year of divergent adjustment. U.S. finance, services, technology, and defense grew in influence, while tradable manufacturing suffered from the strong dollar. Japan and West Germany remained highly competitive exporters but were exposed to currency revaluation. China and South Korea showed rapid industrialization; Chinese retail spending, investment and household saving expanded together, while rising prices reduced real gains relative to nominal incomes. Brazil and Mexico illustrated the debt-overhang problem: large productive economies where inflation, external debt, and scarce foreign exchange distorted firm behavior. Mexico combined a shrinking external surplus, restricted financing and the September earthquakes; Brazil's recovery did not eliminate high inflation, and Argentina's June stabilization announcement did not erase the year's earlier inflation. Financial liberalization was neither uniform nor equivalent to removing every control: Sweden lifted bank lending ceilings while exchange controls remained, and Japan corporatized NTT while the government initially retained all its shares. Japan's later asset boom, later banking problems and the 1986 oil-price collapse are hindsight, not 1985 outcomes.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1985

**1. Coordinated dollar reversal.** The Plaza Accord was the year's most important macro policy event. It showed that the major floating-currency economies could still coordinate when exchange rates became politically dangerous. The United States wanted relief from a large trade deficit; Japan and West Germany faced surplus pressure; France and the United Kingdom joined to stabilize expectations. The turning point had multiple causes: early-year intervention, changing growth and interest-rate expectations, and September coordination occurred at different times, and the dollar had already fallen from about DM 3.48 on 26 February to DM 2.785 at the end of July. U.S. manufacturers and Japanese and West German exporters faced opposite competitive exposures, and adjustment depended on contracts, pricing and investment rather than an instantaneous trade-balance improvement. The forward link was major: yen and deutsche mark appreciation reshaped 1986 to 1987 trade, monetary policy, and asset markets.

**2. Durable advanced-economy disinflation.** The inflationary regime of the 1970s had largely broken in the United States, Japan, and Western Europe. BIS commentary tied the improvement to lower commodity prices, monetary credibility, and reduced inflation expectations. IMF retrospective CPI inflation was 3.53% in the United States, 2.06% in Japan and 2.08% in West Germany. Yet disinflation did not solve unemployment. Western Europe still faced high joblessness, with IMF rates of 8.70% in France, 11.38% in the United Kingdom and 21.30% in Spain, and the social cost of early-1980s stabilization shaped later debates over labor-market reform. Low Japanese inflation contrasted with extreme Latin American price increases, such as Brazil's 226.03%; country-specific CPI baskets, survey coverage and price controls prevent a single inflation or unemployment league table.

**3. The oil-price regime was cracking.** OPEC had lost market share after conservation, recession, and non-OPEC supply growth. Saudi Arabia's late-1985 shift toward market share changed producer incentives. The full collapse arrived in 1986, but the causal turn belongs to 1985. The countries most exposed were Saudi Arabia, Mexico, Iran, the USSR, Nigeria, Indonesia, and Venezuela. The falling U.S. crude first-purchase price was a national benchmark, not proof of a particular world spot price.

**4. Debt and commodity weakness constrained developing economies.** Latin America was still inside the post-1982 debt crisis. Brazil and Mexico had large industrial bases, but inflation, indexation, external debt service, and weak access to voluntary finance shaped investment and consumption. Commodity-exporting countries faced weaker purchasing power and tighter external accounts. For a debtor, export earnings, import compression, interest obligations and reserve losses could matter more than world GDP growth.

**5. East Asian industrialization accelerated.** Japan was already an advanced industrial leader, but China and South Korea showed the next layer of Asian growth. China expanded through reform-era rural industry and local enterprises. South Korea grew through export manufacturing, state-guided finance, and chaebol scale. This was an early stage of the supply-chain shift that later transformed global manufacturing. The mechanisms differed: China's preliminary GDP, gross-output, retail and agricultural statistics measured different activities; Japan faced exchange-rate adjustment with its posted discount rate unchanged during 1985; and India's targeted delicensing changed entry and capacity constraints without abolishing its regulatory framework.

**6. Financial liberalization increased both efficiency and risk.** The United States saw strong equity returns and more restructuring finance. The United Kingdom was preparing for the 1986 Big Bang. Australia operated with a newly floated currency. Sweden's credit liberalization began changing bank lending: the removal of bank lending ceilings on 21 November could shift business previously outside regulated banks back onto their balance sheets, so rising recorded loans were not automatically new aggregate spending. Interest-rate instruments also differed: discount facilities, market funding rates and bank lending rates are not substitutes. These moves improved financial flexibility but also created later leverage, property, and banking vulnerabilities.

**7. European integration and exchange-rate discipline strengthened.** The European Monetary System constrained policy choices, while Spain and Portugal were preparing to join the European Community in 1986. Europe's core problem was balancing disinflation, competitiveness, and high unemployment. The forward link was the Single European Act period and, eventually, monetary union.

The U.S. dollar remained the central reserve and invoice currency, but its overvaluation became a coordinated policy target. ### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

Major advanced-economy rates generally eased as disinflation became credible. BIS described U.S. monetary relaxation, lower U.S. rates, falling European money-market rates, and lower bond yields. The Federal Reserve announced a discount-rate reduction from 8% to 7.5% on 17 May, effective 20 May. The Federal Open Market Committee's May record describes reserve-pressure operations, not a modern announced federal-funds target; its 6-10% funds-rate range was a consultation trigger rather than an operating corridor. The same record connects dollar strength with pressure on manufacturing, agriculture and extractive industries, while lower rates supported housing and raised concerns about office and multifamily overbuilding.

West Germany retained Bundesbank credibility; EMS countries faced exchange-rate constraints. West Germany used purchase and resale operations and discount and Lombard facilities, and the United Kingdom used money-market operations and occasional official lending; dated 1985 UK and German administered-rate schedules are Data not available in the sources cited, and annual Macrohistory Database short rates are market rates, not administered-rate schedules. Japan faced a surplus and currency-appreciation shock after Plaza. The Bank of Japan's official discount rate on commercial bills and eligible government collateral remained 5% throughout 1985, with 5.25% on other collateral; late-October call-money tightening did not require a posted discount-rate increase, and the next posted cut took effect on 30 January 1986. The United Kingdom combined high unemployment, North Sea oil, anti-inflation policy, and pre-Big Bang financial reform.

Equity markets were strong, with the S&P 500 returning about 31.7 percent in 1985. ### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

U.S. trade deficits and Japanese and German surpluses drove trade tension. Plaza can be read as an anti-protectionist currency agreement as much as a monetary event, but it was not a new fixed-rate system: European Monetary System constraints coexisted with floating dollar exchange rates, and Italy's July exchange-rate adjustment, reported by the BIS, is not an annual lira return. The Cold War shaped defense spending, technology controls, and Soviet-bloc trade. The Iran-Iraq War affected oil, shipping risk, and Middle Eastern budgets. The BIS report distinguishes current-account deficits from their financing, including reserves, official credit and bank claims. Reporting-bank exposures are not total national external debt. Its report extends into early 1986, and some agreements it describes were completed by spring 1986 rather than during 1985.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil was the key commodity. U.S. crude first-purchase prices stayed around the mid-$20s per barrel during much of 1985, but the market was unstable. OPEC discipline weakened, and Saudi Arabia's market-share strategy prepared the 1986 price collapse. Importers gained disinflation and income benefits; exporters faced fiscal and external stress. North Sea oil supported the UK, Soviet oil and gas supported hard-currency earnings, and Middle Eastern producers faced a strategic price-versus-volume tradeoff.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor markets diverged. U.S. employment improved after the early-1980s recession, with IMF unemployment of 7.19%. Japan retained low unemployment by OECD standards, at 2.62%. Western Europe faced high and persistent unemployment. China and India had very large young populations and needed labor absorption outside low-productivity agriculture. Gulf economies relied on expatriate labor tied to oil revenue. Because definitions vary sharply, unemployment is not treated as a directly comparable cross-country statistic: Argentina's older observations derive from May and October surveys, Indonesia's modern reporting conventions cannot automatically be projected backward, missing Soviet unemployment is not zero unemployment, and China's recorded job placements and wage-earner population are not a labor-force unemployment rate.

Chinese survey evidence illustrates purchasing-power differences: rural real income rose 8.4% and employee real wages 4.7%, while nominal measures rose much faster. China's year-end population of 1,046.39 million is not interchangeable with annual-average reconstructions. Japan's statistical table gives 1985 employment shares of 8.8% primary, 34.3% secondary and 56.5% tertiary industry; its footnote includes unclassifiable activity, so the shares need not total exactly 100%. **Economic narrative:**

The United States remained the world's largest economy and the main source of global imbalance. Growth slowed after the 1984 boom, while inflation stayed controlled and interest rates eased. Strong domestic demand and the earlier dollar surge widened the external deficit and intensified trade pressure. The modern-vintage growth slowdown and continuing current-account deficit fit the contemporary Federal Reserve concern over dollar pressure on tradable industries; easier financing could support construction and household demand without immediately repairing manufacturers' export competitiveness. Annual averages conceal the February dollar peak and the September policy event.

The Plaza Accord was the decisive policy event. It aimed to lower the dollar and reduce protectionist pressure without returning to fixed exchange rates. Micro effects were uneven: finance, services, defense, retail, and technology gained, while autos, steel, machine tools, and other tradables faced import pressure.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:**

Leading sectors: finance, business services, defense, software, semiconductors, retail, autos, energy, and construction. S&P 500 total return was about 31.7%. Microsoft's own chronology dates Excel for Macintosh shipments to 30 September and retail Windows to 20 November, and its fiscal 1985 sales were US$140 million; these are product and firm events, not a quantified contribution to national productivity. The FOMC record noted concerns about office and multifamily overbuilding as lower rates supported housing. Newspaper asking-price indices rose 30.99% in Boston and 15.80% in New York City (section 6D); they are not generalized to all U.S. property.

### 6C. Currencies

The dollar reversal was the main currency event. Plaza aimed for orderly appreciation of the yen, deutsche mark, franc, and pound against the dollar. The dollar reached approximately DM 3.48 on 26 February; at 31 July it was DM 2.785, a 19.97% decline in the DM value of one dollar from that peak (the reciprocal gain in the dollar value of the mark is a different figure). This pre-Plaza movement cannot be attributed to the September announcement.

After the G5 announcement of 22 September, the dollar declined on 23 September before official intervention. Tokyo was closed for a holiday; the Bundesbank began intervention at the Frankfurt afternoon fixing, followed by U.S. operations, and Tokyo traded and intervened on 24 September. U.S. dollar sales from August to October totaled US$3,198.7 million, split equally between the Treasury and the Federal Reserve; the 16 October daily operations are included in that total, not additional to it. By the end of October the dollar was approximately 13% lower against the yen, 10.5% against the mark and 8% against sterling than in the week before the meeting; these are neither calendar-year returns nor proof of a single causal intervention effect. Late-October Japanese money-market tightening affected expectations, and the Federal Reserve Bank of New York records official denials of a coordinated interest-rate agreement, so market speculation is not treated as an actual agreement.

In annual-average WDI terms, more local currency was needed per dollar in 1985 than in 1984 for each of these currencies despite the reversal from the February peak: 2.9440 marks (+3.44%), 238.5358 yen (+0.43%), 8.9852 French francs (+2.82%), 0.7792 pounds (+3.65%), 1,909.4392 lire (+8.68%) and 1.4319 Australian dollars (+25.66%). ## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

Active risks included Latin American debt stress, the Iran-Iraq War, high European unemployment, U.S. trade imbalance, oil-market instability, and rising protectionist pressure. The Plaza Accord reduced the risk of a disorderly dollar fall or protectionist spiral, but did not remove global imbalances. Exchange-rate correction itself carried risks: import-price pass-through, exporter margin compression and uncertain foreign demand for dollar assets. The early dollar decline and the later official action do not establish how large the counterfactual movement would have been.

Mexico's September earthquakes were a realized physical shock with funding consequences; Banco de México's account does not provide a national damage percentage. Debtors also faced an adverse combination of weak export purchasing power, constrained finance and uncertain interest burdens. A rescheduling was not debt forgiveness, nor was a decline in bank claims necessarily cash repayment.

The oil-price collapse was the main crisis building in plain sight. OPEC's loss of market share and Saudi Arabia's frustration with price defense were visible, but the full 1986 price fall was not yet realized, and its fiscal losses belong to the next year. Oil-price weakness and producer strategy raised vulnerability, especially for foreign-exchange-dependent exporters. The Soviet exposure to oil revenue, planning inefficiency, and technological lag is a hindsight-labeled vulnerability. Financial liberalization was another hindsight risk: in 1985 it looked like modernization, but later leverage, property, and banking problems showed the downside; Sweden's later banking crisis and Japan's later asset-price bust were not 1985 observed failures. Potential protectionism, continuing geopolitical conflict and unequal labor adjustment remained risks; probability estimates for them are not given.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1985 vs. 1984)

Compared with 1984, 1985 was slower, less balanced, and more policy-coordinated. The United States moved from exceptional rebound toward moderate growth and larger external imbalance. The dollar shifted from a tolerated market outcome to a coordinated policy problem. Oil moved from unstable price defense toward market-share conflict. East Asian industrialization accelerated, especially in China and South Korea. Europe remained constrained by unemployment and exchange-rate discipline, while Spain prepared for EC entry. Brazil and Mexico remained trapped by debt, inflation, and external financing constraints.

World trade lost momentum: GATT merchandise volume growth fell from 9.5% to 3.0%, and the IMF's goods-and-services trade volume growth from 8.587% to 2.638%; the different scopes support the same direction without numerical reconciliation. Modern world output composites slowed, but the IMF PPP-weighted 3.659%, market-weighted 3.706% and WDI 3.660% are not three independent estimates, and current-dollar GDP levels also reflect price and currency changes. The dollar changed direction within 1985 before Plaza, so annual-average exchange rates can move differently from February-peak or September-to-October comparisons.

Mexico's original current-account surplus shrank from a revised US$4,238.5 million to US$541.0 million; its earlier 1984 report gives US$3,967.4 million instead, and the two are different vintages with no common-denominator ratio. Chinese real activity expanded while grain output fell and nominal spending outpaced real spending. Bank regulation and ownership changed in specific stages: Swedish lending ceilings ended, NTT became a government-owned company, and India's delicensing remained conditional. In the Maddison Project Database 2023 records, the selection of the twenty largest records was unchanged from 1984; that selection is not a certified ranking of economies and does not show that no sovereign economy entered or left a complete historical ranking.

The structural breaks were clear: the Plaza Accord changed currency expectations; the OPEC/Saudi shift changed energy expectations; Gorbachev's accession changed the Soviet reform horizon; financial liberalization increased the role of asset prices and credit; and developing-country debt problems persisted rather than resolving naturally with global recovery.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

High-confidence figures are the WDI-style current-dollar GDP observations for most market economies and the broad qualitative claims supported by IMF, World Bank, BIS, UNCTAD, OECD, and central-bank-era sources. Dated events and transcribed source cells are also strong: the Maddison, IMF WEO and Macrohistory Database observations, GATT trade estimates, the Chinese statistical communiqué's definitions, Federal Reserve Bank of New York intervention amounts and dates, the May U.S. discount-rate decision and NTT's ownership chronology. Confidence attaches to those source definitions and dates, not to territorial comparability, causal attribution or the source estimates themselves. Medium-confidence areas include West Germany and UK current-dollar GDP where WDI-style and IMF historical tables differ, China because of transition-economy measurement, Iran because of war and exchange controls, and Saudi Arabia because oil price and production changes dominate nominal GDP.

The weakest figure is the Soviet Union's nominal GDP. It is useful for rank context but not directly comparable with market GDP because of administered prices, non-market exchange rates, secrecy, and planning. No cited source supports the US$2.2 trillion nominal proxy or a 20 to 30 percent uncertainty band; both are rough placeholders for ranking, and the Maddison real aggregate is shown alongside as a cross-check.

**Vintage differences.** The world aggregates in the text come from earlier sources: nominal GDP of about $12.6 trillion matches the current IMF vintage ($12.633 trillion), while the current WDI vintage gives $13.051 trillion; world real growth of about 3.1% from the earlier World Bank-sourced table differs from the current WDI 3.660% and the IMF's 3.659% (PPP weights) and 3.706% (market weights). Header growth rates come from earlier-vintage sources and differ from the IMF WEO rates used in the country tables, most notably for Australia (about 3.7% against 5.46%, with Maddison 5.062%), South Korea (about 7.2% against 7.94%), China (about 13.8% against 13.50%, with the preliminary official 12.5% and Maddison 8.630%), India (about 5.4% against 5.25% for the fiscal year, with Maddison 3.979%) and the United Kingdom (about 4.2% against 4.09%, with Maddison 3.536%). Rounded population levels come from the earlier sources; the Maddison records differ materially for India (755.0 million against about 784 million), China (1,051.0 million against about 1.076 billion, with an official year-end figure of 1,046.39 million), Brazil (137.4 million against about 135 million) and Mexico (76.8 million against about 78 million), and the Maddison Germany record (77.7 million) covers the combined territory rather than the Federal Republic's about 61 million.
