# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1962

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1962 was a strong expansion year inside a still-functioning Bretton Woods order. Maddison world GDP was about 9.14 trillion 1990 international Geary-Khamis dollars, up about 4.7% from 1961. The United States remained the system's largest economy and monetary anchor, but the fastest visible industrial momentum came from Japan, Western Europe, Spain, Canada, and several middle-income economies.

The key macro pattern was growth under fixed exchange rates. The dollar was officially convertible into gold at $35 per ounce, most major currencies were pegged, and capital controls limited destabilizing flows. Yet stress signs were visible: the Federal Reserve opened swap lines, the London Gold Pool defended the gold price, Canada returned to a fixed exchange rate, and U.S. equities suffered the Kennedy Slide.

At the micro level, growth was transmitted through autos, steel, chemicals, housing, electricity, transport, oil, consumer appliances, and early electronics. In market economies, firms, households, and banks converted income growth into durable-goods demand and investment. In the USSR, China, and Poland, plan allocation, official prices, and rationing make standard market indicators weak or non-comparable.

Several 1962 decisions had long forward linkages. The European Common Agricultural Policy began, the U.S. Trade Expansion Act prepared the Kennedy Round, OPEC's early producer-bargaining logic continued, and the Cuban Missile Crisis highlighted the resource burden and tail risk of Cold War rivalry.

Bretton Woods required active management rather than automatic stability. Canada established a new parity in May but still faced reserve pressure and emergency tightening in June. Britain cut Bank Rate three times in the spring, while the Federal Reserve left its discount rate unchanged and attended to external confidence. During the October Cuban missile crisis, London gold trading reached exceptional intensity and the fixing approached $35.20 an ounce; this is evidence of financial stress, not evidence that the year's stock-market decline was caused by the crisis. Growth was uneven: Argentina and Poland contracted, and China's direction of growth is disputed between major retrospective sources.

Corporate and household mechanisms mattered. European consumption and fixed investment grew, automobile producers expanded, and labor shortages required recruitment and housing investment: Volkswagen's foreign workforce rose from 730 to 4,494, and Toyota's worldwide physical production rose 9.20%. Mexico combined public investment and credit expansion with manufacturing growth, while India redirected financial resources toward defense after the border war. EEC internal industrial tariffs were cut further and agricultural market rules began operating; the U.S. Trade Expansion Act created negotiating authority rather than an immediate tariff cut. Italy legislated electricity nationalization, and U.S. drug-efficacy legislation and the Telstar satellite experiments changed the institutional setting of innovation.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1962

**1. Bretton Woods stability required active defense.** The fixed-rate system still worked, but the Gold Pool, Federal Reserve swap lines, and Canadian parity change showed that confidence had to be managed. This theme links 1962 directly to the later 1960s dollar-gold strains.

**2. Catch-up growth was still powerful.** The United States was largest, but Japan, Spain, Italy, France, the Netherlands, Canada, and West Germany grew faster. Capital deepening, technology transfer, exports, and urbanization narrowed parts of the productivity gap with the United States.

**3. Trade liberalization and regional integration accelerated.** The EEC's Common Agricultural Policy and the U.S. Trade Expansion Act made trade policy a strategic Cold War and Atlantic-alliance instrument, not just a tariff issue.

**4. Non-market economies were large but hard to measure.** The Soviet Union, China, and Poland ranked high in Maddison, yet official prices, plan targets, and administrative allocation make GDP, unemployment, exchange-rate, and productivity comparisons uncertain.

**5. Oil politics were becoming institutionalized.** Crude prices were low by later standards, but OPEC's creation and early producer demands over posted prices made petroleum a strategic bargaining issue. Venezuela's top-20 position reflected oil's scale.

**6. Household modernization was a growth mechanism.** Automobiles, appliances, housing, roads, televisions, and electricity converted macro growth into daily living-standard changes in rich and high-growth economies, while developing and socialist economies lagged in different ways.

**7. Financial markets were controlled, but not irrelevant.** Capital controls limited global contagion, yet the Kennedy Slide, Canadian exchange pressure, bond-yield movements, and gold-market defense showed that expectations mattered.

**8. Expansion could increase external vulnerability.** Italy's imports rose with consumer and capital-goods demand while its current-account surplus shrank from $360 million to $130 million. Brazil and Argentina experienced exchange instability alongside much higher city price inflation, and a fixed official or administrative quotation did not guarantee access to foreign currency.

**9. Growth measurement was especially consequential in planned economies.** Soviet national-income and industrial-output statistics are not GDP. China's NBS and Wu/MPD reconstructions disagree even on the sign of 1962 growth (-5.6% against +6.82%), which affects comparisons with 1961, not merely a footnote.

**10. Human capital and complementary investment limited industrial expansion.** Recruitment, worker accommodation and schooling changed effective labor supply. Canadian youth participation reflected education and summer employment, while Volkswagen linked labor recruitment to about DM 40 million of housing expenditure.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

Maddison world GDP was about 9.14 trillion 1990 international Geary-Khamis dollars, with estimated real growth of 4.7%. This is a historical purchasing-power-style estimate in 1990 international Geary-Khamis dollars, not a nominal-dollar aggregate. IMF reporting indicates world trade expanded in value from roughly $95 billion in 1959 to about $124 billion by 1962, with about 5 percent growth from 1961 to 1962. Reserve-currency dynamics were dollar-centered, with sterling still important but secondary. Inflation was moderate in many advanced economies and higher or less reliably measured in several Latin American and developing economies. Oil was cheap and stable, gold was fixed officially at $35 per ounce, and capital flows were dominated by official finance, trade credit, aid, controlled bank lending, and direct investment.

UN World Population Prospects 2024 places 1 July world population at 3,123,374,315, against 3,064,869,675 in 1961, an increase of 58,504,640 or 1.91%. Trade measures must remain separate. The Economic Report of the President 1964 gives world merchandise exports (current dollars, FOB) of $133.4 billion in 1961 and $140.6 billion in 1962, including an estimated $16.9 billion Sino-Soviet-bloc component in 1962. The UN Statistical Yearbook 1964 gives export-volume indices of 124 and 131 (1958=100), implying 5.65% growth, but explicitly excludes Albania, Bulgaria, mainland China, Czechoslovakia, East Germany, Hungary, North Korea, North Vietnam, Mongolia, Poland, Romania and the USSR; an earlier 1953-based series shows 159 to 167 (+5.03%) and is not spliced with it. BIS's $242.7 billion to $256.0 billion is nominal exports-plus-imports turnover, about 5.48% growth. There is no verified global CPI aggregate: modest inflation in several industrial economies coexisted with steep increases in Buenos Aires, São Paulo and Djakarta. Canada's experience showed the importance of capital flows: BIS records long-term inflows of only $10 million in the first half and $660 million in the second.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The major economies operated under Bretton Woods fixed exchange rates. The United States held the discount rate around 3 percent while balancing domestic expansion against dollar-gold credibility. The United Kingdom defended sterling at $2.80. Japan maintained ¥360 per dollar. West Germany, France, Italy, the Netherlands, Switzerland, India, Mexico, and Spain had fixed or tightly managed official parities. Canada returned to a fixed par value of US$0.925 per Canadian dollar in May 1962. U.S. equities corrected sharply, with the Dow down about 10.8 percent for the year, while U.S. 10-year Treasury yields were around 4 percent late in the year.

At year-end, official discount rates were 3% in the United States and West Germany, 4.5% in Britain and 6.57% in Japan. Britain's reductions occurred on 8 March, 22 March and 26 April; Japan cut in late October and November; the Dutch rate rose from 3.5% to 4% on 25 April; Sweden cut by a total of one point in April and June. The Federal Reserve's 18 December adjustment sought a modest reduction in free reserves while the discount rate remained unchanged. BIS reports the U.S. three-month bill rate rising from 2.60% in December 1961 to 2.87% in December 1962, while its long-government-bond yield fell from 4.06% to 3.87%, a narrower long-short spread. Equity prices declined in most reconstructed national series despite output expansion.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The EEC deepened through the launch of the Common Agricultural Policy. The United States passed the Trade Expansion Act, giving the president negotiating authority for broader tariff reductions and preparing the later Kennedy Round. The Cuban Missile Crisis did not create a sustained economic crash, but it showed how Cold War risk could dominate expectations. The Sino-Indian War shifted India's fiscal and infrastructure priorities. Decolonization continued to affect trade, aid, and investment flows.

The EEC entered its second stage on 1 January. On 1 July internal industrial tariffs fell another 10 percentage points of original duties, leaving them at 50% of their original level, and agricultural regulations for cereals, pigmeat, eggs and poultry became operational at the end of July. Greece's association took effect on 1 November. The Trade Expansion Act was enacted on 11 October; its general authority allowed negotiations for reductions of up to 50% of specified existing duties, so enactment itself did not deliver cuts. The Dillon Round concluded in 1962, and the long-term cotton-textile arrangement began on 1 October, showing that liberalization coexisted with sectoral management.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil supply was ample and prices were low, but producer politics were changing. OPEC's founding members were challenging posted-price reductions and seeking stronger control over revenues. Metals demand followed autos, construction, electrification, machinery, and defense-linked industry. Agriculture was uneven: advanced economies improved productivity, while China and India faced food-security constraints. In Europe, the CAP made agriculture a central regional policy issue.

BIS's preliminary physical-output table gives world petroleum output rising from 1,119 to 1,211 million tonnes (+8.22%), natural gas from 509 to 540 billion cubic metres (+6.09%), steel from 362 to 370 million tonnes (+2.21%) and refinery copper from 5.133 to 5.303 million tonnes (+3.31%). Wheat rose from 237 to 261 million tonnes and rice from 242 to 248 million tonnes; sugar was about 52 million tonnes in both years. Prices did not simply follow output: BIS records changes during 1962 of +1.3% for London copper, -9.5% for London tin, -26.6% for Pittsburgh steel scrap and +74.1% for New York sugar, while listed Bradford and Venezuelan petroleum prices were unchanged. New York world-market sugar moved from a January low of 2.05 cents per pound to 4.80 cents at year-end, as Cuban production fell to 4.8 million tonnes from almost 7 million in 1960.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

The Maddison population series puts world population near 3.14 billion. Labor markets were shaped by urbanization, rural-to-industrial migration, and postwar demographic trends. West Germany, France, Italy, Switzerland, and the Netherlands faced labor shortages and migration pressures. Japan absorbed rural workers into factories and construction. India, China, Indonesia, Mexico, and Brazil still had very large agricultural labor shares.

Unemployment was not uniformly low: the reconstructed U.S. rate was 5.5%, Canada's annual rate was 5.9%, down from 7.2%, and West Germany's historical rate was 0.6%. BIS reports an increase of 160,000 foreign workers in Germany during 1962. In Canada, schooling and later labor-force entry offset some of the postwar birth cohort's arrival at working age, and summer student job seeking affected within-year unemployment. A zero unemployment assumption for planned economies would erase underemployment and administrative allocation rather than measure them.

The United States remained the largest economy and the monetary anchor of Bretton Woods.

### 5B. Corporate landscape

A verified global ranking of 1962 companies by revenue or market capitalization is not available. Data not available for a reliable top-20 corporate list. The most important firms were in oil, autos, steel, chemicals, electrical machinery, finance, retail, and telecommunications. Examples include General Motors, Ford, IBM, General Electric, AT&T, Royal Dutch Shell, Unilever, Volkswagen, Siemens, BASF, Bayer, Fiat, Renault, Toyota, Nissan, Sony, Matsushita, Toshiba, Hitachi, and state-linked enterprises in France, Italy, India, China, and the Soviet bloc. The main corporate story was scale, export capability, technology absorption, and state involvement rather than a wave of globally comparable IPOs or M&A.

Italy's ENEL law of 6 December is a documented nationalization case, although completed transfers of every electricity asset by 31 December are not established. SEC filings document securities issuance and Chapter X proceedings, but the SEC fiscal year runs July 1962 to June 1963, and registration statements are neither IPO counts nor completed sales. Fortune's lists cover U.S. industrials only, not a matched global universe.

**Households and banks.** BIS records real private-consumption increases of 4.6% in the United States, 6.8% in France and 6.6% in Italy, and fixed-investment growth of 7.4%, 7.2% and 8.9% respectively. Canadian consumer spending, youth employment and housing displayed different within-year patterns. Mexican credit expansion of 14.5% could support durable purchases and working capital, while rapid inflation in Buenos Aires, São Paulo and Djakarta eroded money balances and wage purchasing power. A cross-country household-debt, confidence or saving-rate panel is not covered by the sources cited.

### 5C. Innovation & technology

The main technologies were mainframe computing, semiconductors, aerospace, telecommunications, nuclear power, chemicals, pharmaceuticals, and industrial automation. U.S. R&D was tied to corporations, universities, defense, and space. Japan's advantage was rapid commercialization and manufacturing learning. Europe specialized in autos, chemicals, rail, aircraft, power systems, and industrial engineering. Telstar 1's 10 July launch and the ensuing transatlantic television demonstrations established a communications milestone, not immediate profitability. The U.S. drug amendments signed on 10 October required demonstration of efficacy as well as safety. The Economic Report of the President 1964, sourced to NSF, estimates U.S. R&D expenditure at $16.42 billion for 1962-63 against $14.74 billion for 1961-62; federal funding was $11.00 billion, industry funding $5.00 billion and university and nonprofit funding $0.43 billion, while industry performed $11.56 billion. The total combines fiscal and calendar observations, so it is not a uniform calendar 1962 figure. Globally comparable patent tables are not covered by the sources cited.

### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Labor shifted from agriculture toward industry and services. The United States was already service-heavy; Europe and Japan were industrializing and urbanizing; India, China, Indonesia, Mexico, and Brazil retained large rural labor forces. Wage growth was strongest where productivity catch-up and industrial labor demand were strongest. Unemployment comparisons are weak because definitions differ and socialist economies reported employment administratively.

Volkswagen's recruitment and housing expenditure show how labor mobility required complementary capital. Canadian schooling reduced young people's participation even as larger birth cohorts reached working age. In Italy, BIS discusses narrowing regional wage differentials and progress toward equal pay. The 11 April U.S. presidential conference addressed the west-coast maritime dispute and instructed the Attorney General to seek an emergency injunction, dated evidence of labor conflict affecting logistics.

### 6B. Fixed income

U.S. 10-year Treasury yields were around 4 percent in the second half of 1962. U.K. 10-year yields were higher, around the 5-6 percent range during parts of the year. JST annual long-rate observations are 3.95% for the United States, 5.98% for Britain, 5.89% for the Federal Republic, 6.01% for France, 5.78% for Italy, 5.11% for Canada, 4.21% for the Netherlands, 4.99% for Sweden and 4.92% for Australia; they are heterogeneous long-rate series, not a matched 10-year panel. Japan's 9.79% historical long rate is not a 10-year government yield. Credit spreads, complete yield curves and a sovereign-default inventory are not covered by the sources cited. Sovereign debt stress existed in some developing economies, but official finance, IMF support, trade credit, and bank lending mattered more than liquid emerging-market bond trading.

### 6C. Currencies

Most major currencies were fixed or tightly managed. Sterling was $2.80, the yen ¥360 per dollar, the DM about 4 per dollar, the French new franc about 4.94 per dollar, the lira about 625 per dollar, the rupee about 4.76 per dollar, the Mexican peso about 12.50 per dollar, and the Spanish peseta about 60 per dollar. Non-market and multiple-rate systems are not comparable: Soviet ruble, Chinese yuan, Polish zloty, Brazilian cruzeiro, Argentine exchange arrangements, and Indonesian rupiah figures require caution.

Official parities and accessible rates must remain separate. Canada moved from a float to US$0.925 per Canadian dollar in May; most European and Japanese parities were unchanged. Brazil's controlled free market and parallel market diverged sharply (475 cruzeiros per dollar in September, above 800 in the parallel market in early December), Argentina's peso weakened from about 83 to 150 per dollar between March and November, and Japan's parallel-market quotation was 398.5 yen against the 360 parity. Australia's pound preceded decimalization and Mexico's old peso preceded later redenomination, so back-converted database units are not historical quotations.

### 6D. Alternative assets

Gold's official monetary price was $35 per ounce. The London Gold Pool defended that price in the private market. The London fixing approached $35.20 on 25 October amid heavy demand and intervention and was around $35.08 by late November. U.S. gold holdings ended 1962 at $16,057 million, down $890 million at the official valuation. Real estate was important but not globally comparable; housing demand was strong in the United States, Canada, Australia, Japan, Spain, Italy, France, Germany, and the Netherlands. Commodity exposure mattered through producers such as Venezuela, the USSR, Canada, Australia, Argentina, and Brazil.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The Cuban Missile Crisis was the year's largest tail-risk event. It did not create a sustained economic collapse, but it highlighted the economic weight of Cold War defense, science, communications, and industrial capacity. Canada's exchange-rate crisis was a concrete Bretton Woods stress event. The Kennedy Slide was a market-risk event rather than a recession signal. China was still recovering from the Great Leap Forward crisis; India absorbed the Sino-Indian War; Argentina's political crisis damaged confidence; Brazil and Indonesia showed rising macro instability.

With hindsight, the deepest vulnerability was Bretton Woods itself. Swap lines and the Gold Pool bought time but did not remove the contradiction between global dollar liquidity and gold convertibility. Another hindsight risk was oil: OPEC looked institutionally limited in 1962, but producer coordination was already forming.

Observed risks were concrete. Canada's reserve and capital-flow episode led to policy intervention; the May equity break showed valuation and market-functioning risk despite output expansion; the Cuban missile crisis produced a documented gold-market response; India's emergency defense financing altered public resource claims; and rapid city-price inflation and exchange instability in Brazil, Argentina and Indonesia threatened real incomes and access to imported inputs. China's post-famine output direction remains reconstruction-dependent, so calling 1962 a proven recovery would prejudge the evidence conflict.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1962 vs. 1961)

World GDP growth accelerated to about 4.7% in the Maddison series. The United States moved from recovery toward stronger expansion, though not full employment. Western Europe remained strong but moderated in some areas. Japan's high-growth model gained further scale. Spain surged after stabilization, while Argentina weakened and Poland contracted in the Maddison reconstruction.

In the MPD 2023 series, growth accelerated by 3.69 percentage points in the United States, 4.05 in Canada and 5.57 in Australia, while slowing by 3.10 points in Japan and 2.29 in Britain; Spain slowed by 2.12 points in this vintage. China's apparent 24.97-point improvement is confined to the Wu/MPD reconstruction. World population rose 1.91% (UN WPP 2024), and the limited-geography UN export volume index rose 5.65%.

Ranking changes were modest but meaningful. Venezuela entered the top 20 and Czechoslovakia exited. Japan moved ahead of France; France moved ahead of China; Italy moved ahead of India; the Netherlands moved ahead of Poland; Switzerland moved ahead of Czechoslovakia. Policy deltas were larger: the CAP began, the U.S. Trade Expansion Act passed, Canada fixed its currency, and central-bank swap lines became part of dollar defense.

Further policy shifts included Canada's emergency tightening and later cuts, British and Japanese rate reductions, the Dutch increase, European agricultural rules and Italian electricity nationalization. On the MPD 2023 cohort the top 20 is unchanged from 1961, with Italy and India, Mexico and Argentina, and the Netherlands and Poland exchanging places.

New risks included the Cuban Missile Crisis, equity-market fragility, worsening Brazilian and Argentine instability, and continuing Chinese recovery uncertainty. Resolved or eased risks included the immediate U.S. recession risk from 1960-61 and some postwar European payments constraints.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

**High confidence.** The United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Western Europe, Japan, Australia, and Switzerland have relatively reliable national accounts, central-bank records, exchange-rate parities, and market data.

**Medium confidence.** India, Mexico, Brazil, Argentina, Venezuela, and Spain have usable GDP series but weaker comparability for inflation, labor markets, fiscal balances, and informal activity.

**Low confidence.** The Soviet Union, China, Poland, and Indonesia require special caution. Plan prices, rationing, exchange controls, administrative allocation, informal activity, and crisis-period disruptions weaken direct comparison.

**Data convention.** The top-20 ranking uses the directly reproduced Maddison 2010 horizontal GDP level series in 1990 international Geary-Khamis dollars, cross-checked against Maddison Project Database 2023/Our World in Data methodology and WDI-style current-dollar national accounts where available. It is not a current-dollar market-exchange ranking. Soviet-bloc and non-market estimates are explicitly uncertain, with a rough plus or minus 20-30 percent uncertainty band for output levels.

**Conflicts.** China is the clearest example: Maddison 2010 gives 0.4% growth, MPD 2023 +6.82% and the NBS 2016 revision -5.6%, so the sources disagree even on the sign. Spain's growth is 11.5% in Maddison 2010 but 9.75% in MPD 2023. Italy's JST-derived current account (4.28% of GDP) is incompatible in magnitude with BIS's $130 million surplus and is withheld; Canada's JST current account has an unresolved CAD/USD lineage; Britain's revised Bank of England series implies a positive 1961 current account, while BIS reported a GBP 70 million deficit for 1961. The ranking's lower boundary is vintage-dependent: MPD 2023 places Czechoslovakia and Sweden, not Switzerland and Venezuela, at ranks 19 and 20, and does not certify the Maddison 2010 world GDP of 9.14 trillion 1990 GK$. Germany also has boundary complications because Maddison historical Germany, West Germany, and East Germany are not the same statistical object.

**Missing data.** Comparable 1962 values for unemployment, public debt, current accounts and fiscal balances are not available in the sources cited for the planned economies and most developing economies. City consumer-price indices (Buenos Aires, São Paulo, Djakarta, Mexico City, the Indian index excluding rent) and the Soviet state-retail index are shown in the notes, not as national CPI. Debt ratios are JST historical public-debt series (central government only for France) and fiscal balances are central-government or reconstructed historical measures. A verified global company ranking, a calendar IPO/M&A/failure inventory, household-debt or saving panels, a global patent table and annual property series for most major cities are not covered by the sources cited. JST Macrohistory values are used under the CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 license.
