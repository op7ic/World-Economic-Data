# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1911

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1911 sat near the high point of the first globalization. The classical gold-standard order was still functioning, London still intermediated a large share of world trade finance and cross-border capital, and industrial production was expanding in Germany, France, Japan, and parts of the British Empire. The year was not a single global crisis year, but it was a year in which several structural weaknesses became visible: fragile monetary systems without modern lenders of last resort, imperial fiscal stress, dependence on commodity and capital flows, and political instability in large agrarian economies.

The biggest structural event was China's 1911 Revolution. Economically, the revolution grew out of a fiscal, railway, and sovereignty crisis as much as a constitutional crisis. The Qing state's attempt to nationalize locally financed railway ventures and negotiate foreign financing, including the Hukuang railway loan contract signed on 20 May, helped trigger provincial opposition. China remained one of the world's largest economies in aggregate output, but its fiscal capacity and per-capita income were very low, and the collapse of imperial authority created a shock to taxation, debt expectations, and infrastructure planning.

In the industrial core, the United States remained the largest economy in the Maddison historical series. The Supreme Court decisions against Standard Oil on 15 May and American Tobacco on 29 May marked a major turn in the microeconomic governance of large corporations. Industrial expansion was uneven: in the comparative official production tables, U.S. crude steel output fell 9.27% and pig iron 12.81%, while German crude steel rose 9.17% and French crude steel 10.35%. Germany continued to deepen its heavy industrial, chemical, electrical, and banking strengths. Britain remained the center of global finance, shipping, and sterling credit, even as its relative industrial lead narrowed. Japan continued its Meiji-era industrial ascent. In September, the Moroccan crisis and seasonal funding demands coincided with higher European discount rates, including a Reichsbank increase from 4% to 5% on 19 September, and Italy declared war on the Ottoman Empire on 29 September.

The assessment uses historical GDP concepts rather than modern nominal GDP rankings. The principal numerical base is the Maddison Database 2010 in million 1990 international Geary-Khamis dollars, cross-checked against the Maddison Project Database 2023 and secondary Maddison ranking summaries. The two vintages differ materially for some economies: Maddison 2010 shows growth of 6.51% for Italy and 0.71% for Mexico, while the Maddison Project Database 2023 shows 2.02% and -4.08%. Exact 1911 annual values are not available for several large empires and non-market or weak-statistical economies. Where this happens, the table states "Data not available" or uses a clearly labeled 1913 benchmark proxy. The weakest parts of the ranking are China, Russia, Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire, and colonial or partitioned territories.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1911

**1. The classical gold-standard order still disciplined policy.** The monetary system was built around gold convertibility, fixed exchange parities, and credibility in external payments. This mattered because countries with gold-standard commitments could not freely pursue independent monetary policy without risking reserve losses. Britain, France, Germany, Japan, the United States, and many colonial or sterling-linked economies were inside or tied to this system. The gold standard helped stabilize exchange rates and encouraged cross-border capital flows, but it also transmitted shocks through gold flows, interest-rate changes, and reserve pressure. Annual dollar rates in the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database moved by less than 0.2% for the mark, the French franc, the guilder and sterling, while the peseta, outside gold convertibility, depreciated 1.26%.

In 1911, the system still appeared durable. Hindsight changes the interpretation: the gold standard's success depended on cooperation, confidence, and peace. The war beginning in 1914 showed how quickly convertibility and international capital markets could be suspended or redirected when geopolitical constraints changed.

**2. Industrial scale and corporate governance moved to the center of microeconomics.** The most visible corporate-governance development was the United States' antitrust action against Standard Oil and American Tobacco. These cases showed that the state could challenge dominant trusts even in a generally pro-business age. The economic significance was not only legal. Large-scale industrial organization, vertical integration, transport networks, pricing power, and market access were now central questions for policymakers. Both decisions ordered court-supervised restructuring rather than immediate liquidation.

The forward link is clear: twentieth-century capitalism would be shaped by the tension between scale efficiency and monopoly control. The 1911 trust decisions became reference points for later competition policy, while Germany's cartels, Britain's older industrial firms, and Japan's zaibatsu showed that countries handled industrial concentration differently. In the same year the Computing-Tabulating-Recording Company, the predecessor of IBM, was incorporated on 16 June.

**3. China revealed the economic fragility of late imperial state finance.** China's 1911 Revolution was not simply a political event. It exposed a fiscal state that had difficulty financing railways, servicing obligations, balancing provincial interests, and defending sovereignty over infrastructure concessions. The Railway Protection Movement is therefore a macro-micro bridge: local shareholders, provincial tax levies, foreign loans, railway concessions, and central government authority all collided. The Hukuang contract of 20 May committed foreign financing to railway lines, but its signature did not mean disbursed investment or completed track.

The forward link was the Republican era's fiscal fragmentation. What was knowable in 1911 was state breakdown; what became clearer later was that weak central fiscal capacity would limit national development and complicate monetary unification for decades.

**4. Commodity exporters prospered but remained exposed.** Argentina, Canada, Australia, Brazil, the Dutch East Indies, India, and many African and Latin American economies were tied to global demand for wheat, meat, wool, rubber, coffee, sugar, cotton, metals, and oil. The first globalization rewarded economies that could connect land, railways, ports, and foreign capital to European demand. In London, the Rio coffee quotation rose 38.10% in 1911, Java's reporting sugar factories raised output 16.79%, and Mexican crude-oil output more than tripled, while copper eased 1.75%.

The vulnerability was concentration. Commodity booms supported railways and urban growth, but price declines, capital-flow reversals, or war could cut export revenue and credit. This vulnerability became much more obvious after 1914 and again in the interwar period.

**5. Imperial and military competition began to crowd into economic life.** 1911 was not yet a world-war year, but it was deeply shaped by imperial rivalry. Italy declared war on the Ottoman Empire over Libya on 29 September 1911. The Franco-German Morocco-Congo agreements were signed on 4 November, with territorial transfers following in 1912-1913. Naval spending, arms industries, railway strategy, and colonial finance were already economically relevant. Germany and Britain competed in naval and industrial capacity, Russia expanded rail and military infrastructure, and Japan's imperial sphere widened after the annexation of Korea in 1910.

The forward link is 1914. Military-industrial capacity, fiscal space, gold reserves, railway networks, and access to capital markets would determine how states mobilized once war began.

**6. Labor, migration, and urbanization were changing domestic political economy.** Mass migration and urbanization were major economic forces. Canada, Argentina, Australia, and the United States absorbed immigrants; European economies experienced both emigration and internal migration; Japan moved labor from agriculture into textiles and industry; India and China remained largely agrarian but increasingly linked to railways, ports, and migrant labor circuits. Canada Year Book counts of immigrants by destination rose from 208,794 to 311,084 between the years labelled 1910 and 1911.

Labor movements and social legislation became more important. Britain's National Insurance Act of 1911 was a signal that industrial economies were building social-policy institutions to manage health, unemployment, and working-class insecurity; its ordinary commencement date was 15 July 1912, so its benefits were not 1911 household income. Japan's Factory Act was also passed in 1911 but was implemented only from 1 September 1916. This trend would accelerate in the twentieth century.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

**World GDP:** Data not available for 1911 on a consistent basis. The Maddison 2010 horizontal file gives a **1913 world total of about 2,733,190 million 1990 international Geary-Khamis dollars**, but that is a benchmark near 1911, not a 1911 value. The Maddison Project Database 2023 has 41 eligible country records for 1911, with no Russian Empire, Ottoman Empire, Austria-Hungary, Hungary, Poland or Czechoslovakia record and no 1910 value for China, so summing its records would not measure world output. The Maddison Project Database 2023 and the Our World in Data processing indicate that historical GDP estimates are reconstructed from GDP per capita and population series, adjusted for inflation and purchasing-power differences. Therefore, global aggregate values should be treated as reconstructed PPP estimates, not official national accounts.

**World growth rate:** Data not available. The available annual Maddison rows allow growth calculations for many countries, but not for all major economies or the world aggregate in 1911. A defensible global growth figure cannot be produced without interpolation.

**World population:** About 1,761.4 million in 1911 against 1,744.9 million in 1910 (+0.94%) in the January 2026 Federico-Tena reconstruction on 1991 borders. This is an author-published reconstruction with interpolated country series, not a census count.

**Global trade volume:** Data not available for a single exact 1911 world trade volume. Historical trade databases cover exports and imports from 1800 to 1938, and international trade and capital flows were near their pre-1914 peak, but the exact 1911 volume is not estimated.

**Reserve currency dynamics:** Sterling was the main reserve and trade-finance currency of the classical gold-standard era. The exact 1911 reserve-currency composition is Data not available. A near benchmark is that by 1913 sterling accounted for a very large share of known foreign-exchange reserves. Gold remained the central reserve asset, and countries defended convertibility through interest-rate and reserve policies.

**Global inflation trends:** Data not available as a comparable global CPI measure. The gold-standard environment limited long-run inflation in the core economies but did not prevent relative-price swings in commodities, harvest-dependent food prices, or country-specific price movements. **Key commodity prices:** Gold was fixed in the United States at $20.67 per troy ounce. **International capital flow direction:** Capital moved primarily from Britain, France, Germany, the Netherlands, and other European centers toward settler economies, railways, sovereign borrowers, colonies, and infrastructure. London was the key node. Macrohistory Database current-account reconstructions show surpluses of 9.24% of GDP for the United Kingdom, 1.58% for France and 1.26% for Germany, and deficits of 15.07% for Canada and 5.94% for the Netherlands. ### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The monetary context was the classical gold standard. Exchange rates among gold-standard currencies were fixed by gold parities and only moved within narrow gold points under normal conditions. This reduced exchange-rate risk but made monetary policy subordinate to reserve defense. Countries with weaker fiscal credibility or political instability faced higher borrowing costs and external pressure.

The United States had no central bank in 1911. The Panic of 1907 had already exposed the fragility of a decentralized banking system, and the National Monetary Commission's work was part of the institutional path toward the Federal Reserve Act of 1913. For the United Kingdom, the Bank of England Bank Rate was in the range of roughly 3.0-4.0% during 1911, with quarterly values around 3.5%, 3.0%, 3.33%, and 4.0%; the Bank of England millennium dataset gives an annual mean of 3.47% and a year-end rate of 4%. The Reichsbank raised its discount rate from 4% to 5% on 19 September, and the Bank of Japan raised its commercial-bill discount rate from 4.75% to 5.48% on 27 September. The Javasche Bank's bill discount rate in the Dutch East Indies stood at 3.5% at the end of both 1910 and 1911. Official rates for France, Italy, Spain, Belgium and the Netherlands are Data not available; the Macrohistory Database gives market or reconstructed short-term rates for them, which are reported in the country tables as such.

The September tightening was a liquidity episode rather than a banking collapse. The contemporary analysis of Raphaël-Georges Lévy linked it to withdrawals of short-term funds during the Moroccan crisis, across borders, as well as to ordinary quarter-end demand.

Stock-market evidence is uneven. The Dow Jones Industrial Average ended 1911 nearly unchanged at about 81.68. London remained the world's central securities market, but modern FTSE-style index data do not exist for 1911; the Campbell-Grossman-Turner reconstruction of all sampled common equities fell 2.45% between December 1910 and December 1911. ### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The main global trade regime was not a single multilateral institution but a network of gold-standard finance, imperial preferences, bilateral treaties, colonial tariffs, and national tariff systems. The United States remained tariff-protected; Canada debated reciprocity with the United States and rejected it politically in the 1911 election. European powers combined trade openness in some areas with strategic protection in others. The United States-Japan Treaty of Commerce and Navigation, signed on 21 February, came into operation on 17 July under its Article XVII; an accompanying declaration maintained Japan's existing restrictions on labor emigration to the United States.

Geopolitically, the Italo-Turkish War began in 1911, exposing the economic strain of imperial conflict. The Morocco-Congo agreements of 4 November settled the second Moroccan crisis, though the territorial transfers took place in stages in 1912-1913. China entered revolution. Mexico's revolution disrupted railways, mining, oil, and foreign-investment expectations. These were not yet systemic global financial crises, but they were warnings that political risk could interrupt the first globalization.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Energy remained coal-centered in most industrial economies. Oil was rising in importance, especially in the United States, the Caucasus, Mexico, the Dutch East Indies, and parts of the Middle East, but the world oil system was not yet governed by OPEC or modern posted-price institutions. Mexican crude-oil output rose from 3,634,080 to 12,552,798 barrels (+245.42%) in the official historical petroleum table, while British Indian and Burmese petroleum output rose 5.10% to 225.8 million gallons. Electricity, petroleum, and internal-combustion technologies were beginning to change industrial organization and transport, but coal and steam still dominated heavy industry and shipping. In the Belgian official comparative tables (metric tonnes, excluding lignite, several 1911 entries preliminary), coal output rose 2.72% in Great Britain and Ireland to 275.98 million tonnes and 5.18% in Germany to 160.75 million tonnes, while U.S. output fell 1.08% to 450.11 million tonnes.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

The world labor force remained overwhelmingly agricultural, but the leading economies were urbanizing. Industrial labor markets expanded in the United States, Germany, Britain, Japan, and parts of continental Europe. Immigration supported growth in the United States, Canada, Argentina, and Australia. Emigration from Italy, Eastern Europe, and other regions affected wages, remittances, and household strategies.

National historical estimates in the Macrohistory Database give 6.7% for the United States, 3.0% for the United Kingdom, 1.9% for Germany, 5.7% for France, 3.77% for the Netherlands and 2.9% for Australia; these are reconstructions from union, insurance and later estimates, not harmonized labor-force surveys, and cannot be averaged into a global rate. **Economic narrative:**

The United States was the largest economy in the available historical accounts and, in the Maddison annual series, expanded from 1910 to 1911, by 3.26% in the 2010 vintage and 2.63% in the 2023 vintage. The economy combined mass manufacturing, rail transport, steel, petroleum, meatpacking, farm exports, and an increasingly national consumer market. The absence of a central bank remained a central institutional weakness. After the Panic of 1907, the Aldrich-Vreeland framework and National Monetary Commission work were still feeding into the reform debate that produced the Federal Reserve System in 1913.

Output growth did not mean a uniform industrial boom. The Belgian official comparative tables record U.S. crude steel falling from 26.51 to 24.06 million metric tonnes (-9.27%), pig iron from 27.10 to 23.63 million tonnes (-12.81%) and coal by 1.08%, with the 1911 coal figure preliminary. The Macrohistory unemployment estimate rose from 5.9% to 6.7% and consumer prices were flat.

The dominant microeconomic event was trust-busting. The Supreme Court's Standard Oil decision of 15 May and American Tobacco decision of 29 May were not merely legal milestones; they reorganized large pools of capital, management, and industrial assets under court-supervised remedies. The breakup of Standard Oil left successor firms with valuable refineries, pipelines, brands, and technical capacity, so the ruling changed ownership structure more than the underlying demand for petroleum. Financial markets were calmer than in 1907: the Dow Jones Industrial Average ended 1911 almost flat, with a reported year-end close of 81.68 and annual change of about +0.39%.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** The Computing-Tabulating-Recording Company was incorporated on 16 June from International Time Recording, Computing Scale and the Tabulating Machine Company, the predecessor of IBM. Total bank loans in the Macrohistory Database rose 4.35%, while real consumption per person in the Barro-Ursúa reconstruction fell 2.00% and the Macrohistory nominal house-price index fell 1.05%. Newspaper asking-price indices rose 0.49% in New York City and 1.09% in Boston, and asking rents rose 9.44% and 2.27%. The nominal equity total return in the rate-of-return reconstruction was +5.86% after -7.57% in 1910, with a price gain of only 0.66%. Banking remained fragmented and seasonally strained, with no lender of last resort. Leading sectors were steel, petroleum, railroads, electrical equipment, meatpacking, farm machinery and consumer staples; catalog retail and automobile diffusion were visible but still early, and parts of rail finance faced regulation and debt pressure.

**Economic narrative:**

China was still one of the world's largest economies by aggregate output because of its enormous population, but its per-capita income was very low and its fiscal-financial institutions were under severe stress. The key economic-political shock of 1911 was the railway-finance crisis. Qing attempts to nationalize locally financed railway projects and support them through foreign loans triggered a broad protest movement, particularly in Sichuan, and helped set off the political collapse of the dynasty. The Hukuang railway loan contract with the foreign banking group was signed on 20 May after substantial revision; it committed financing for specified lines, not completed construction.

The Xinhai Revolution did not instantly transform China's production base, which remained overwhelmingly agrarian, but it disrupted tax administration, debt service expectations, provincial finance, and railway planning. Treaty ports, foreign banks, customs revenues, and provincial elites were all economically important. Hindsight note: what was visible in 1911 was a fiscal and political breakdown; the longer-run fragmentation of the Republican era was not yet fully knowable.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** In the Ma-de Jong reconstruction, real GDP per person fell from 37.58 taels at 1912 prices in 1910 to 36.10 in 1911 (-3.94%), a decline in average output rather than in total GDP. Grain agriculture, handicrafts, tea, silk, cotton textiles, inland commerce and treaty-port shipping dominated activity, while state finance, railway concessions and provincial credit were under stress. **Economic narrative:**

The Russian Empire was one of the largest aggregate economies, but its ranking is uncertain because historical data mix imperial boundaries, successor-state territories, and benchmark interpolation. In economic structure it was a land-rich empire with fast-growing industrial pockets around St. Petersburg, Moscow, the Donbas, Baku, Poland, and the Baltic region, set within a much larger agrarian economy. Grain exports, rail expansion, and foreign capital were central to its integration into global markets.

The post-1905 reform environment still mattered in 1911. ### 6C. Currencies

Gold-standard currencies traded near fixed parities. Sterling, the dollar, the mark, the franc, the yen, and other gold-linked currencies were constrained by convertibility and gold points: annual Macrohistory rates were 0.2057 pounds, 4.1979 marks, 5.1958 French francs, 5.18 Belgian francs and 2.4853 guilders per dollar, each within 0.2% of the 1910 rate, and the Bank of Japan's December quotation of USD 49.500 per 100 yen implies 2.0202 yen per dollar. The inconvertible peseta depreciated 1.26% to 5.61 per dollar. Silver and mixed-currency areas, including parts of China, were harder to summarize with one exchange rate. Colonial currency systems often linked local money to sterling or metropolitan finance; British India's Council bills realized 16.061d per rupee in FY 1910-11.

Devaluations and revaluations in 1911: no globally systemic devaluation event is identified. Capital controls in the modern sense were not central in gold-standard core economies, though many colonial and weak-state systems had administrative constraints and concessionary finance. ### 6D. Alternative assets

Gold was fixed in the U.S. at $20.67 per troy ounce, so no market return is implied; silver eased 0.49%. Real estate data for major cities in 1911 are Data not available in a comparable global form. Quality-adjusted newspaper asking-price indices compiled by the Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia rose 0.49% in New York City and 1.09% in Boston, while asking rents rose 9.44% and 2.27%; these are asking prices sampled mostly in May, not completed sales. Macrohistory nominal house-price indices rose 6.82% in Australia, 5.19% in the Netherlands, 3.14% in France and 2.25% in Germany, but fell 4.09% in Belgium, 2.78% in the United Kingdom and 1.05% in the United States. Land values mattered greatly in Argentina, Canada, Australia, the United States, and colonial plantation economies. Commodity-linked land and mining concessions were major alternative assets, but comparable price indices are not available.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

**China's 1911 Revolution:** This was the largest political-economic shock of the year. It disrupted central authority, railway finance, debt expectations, and provincial revenue systems. It was not yet clear in 1911 how long fragmentation would last.

**Mexican Revolution:** Mexico's political upheaval affected railways, oil, mining, land, and foreign investment. In 1911 the full economic cost was not yet visible, but the risk to property rights and infrastructure was already clear; the 2023 Maddison vintage shows a 4.08% fall in output even as oil output tripled.

**Italo-Turkish War:** The conflict over Libya, declared on 29 September, added fiscal and geopolitical pressure to Italy and the Ottoman Empire. It also signaled that imperial conflict could interrupt trade, debt, and regional investment.

**Moroccan crisis and September funding stress:** Diplomatic tension between France and Germany coincided with withdrawals of short-term funds and a Reichsbank discount-rate increase. The episode ended with the November Morocco-Congo agreements, but it showed how political risk could transmit through money markets.

**Gold-standard fragility:** Hindsight risk. In 1911 the gold standard looked stable; retrospectively, it was vulnerable to war, banking panics, sudden stops, and reserve loss. The absence of a U.S. central bank was a known vulnerability; the systemic fragility of the whole prewar order was less obvious to contemporaries.

**Commodity concentration:** Export economies such as Argentina, Canada, Brazil, Australia, and the Dutch East Indies benefited from global demand but were exposed to price swings and capital-flow reversals. This was a visible risk, though its full implications became clearer after the prewar globalization broke.

**Military-industrial buildup:** Defense spending and naval rivalry were not yet a macro crisis in 1911, but they were absorbing fiscal and industrial resources. With hindsight, the military-industrial base of 1911 was a precondition for the scale of mobilization after 1914.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1911 vs. 1910)

The most important delta from 1910 to 1911 was political rather than a synchronized global business-cycle break. China moved from late Qing reform crisis to revolution. Mexico moved deeper into revolutionary uncertainty. Italy moved from peace into colonial war. Canada moved from a trade-policy debate to rejection of U.S. reciprocity through election politics. France and Germany passed through the second Moroccan crisis to the November agreements.

In the Maddison 2010 annual series where exact data exist, several major economies grew strongly: the United States (3.26% after 1.02%), Germany, the United Kingdom, France (9.81% after -6.10%), Italy, Japan, Canada, Spain (7.17% after -3.50%), and the Dutch East Indies. India shows a small decline in the annual series, and growth slowed sharply in Argentina and Australia. The Maddison Project Database 2023 confirms most of these directions but gives much lower growth for Italy (2.02%) and a contraction for Mexico (-4.08%). Physical output diverged: crude steel fell 9.27% in the United States but rose 9-13% in Germany, France and Belgium. These growth rates are reconstructed historical estimates, not modern national-account releases.

The policy-regime delta was also institutional. The United States had not yet created the Federal Reserve, but the post-1907 reform process was advancing. Britain passed major social-insurance legislation, Japan a factory act and Australia a Commonwealth Bank act, each with later implementation dates. Corporate-governance policy in the United States shifted sharply through antitrust enforcement. Official discount rates rose in Germany and Japan in September, while Bank Rate ended the year at 4%.

In global power terms, the United States and Germany continued to rise relative to Britain in industrial output, while Britain retained financial centrality. Japan remained the most important non-Western industrializer. China's aggregate size remained immense, but the state crisis reduced its ability to mobilize resources effectively.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

**Most reliable:** Maddison annual GDP, population and per-capita rows for the United States, Germany, United Kingdom, India, France, Italy, Japan, Dutch East Indies/Indonesia, Spain, Canada, Belgium, Argentina, Mexico, Netherlands, Australia, and Brazil. The dated Bank of Japan and Reichsbank rate changes, the U.S. gold price, statutory and judicial dates, and the Dow Jones annual close are also relatively high confidence, as are official production, fiscal and banking returns where their territory and fiscal year are stated.

**Moderate confidence:** The top-20 ranking as a historical-economy ranking. It is directionally defensible, but exact positions depend on whether one ranks sovereign states, empires, colonies, or modern-territory reconstructions. Austria-Hungary, British India, the Dutch East Indies, and Ottoman/Turkey require explicit classification choices. Retrospective consumer-price, unemployment, debt, current-account, credit, house-price and equity-return reconstructions from the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database are moderate in quality; their perimeters and definitions differ by country.

**Low confidence or proxy-only:** China, Russia, Austria-Hungary, and the Ottoman Empire for exact 1911 GDP. For China and Russia, benchmark values around 1913 exist, but exact 1911 values in the Maddison 2010 annual table are not available; the Maddison Project Database 2023 has a Chinese 1911 level in 2011 international dollars but no 1910 level, so no growth rate can be derived. For Austria-Hungary, the empire aggregate comes from Schulze/Maddison cross-reference rather than a direct 1911 annual country row. For the Ottoman Empire, a modern Turkey proxy is not the same as the full empire.

**Historical data conflicts:** Secondary Maddison ranking summaries differ on Spain's 1913 value. Some tables show Spain around 45,686 million 1990 int-$; the Maddison 2010 workbook row used here shows 41,653 million for Spain in 1913 and 40,332 million for 1911. Austria-Hungary is excluded from some main Maddison top-ten tables but appears in notes or Schulze comparisons. Russia's 1913 figure also differs depending on whether a Russian Empire or former-USSR territorial proxy is used. The growth column follows Maddison 2010, consistent with the levels shown; the Maddison Project Database 2023 differs by more than a percentage point for Italy (2.02% against 6.51%), Mexico (-4.08% against 0.71%) and the Dutch East Indies (4.66% against 5.63%), and its Austria record covers only the later republic's territory. U.S. unemployment is 6.7% in the Macrohistory Database but 6.2% in Lebergott's 1957 estimate. For the United Kingdom, the Bank of England millennium dataset and the Macrohistory Database give different consumer-price inflation (0.28% against 0.10%), unemployment (4.73% against 3.0%), debt (31.23% against 31.61% of GDP) and current-account (8.84% against 9.24% of GDP) values; the Macrohistory values are shown, and the two sources are not blended. Japan's official commercial-bill discount rate (5.48% at year-end) differs from the Macrohistory short-term rate (6.86%). The Australian debt ratio falls 12.88 points in one year, a move that may reflect coverage rather than repayment. The 1911 balance of Dutch East Indies postal savings differs slightly between successive colonial reports, so no balance growth is shown. The United States-Japan commercial treaty took effect on 17 July under its Article XVII, although ratifications were exchanged on 4 April; the operative date is used. The 1910 report shows log-linear interpolated 1910 values for China and Russia (growth +0.78% and +3.21%); these interpolations are not annual observations, so no 1911 growth is derived from them.

**Unavailable or not comparable:** Budget balances relative to GDP are not shown for any economy because fiscal years and government coverage are not reconciled with calendar GDP. Official policy rates for France, Italy, Spain, Belgium and the Netherlands, and all macro indicators for China, Russia, Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire, Argentina, Mexico and Brazil, are Data not available; for British India only institutional rates, fiscal-year accounts and colonial debt stocks exist. World GDP, world growth, world trade volume, global inflation, reserve composition, corporate rankings, credit spreads and comparable real-estate data are also Data not available. Unemployment is especially problematic because definitions and registration systems differed widely. Data not available means that no verified observation with the stated definition and territory was found for 1911, not that no historical record exists.

**What does not exist in modern form:** A globally consistent 1911 national-accounts dataset equivalent to post-1990 World Bank/IMF data does not exist. Historical GDP is reconstructed from later national accounts, population series, commodity output, fiscal records, and scholarly estimates. For non-market, colonial, and weak-statistical economies, uncertainty can be large, and for some Soviet-bloc or developing-country histories the error bands can plausibly be 20-30% or more.
