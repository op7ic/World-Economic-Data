# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1958

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1958 was a hinge year between postwar reconstruction and the high-growth 1960s. The world economy began the year under the shadow of the 1957 to 1958 U.S. recession, falling industrial production, and weaker raw-material prices. By mid-year, U.S. output had turned upward, monetary policy in several large economies had shifted from restraint toward ease, and confidence returned to equity markets. The year was not one of spectacular global expansion, but it marked a transition from recession anxiety to renewed industrial growth.

The most important institutional change was the practical deepening of Bretton Woods. At the end of 1958, major Western European countries restored external current-account convertibility, ending much of the postwar dollar shortage regime. The European Economic Community also came into force on January 1, 1958. These two developments did not instantly create a modern global capital market, but they widened trade and payments channels and set up the 1960s expansion in European trade integration.

The distributional story was uneven. Industrial economies recovered or continued rebuilding, while many primary-producing countries were hurt by the decline in commodity prices that had begun after the mid-1950s boom. Exporters of minerals and agricultural products faced weaker terms of trade, tighter foreign-exchange positions, and slower public investment. This divergence between manufacturing centers and commodity exporters was one of the defining economic tensions of the year.

Two non-market economy events were historically large but statistically difficult: the USSR continued heavy-industrial and military-linked growth under central planning, and China launched the Great Leap Forward. China reported strong industrial mobilization in 1958, but the quality of output, agricultural disruption, and later famine make conventional GDP interpretation especially hazardous. The economic signal was not sustainable productivity growth; it was coercive mobilization and misallocation that would become catastrophic in subsequent years.

At the micro level, 1958 was a year of mechanisms that later became macro forces. The jet age entered commercial service, the integrated circuit was demonstrated, Sony adopted its global corporate name, Toyota began U.S. exports, and NASA was created. These events did not dominate 1958 GDP, but they foreshadowed future productivity, trade, consumer electronics, aerospace, and transport changes.

The defining cyclical event was the United States' recession and subsequent recovery, not a uniform world contraction. NBER dates the peak to August 1957 and the trough to April 1958. The annual average still captures depressed production: Federal Reserve industrial-production observations imply output fell 6.48%, while retrospective BEA real GDP fell 0.74%. The Maddison Project Database (MPD) 2023 reconstruction instead gives U.S. GDP growth of -1.00%. These are different series, not rounding variants. Monetary policy eased early and began tightening again later in the year.

Trade and raw-material markets transmitted the downturn unevenly. The UN export quantum index, which has limited coverage, fell from 131 to 128, about 2.29%; current-dollar exports fell from $98.7 billion to $94.0 billion. This is not complete world trade: the series excludes the USSR, mainland China, East Germany and specified eastern European economies. Copper recovered substantially from its February trough, while coffee continued falling. Country and commodity composition therefore matter more than a single narrative of falling prices.

The EEC began operating on 1 January. December external current-account convertibility and France's devaluation changed European payments arrangements, but neither describes the full-year regime. Convertibility principally concerned nonresident current balances; restrictions on capital and residents persisted. France's late-December move from 420 to 493.706 old francs per dollar raised the dollar quotation by 17.55% and lowered the franc's dollar value by 14.93%; the 17.55% figure measures the increase in francs per dollar, not the size of the fall in the franc's value.

China presents a major unresolved measurement conflict. The National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) retrospective constant-price series reports 21.3% GDP growth; MPD's Wu-derived territorial series implies only 0.60%. Neither permits contemporary Great Leap production announcements to be treated as usable output. The 1958 mobilization must also be distinguished from later famine outcomes, which are hindsight, not an observed full-year 1958 mortality estimate. Corporate mechanisms elsewhere are clearer: U.S. businesses rebuilt liquidity rather than fixed investment; Canadian mortgage approvals expanded; Mexican private construction weakened as funds shifted toward dollars and gold.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1958

**1. Recession to recovery in the U.S. and its global spillovers.** The U.S. recession that began in August 1957 ended in April 1958. Because the United States was the largest economy and a central source of import demand, its downturn affected industrial production, trade expectations, and commodity prices worldwide. The Federal Reserve responded with discount-rate cuts, lower reserve ratios, and open-market purchases before later moving away from emergency ease as recovery strengthened. Forward linkage: the rebound helped power the 1959 expansion, but it also reinforced the Bretton Woods tension between U.S. domestic policy and international gold-dollar confidence.

**2. Bretton Woods becomes operational in a fuller sense.** The restoration of current-account convertibility by major Western European economies in late 1958 was a major break from the postwar world of exchange controls and bilateral payments restrictions. The dollar remained anchored to gold at $35 per ounce, while other currencies operated through fixed parities and official intervention. Forward linkage: convertibility increased trade and payments flexibility, but it also exposed the system to the dollar-liquidity and gold-convertibility pressures that would grow in the 1960s.

**3. European integration moves from treaty to institution.** The Treaty of Rome entered into force on January 1, 1958, creating the EEC among France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg. The EEC did not immediately eliminate barriers, but it gave a legal and institutional path toward a customs union and common market. Forward linkage: this became a central driver of Western European trade deepening, scale economies, and intra-European industrial competition.

**4. Commodity exporters remain under pressure.** The IMF described continuing price declines for many primary products from 1956 into 1958. Countries dependent on commodity exports faced weaker export earnings and harder balance-of-payments choices. The impact was strongest in Latin America, parts of Africa, Southeast Asia, and raw-material exporters outside the industrial core. Forward linkage: weaker terms of trade increased reliance on import controls, development loans, and state-led industrialization.

**5. Central planning at scale, with divergent outcomes.** The USSR remained a large heavy-industrial economy, but its GDP and productivity statistics are not directly comparable with market economies. China launched the Great Leap Forward, emphasizing communes, steel targets, and mass mobilization. At the time, some output claims looked impressive; in hindsight, the campaign produced severe misallocation and agricultural disruption. Forward linkage: China would suffer severe economic and humanitarian consequences from 1959 to 1961.

**6. Technology foundations move from laboratory to economy.** Jack Kilby's integrated circuit demonstration, NASA's creation, commercial jet service, and Japanese consumer electronics branding were not yet macro-dominant. They did, however, mark the early formation of semiconductor, aerospace, jet transport, and global electronics sectors. Forward linkage: the 1960s and 1970s would convert these into productivity, military, consumer, and trade transformations.

**7. Recovery within a weak annual average.** The U.S. recession ended in spring, yet annual unemployment rose from 4.3% to 6.8%. The Federal Reserve's early easing and later reversal show why a single annual interest rate cannot summarize policy. The forward link is a recovery entering 1959, not uninterrupted 1958 growth.

**8. Payments integration without capital-account freedom.** EEC implementation and December convertibility reduced Western European payment frictions. The European Payments Union (EPU) was wound up and the European Monetary Agreement took over; Greece, Iceland and Turkey were exceptions among EPU members. These changes enabled later trade integration while leaving important controls intact.

**9. Different industrial and primary-product cycles.** Coffee oversupply pressed Latin American export earnings; copper moved upward after February. U.S. steel production contracted sharply while public infrastructure and development programs elsewhere continued. This divergence helps explain why some growing economies nevertheless faced reserve constraints.

**10. Finance did not translate mechanically into plant expenditure.** U.S. companies used long-term issuance to strengthen liquid assets and repay short-term debt; Canadian debt conversion lengthened maturities; Mexican dollar hoarding impaired domestic mortgage-security placement. Balance-sheet composition is the operative mechanism, not a universal return of confidence.

**11. State-led transformations require institution-specific measures.** China's mobilization, Indonesia's nationalization law and India's externally constrained investment plan differed fundamentally. Western unemployment, corporate market capitalization and sovereign yields cannot simply be inserted into every planned or controlled economy.

**12. Innovation was a pipeline, not immediate GDP.** Kilby's working integrated circuit, NASA's establishment, Sony's transistor-radio exports and transatlantic jet services mark dated capabilities and institutions. Their later importance is plausible historical linkage, not measured 1958 productivity.

**World GDP and growth.** Data not available as a single official 1958 world GDP figure. Maddison/PWT-style PPP series imply a world economy in the broad range of 10 to 12 trillion constant 2011 international dollars, an indicative range rather than a certified world total. Growth was uneven: the U.S. contracted in real terms for part of the year, Western Europe slowed but generally avoided a deep synchronized recession, Japan remained in high-growth transition, and many commodity exporters weakened.

**Global trade.** World export value had exceeded $100 billion in 1957, then fell in the first half of 1958 before recovering in the second half. The second-half volume recovery was strong enough that world export volume reached a new high in that part of the year, but the annual trade story was still marked by weak commodity prices and a mid-year industrial slowdown. On the UN's limited-coverage basis, export quantum fell from 131 to 128 (-2.29%) and unit values from 103 to 100 (-2.91%), both on 1953=100, while current-dollar exports fell from $98.7 billion to $94.0 billion (-4.76%). The UN's separate constant-price exports of 95.8 and 94.0 do not yield the identical quantum change; the table-vintage and rounding discrepancy is left unreconciled. BIS's preliminary imports-plus-exports turnover of 209.3 and 196.7 billion dollars implies -6.02%; imports are CIF, exports FOB, and its treatment of eastern-bloc trade differs. It is neither exports alone nor an independent confirmation of the same quantum series.

**Reserve currency dynamics.** The U.S. dollar was the anchor currency under Bretton Woods, convertible into gold for official holders at $35 per ounce. European external convertibility in December 1958 reduced the postwar dollar-gap regime and increased the practical role of dollar-based payments. U.S. gold holdings still represented a very large share of world monetary gold, but U.S. balance-of-payments deficits and gold outflows were becoming a structural concern. BIS records U.S. official gold holdings falling $2.275 billion to $20.582 billion, while U.K. holdings rose $1.250 billion to $2.850 billion and Italy's rose $634 million to $1.086 billion. These are end-year gold stocks valued in dollars, not a full currency composition of reserves or gross capital flows.

**Inflation trends.** Inflation pressure eased in many industrial countries after the 1956 to 1957 phase of restraint. The United States moved from inflation concern to recession response. France remained a special case, with inflation and currency stress leading to stabilization measures under the new Fifth Republic. In developing countries, inflation data are often not comparable or not available; the city cost-of-living indices shown in the country notes are not national CPIs.

**Key commodities.** Gold remained officially fixed at $35 per ounce, though London market tensions briefly lifted the price above the official level. The U.S. domestic crude first-purchase annual average was $3.01 per barrel in 1958, versus $3.09 in 1957, per the U.S. Energy Information Administration. U.S. copper producer prices were weak by late-1950s standards, with the annual average near 26 cents per pound in available U.S. data. Agricultural staple prices varied by crop and region; globally comparable annual data are not available. The most reliable general statement is that primary-product prices were under downward pressure, with copper rebounding within the year and coffee continuing to fall.

**Capital flows.** Capital tended to flow from the United States toward Europe through private investment, reconstruction-linked finance, and official channels. European reserve gains were significant in 1958 to 1959. Developing-country capital access remained restricted and often depended on official lending, export earnings, and import controls rather than open capital markets. Canadian long-term inflows nearly financed its current-account deficit; investment-income payments increasingly offset merchandise improvement.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The monetary setting shifted from anti-inflation restraint to recession response. In the United States, the Federal Reserve lowered discount rates, reduced reserve ratios, and supplied reserves through open-market operations. As recovery developed, the downward movement in U.S. rates reversed later in 1958. In West Germany, the Bundesbank eased from earlier restrictive settings, cutting its discount rate from 4% to 3.5% on 17 January and to 3% on 27 June. The United Kingdom reduced Bank Rate from the very tight 1957 level of 7% to 4% by November as the external position improved. Japan remained under a managed credit regime, with policy influenced by balance-of-payments discipline and industrial finance needs; the Bank of Japan cut its basic discount rate in June and September.

The exchange-rate context was fixed-parity Bretton Woods. France changed its exchange-rate and stabilization regime at year-end. Capital controls remained common even after current-account convertibility expanded.

Equity markets rebounded sharply after the U.S. recession trough. The S&P 500 and Dow Jones Industrial Average produced large positive annual returns in 1958 after negative returns in 1957. Bond markets moved in two phases: yields declined during recession and monetary ease, then rose as recovery expectations strengthened. ### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The most important trade development was European current-account convertibility and the EEC's launch. These changes supported trade liberalization, but quantitative restrictions, tariffs, and capital controls remained significant. GATT discussions focused on expanding trade and addressing the imbalance between industrial import demand and the export earnings of primary-producing countries.

The EEC's effective date was 1 January 1958, not the signing of the Treaty of Rome in 1957 or completion of the common market. The December payments change reduced restrictions on external current transactions, not all trade barriers. U.S. zinc and lead quotas introduced on 22 September limited imports to 80% of average 1953–1957 imports. Mandatory U.S. petroleum quotas, by contrast, date to 10 March 1959 and belong to the following year.

Cold War geopolitics affected economic flows in several ways. The Soviet bloc traded largely within administered arrangements and Council for Mutual Economic Assistance channels. Western export controls limited strategic trade with communist countries. U.S. and Soviet space competition increased public R&D and aerospace spending. The geopolitical economy of 1958 was not free trade globalization; it was a partially liberalizing Western system alongside separated socialist trading systems and restricted developing-country finance. India's August financing meeting linked external support to completion of infrastructure and industrial projects. Indonesia's nationalization act altered ownership claims over Dutch enterprises, with compensation provisions rather than a simple statement that all assets were uncompensated confiscations.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil was still a low-price, high-growth energy source. OPEC did not yet exist, and pricing power rested largely with major international oil companies and producing-country concessions. The Middle East, Venezuela, and the United States were central to supply. The year had no 1970s-style oil shock; energy was a growth enabler, not a macro crisis. BIS's source-defined petroleum total, including shale oil and natural gasoline, rose from 901 to 924 million metric tons (+2.55%). U.S. output fell from 353 to 328, while its "Arabian countries" grouping rose from 178 to 215. **U.S. oil-price comparator.** The U.S. Energy Information Administration's domestic crude first-purchase annual average was $3.01 per barrel in 1958, versus $3.09 in 1957, a -2.589% change. These nominal national annual averages are not an international grade quotation, December close, freely traded world spot price or investor holding-period return, and overlapping Census/EIA lineage does not provide independent corroboration of other U.S. price series.

Metals were affected by industrial-cycle weakness. Copper prices softened, and many raw-material exporters faced terms-of-trade pressure. Steel demand was hit by the U.S. recession, but Japan, West Germany, Italy, the USSR, and China all pushed industrial output in different institutional forms. BIS's London copper quotation reached £160 per long ton on 28 February, almost £260 in November and about £220 at year-end; these dated prices imply a substantial intrayear rebound, not an annual-average return. Santos No. 4 coffee in New York fell from approximately 56 U.S. cents per pound at year-start to below 42 at year-end. The coffee agreement effective 1 October involved fifteen Latin American producers, with Brazil's export cut set at 40%; export restraint addressed oversupply rather than creating new demand.

Agriculture was regionally uneven. India was building its Second Five Year Plan around industrialization but still had a vast agricultural employment base. China reorganized agriculture through communes, a decision that would become disastrous. Latin American countries used import substitution and public investment to reduce vulnerability to imported manufactured goods, but food and rural productivity constraints persisted. U.S. No. 2 Hard Winter wheat in New York ranged from $2.275 to $2.4788 per bushel on 4 July and 26 September. BIS's 265 million-ton world wheat figure includes China's contemporary 39.5 million-ton claim versus 23.7 million in 1957; the printed estimate is a contemporary claim, and the harvest itself is not independently confirmed. The 1959 wheat agreement is not a 1958 development.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor-market data are not internationally comparable. The U.S. recession raised unemployment, to a 6.8% annual average that captures labor-market damage beyond the April output trough; many Western European countries still had relatively tight labor markets and continued rural-to-urban movement. Japan's labor force shifted from agriculture toward manufacturing and urban services. In China, the Great Leap Forward moved labor into communes, public works, and small-scale steel production, distorting normal productivity measurement. Italy, Spain and other countries' historical unemployment series have different coverage and denominators; planned-economy labor allocation is not a basis for assuming zero unemployment. Mexican railway disruption, including strikes and August–September floods, reduced transport activity, while falling private construction constrained employment opportunities.

Demographically, the postwar baby boom was still affecting schools, housing, and future labor supply in North America, Western Europe, Australia, and parts of the Soviet bloc. Urbanization remained a structural trend across Japan, Latin America, the Soviet Union, and parts of Asia. Migration was economically significant in Western Europe, where labor shortages encouraged internal and cross-border movement, though detailed annual data for 1958 are incomplete. **Economic narrative:**

The dominant story was the recession and rebound. Durable goods, automobiles, steel, and industrial production weakened sharply in late 1957 and early 1958. Monetary policy turned expansionary. By mid-year, output and confidence improved, and financial markets anticipated recovery.

Micro conditions were cyclical but not structurally weak. The auto sector was hit by recession. Housing and consumer durables were sensitive to credit easing. The federal government's science and aerospace role increased after Sputnik, with NASA created in 1958.

The recession reduced private fixed investment to $59.1 billion, down 8.2%; BIS attributed the $5.3 billion decline entirely to producers' durable equipment, with annual construction broadly unchanged. BEA data give current-dollar GDP of $481.229 billion and real growth of -0.74%, rather than MPD's -1.00%. CPI-U annual inflation was 2.8%, versus 1.8% December-to-December; the industrial-production annual mean of 20.326 (2017=100) fell 6.48%.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Automobiles and steel contracted early, equities rebounded strongly, aerospace and electronics gained policy attention, and the integrated circuit demonstration created a long-run foundation for semiconductors. Household borrowing increased $10.7 billion, almost entirely residential mortgages, rather than a broad automobile-credit boom. Corporate internal saving of $27.5 billion and new securities of $10 billion financed assets and $4 billion of net short-term debt repayment. Banks expanded securities portfolios, which does not by itself show improving loan quality. Philadelphia Fed asking-price indices for four cities appear in section 6D. ### 5C. Innovation & technology

The integrated circuit was demonstrated in 1958 at Texas Instruments, creating the foundation for modern semiconductors; Texas Instruments dates Kilby's working circuit to 12 September. NASA was created by law in July (29 July) and opened in October (1 October), consolidating U.S. civilian space activity after Sputnik. Commercial jet travel crossed an important threshold with transatlantic jet services in 1958, with the first scheduled Comet 4 transatlantic services on 4 October. These were not yet mass-market technologies, but they had large future productivity and industrial-organization effects.

Sony exported its smaller TR-610 before Japanese domestic sales; Toyota's headlight adaptation and inadequate highway performance show the cost of learning a new market. These mechanisms support product-development analysis but not 1958 R&D productivity coefficients.

R&D spending was strongly shaped by the Cold War, aerospace, electronics, and public procurement. ### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Sectoral employment continued to move from agriculture into manufacturing and services in Japan, Western Europe, Latin America, and the Soviet bloc. In the U.S., the cyclical unemployment shock was more visible because labor-market statistics were better; unemployment rose 2.5 percentage points, showing how annual labor-market harm can coexist with spring recovery. In developing economies, underemployment mattered more than open unemployment.

Mexico's railway strike and flood mechanism is documented, but not all transport decline is attributed to unions: rail passenger traffic fell 9% and freight 1%, while aviation passenger kilometers rose 12%. Banco de México inferred road growth from gasoline use rather than a direct traffic census.

Education and workforce upgrading were central but uneven. Japan and Western Europe benefited from rising technical skill and mass education. The USSR emphasized engineering and scientific training. India expanded technical and higher education within a planned development framework. China mobilized labor at scale but often away from productive agriculture and into low-quality industrial campaigns.

Union and labor movements remained important in Europe, the United States, Latin America, and Australia. Wage data are not available in comparable form, but the broad pattern was rising real wages in successful industrial economies and pressure on real incomes where inflation, shortages, or commodity weakness dominated. ### 6C. Currencies

The Bretton Woods fixed-rate system dominated. The dollar was pegged to gold, most major currencies maintained official parities, and capital controls remained common. The major 1958 currency event was Western European current-account convertibility at year-end. France's year-end stabilization and parity adjustment were also important.

France's move from 420 to 493.706 old francs per USD gives (493.706/420−1)×100 = 17.549% on the dollar quotation, while (420/493.706−1)×100 = −14.929% on the franc's value. The IMF describes agreement on 27 December; BIS records the declaration on 29 December. These dated steps are kept distinct rather than declaring one source wrong. The new-franc redenomination was 1960, not 1958.

Multiple exchange-rate systems were common in Latin America and parts of Asia. Therefore exchange-rate tables for Brazil, Argentina, Indonesia, and other controlled regimes are not comparable to simple market quotations. JST's French 4.95 normalized-franc quotation is a parallel-market series through 1958; Japan 391 and Spain 59.6 are also parallel quotations, and Sweden's 5.17 has parallel-market lineage. Canada, Italy, the Netherlands and Australia use end-period IFS series in these years, and the Bank of England's $2.81 per pound is an annual average. Germany's method note identifies IFS but does not establish timing. No uniform annual-average exchange-rate column is imposed. December convertibility was a late-year external-current-account change; Soviet-bloc official exchange schedules, developing-country multiple rates, capital controls and reserve-currency shares remain separate questions, and no reserve-composition percentage is reconstructed from gold stocks.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

**Active crisis: U.S. recession.** The U.S. recession was the most important cyclical risk. It ended in April 1958, but early-year industrial weakness affected global sentiment, trade, and commodity prices. The April trough did not erase unemployment or equipment-investment weakness. Recovery also exposed duration risk: borrowers positioned for continuing low rates experienced falling bond prices as yields rose. The Canadian conversion reduced rollover pressure by changing maturities, but did not remove interest-rate risk.

**Active risk: commodity-price deterioration.** Primary producers faced lower prices and weaker export earnings. This was not a single dramatic crash, but it created balance-of-payments pressure and constrained development budgets. India's financing gap and Latin American commodity exposure were active external vulnerabilities. Mexico shows a portfolio channel: dollar and gold preference constrained domestic financing even as total bank credit grew.

**Active risk: French stabilization and currency pressure.** France faced inflation, fiscal and external pressure, and political transition. The year-end stabilization helped restore credibility, but the risk was real during the year. French stabilization combined currency and fiscal measures, with real purchasing-power effects that cannot be summarized as a painless restoration of confidence.

**Active risk: China Great Leap Forward.** At the time, outsiders had limited reliable information. Hindsight shows that the campaign created severe economic distortions and food-system vulnerability. This is a hindsight-labeled risk because the full scale of the disaster was not openly knowable in 1958. China's production claims warrant explicit skepticism about physical quality and statistical incentives; later mortality is not counted as 1958 observed output or population loss, and the conflicting NBS and Wu reconstructions remain unresolved rather than being chosen to fit that later narrative.

**Systemic vulnerability: Bretton Woods dollar-gold tension.** Convertibility deepened the system, but it also made the U.S. balance-of-payments position more important. The U.S. could supply world liquidity by running deficits, but persistent deficits created gold-convertibility doubts. This contradiction would become clearer in the 1960s.

**Near-miss: synchronized industrial slump.** A deeper U.S. recession could have pulled Western Europe and Japan into a much more severe downturn. Policy easing, U.S. recovery, and European reserve strength prevented that outcome.

**Banking.** No 1958 global systemic banking crisis is identified; the absence of an identified crisis event does not show that every bank was sound. Bank losses, runs outside the covered economies and corporate failure counts are Data not available.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1958 vs. 1957)

The shift from 1957 to 1958 was from inflation restraint and expansion to recession and recovery. In 1957, policymakers worried about overheating and external strain. In early 1958, recession and falling industrial output dominated. By late 1958, recovery and convertibility had become the larger story. U.S. unemployment rose from 4.3% to 6.8%; the annual economy weakened despite recovery after April, and private equipment investment, not all construction, explained the fixed-investment fall.

The international payments regime changed materially. In 1957, Europe was still closer to the postwar controlled-payments system. By the end of 1958, major European currencies had restored external convertibility for current transactions, making Bretton Woods more operational. December convertibility, France's new parity and Indonesia's 31 December law were dated regime events, not conditions prevailing for all twelve months; Argentina's exchange reform and mandatory U.S. oil quotas belong to 1959, and new francs to 1960.

Europe also moved institutionally. The Treaty of Rome was signed in 1957 but entered into force in 1958. The EEC's practical start turned a treaty commitment into an economic framework.

China changed dramatically from the first Five Year Plan approach to the Great Leap Forward. This was a structural break in policy, not merely an acceleration. It shifted resources into mass mobilization and created the conditions for later collapse. China's reconstructed 0.60% growth contrasts with the NBS 21.3%, so movements in its ranking are not precise measures of a historical output change. In the MPD point selection, Czechoslovakia enters and Belgium drops to 21st, and the broader MPD Germany record narrowly moves ahead of China; the ranking ranges used here are unchanged by these conditional moves.

Trade and policy rates moved together with the cycle. Export quantum on the UN's limited-coverage basis fell from 131 to 128, and current exports from $98.7 billion to $94.0 billion, with explicit excluded territories; these differ from BIS's imports-plus-exports turnover. UK Bank Rate fell from 7% to 4%, the West German discount rate from 4% to 3% and Japan's from 8.395% to 7.30%. U.S. easing partially reversed, leaving the New York discount rate at 2.5% versus 3% entering the year. Canada's current-account deficit narrowed from C$1.400 billion to 1.112 billion while institutional housing-mortgage approvals rose from 752 to 1199 million; Mexico's private construction contraction shows that housing finance did not move uniformly.

In financial markets, 1957 had been a negative year for U.S. equities. 1958 became a recovery-rally year. Bond yields moved from recession lows toward recovery expectations by year-end.

In technology and corporate history, 1958 had more durable inflection points than its macro data alone suggest: integrated circuits, NASA, commercial jet services, Sony's global name, and Toyota's first U.S. export push.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

**High-confidence claims.** The U.S. recession dates, the existence and timing of Federal Reserve easing actions, the EEC's entry into force, Western European current-account convertibility at the end of 1958, the Bretton Woods gold-dollar framework, NASA's creation, and the integrated-circuit demonstration are well supported.

**Medium-confidence claims.** GDP rankings among the top 10 are broadly consistent within Maddison-lineage series, but exact order among Japan, India, and France can vary by measure, and no independent second ranking has been constructed. Western European country narratives are reasonably reliable, though annual 1958 values for inflation, unemployment, current accounts, and fiscal balances are not always available on matched definitions. UN price and trade tables, dated BIS official rates (which print 3 May for Sweden's cut against the Riksbank's 8 May), the Federal Reserve, Bank of Canada and Banco de México annual-report mechanisms, the Paris equity endpoints (dated in early January rather than at calendar year-end) and the eight city housing samples (newspaper asking prices and repeat-sales estimates) are strong for what they measure; arithmetic precision does not remove reconstruction uncertainty or sample limitations. Volkswagen's workforce-date and profit inconsistencies are unresolved.

**Low-confidence claims.** Soviet, Chinese, Polish, Indonesian, and some Latin American GDP and sector metrics are estimates with large uncertainty. Planned-economy prices, administered exchange rates, rationing, and non-market allocation make direct comparison difficult. For China in 1958, official output claims are especially unreliable because Great Leap incentives encouraged misreporting.

**Ranking ranges versus MPD point values.** The GDP ranges in section 4 are rounded reconstructions; several MPD 2023 point values lie outside them: the United States (2,963.55 billion), Canada (237.54 billion) and Australia (131.26 billion) fall below their ranges, while Argentina (181.17 billion), Mexico (162.67 billion), the Netherlands (133.41 billion) and Indonesia (142.31 billion) lie above. The MPD order also differs: France ranks ahead of Japan and India, Argentina and Mexico ahead of Spain, Indonesia 15th, Australia 18th, Czechoslovakia 19th and Belgium 21st, and MPD's Germany record (767.71 billion) covers a broader territory than West Germany. These differences are stated rather than reconciled. On units, the ranges and the MPD 2023 point values are both in constant 2011 international dollars, while the growth figures carry their own vintages and concepts (MPD 2023 GDP, contemporary BIS real GNP, retrospective BEA, Bank of England and Banco de México series), and nominal figures such as BEA current-dollar GDP or national-currency budgets are current prices, not PPP volumes.

**Explicit conflicts.** NBS versus Wu/MPD for China; UK Bank of England GDP versus MPD and contemporary GNP; Canadian JST current-account units; the January dates of the Paris equity endpoints versus calendar-year labels; the UN UK equity rebase; historical unemployment denominators; Zurich rent-series discrepancies; and contemporaneous Chinese harvest claims. No source mirror or common IMF or national lineage is counted as independent corroboration.

**Metrics with major gaps.** Comparable unemployment rates, government debt/GDP, current-account balances as percent of GDP, budget balances and policy rates for planned and developing economies; nominal GDP for every economy; fiscal-year matching; 10-year bond yields and spreads outside the United States; exchange-specific stock index returns and sector rotation; real estate prices for most major cities; household debt; corporate revenue outside the United States; global R&D and patents; healthcare and telecom output; and sectoral employment, migration and skill-wage panels are incomplete or not available in consistent annual form.

**Weakest areas.** The lower end of the top-20 ranking is the least certain. Spain, Argentina, Mexico, Poland, Netherlands, Belgium, Sweden, Indonesia, Czechoslovakia, Turkey, South Africa, and Yugoslavia can shift depending on PPP benchmark, territorial definition, and data source. The ranking is a defensible working list, not an exact official one.

**Important data that simply does not exist or is not comparable.** A single official 1958 world GDP, a global corporate revenue ranking including state enterprises, a uniform global unemployment table, and market exchange rates for nonconvertible or multiple-rate economies are not available. For these cases, the correct treatment is explicit uncertainty rather than false precision.
