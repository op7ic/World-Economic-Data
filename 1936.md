# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1936

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1936 was a late-Depression recovery year, but it was not a return to the pre-1929 world economy. Output expanded strongly in many major economies, asset markets recovered, commodity prices strengthened, and unemployment fell from crisis highs. Yet the recovery was politically and institutionally unstable. The old gold-standard order broke further, the remaining gold bloc retreated, exchange controls and capital controls remained widespread, and state direction of industry became more important in Germany, Japan, the Soviet Union, Italy, France, China, Brazil, and Mexico.

Using the Maddison Project Database 2023 method, with GDP estimated from real GDP per capita times population in constant 2011 international dollars, the largest 1936 economies were the United States, Former USSR, China, Germany, United Kingdom, India, France, Japan, Italy, and Indonesia. The MPD country-coverage sum for economies with available 1936 observations is about 5.98 trillion 2011 international dollars, implying roughly 6.3 percent growth from the comparable 1935 country-coverage total. This is not a literal fully measured world GDP total, because country coverage and historical reconstruction are incomplete. It is best read as a consistent historical PPP estimate across available country series.

The year's biggest monetary event was the September 1936 Tripartite Agreement among the United States, United Kingdom, and France after France left the gold standard. This reduced the immediate threat of competitive devaluation among the main democratic financial powers, but it did not rebuild a fully open international monetary system. The dollar and sterling had already left old gold-standard parities, and the franc's devaluation confirmed that internal recovery had become more important than defending pre-Depression exchange rates.

The year's biggest real-economy shifts were rearmament and state-directed investment. Germany's Four Year Plan, Japan's move after the February 26 Incident, Soviet defense-industrial planning, Italy's Ethiopian-war aftershocks, and French and British rearmament all redirected resources toward heavy industry, transport, chemicals, aviation, and military supply. Spain's civil war was the clearest shock: MPD estimates suggest a very large GDP collapse in 1936, and the country's financial and industrial system split into rival wartime economies.

The microeconomic pattern was uneven. Housing, automobiles, consumer durables, and construction helped the United States and United Kingdom; heavy industry and defense supply drove Germany, Japan, the USSR, and Czechoslovakia; commodity rebound helped Canada, Australia, Brazil, Indonesia, and parts of Latin America; labor reforms transformed France; and monetary reform reshaped China. 1936 therefore sits between two eras: the Depression recovery of the mid-1930s and the militarized, controlled, and conflict-driven economies of the late 1930s and 1940s.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1936

**1. Recovery without normalization.** The world economy was growing again, and several major economies posted strong real GDP growth. The United States, Germany, Japan, Brazil, Czechoslovakia, Mexico, and the Netherlands grew rapidly by MPD estimates. However, employment, trade, and finance did not normalize. Unemployment remained high in the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Netherlands, and Australia by the standards of their own later recoveries. World trade was recovering but remained fragmented by tariffs, quotas, exchange controls, clearing agreements, and imperial preferences. The forward linkage was the 1937-1938 setback in several economies and the increasing importance of state-led demand.

**2. Final collapse of the interwar gold-bloc constraint.** France's September devaluation and the Tripartite Agreement shifted the international monetary regime. The United Kingdom had left gold in 1931, the United States had devalued in 1933-1934, and France's move ended the main democratic defense of old gold parity. Countries most affected included France, the Netherlands, Switzerland, and gold-linked financial centers. The significance was not merely exchange-rate technicality: it was a policy-regime shift from external parity first to domestic recovery first.

**3. Rearmament as macroeconomic stimulus and microeconomic allocator.** Germany, Japan, the USSR, Italy, the United Kingdom, France, and Czechoslovakia all saw defense demand become more important. In Germany, hidden finance and exchange controls directed resources toward rearmament. In Japan, Takahashi's assassination weakened fiscal restraint. In the USSR, the Second Five-Year Plan increased the economic significance of defense industry. The forward linkage was a late-1930s investment boom in heavy industry, aircraft, chemicals, metals, and transport equipment, but also a squeeze on consumer goods and external balances.

**4. Managed trade, protection, and bilateralism.** International commerce did not recover through a simple liberalization wave. The United States pursued reciprocal trade agreements, the British Empire maintained imperial preference, Germany relied on clearing agreements and foreign-exchange allocation, Italy faced sanctions and autarkic policy after Ethiopia, and colonial economies depended on metropolitan trade channels. This mattered because micro-level firms did not face a single global market. They operated in segmented currency and quota zones.

**5. Commodity rebound and primary-producer relief.** Grain prices, crude oil prices, and many raw materials improved from early-1930s lows. This helped Canada, Australia, Brazil, Argentina, Indonesia, Mexico, and parts of the United States. The effect was not uniform: producers with debt burdens or drought exposure remained vulnerable, and price recovery did not restore pre-1929 agricultural prosperity everywhere. The forward linkage was continued sensitivity to global demand and to state price-support policies.

**6. Labor-market regime shifts.** 1936 was a landmark year for labor institutions. France's Matignon Agreements, paid holidays, collective bargaining, and the 40-hour week changed employer-worker relations. In the United States, New Deal labor institutions and industrial unionism gained practical importance. In Germany, Italy, Japan, and the USSR, labor organization was increasingly subordinated to state, party, or military goals. The same global recovery therefore produced very different labor-market regimes: democratic bargaining in some economies, coercive mobilization in others.

**7. Asia's monetary and industrial transition.** China's 1935 fabi reform carried into 1936 as a major attempt at monetary centralization after the silver crisis. Japan's industrial structure shifted further toward heavy and chemical industry. India remained a large colonial agrarian economy with important textile, jute, steel, and railway sectors. Indonesia remained a colonial export platform. The forward linkage was decisive: the 1937 Sino-Japanese War would transform East Asian fiscal, monetary, and industrial structures.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

**World GDP and growth.** The MPD country-coverage total for available 1936 observations is approximately 5.98 trillion constant 2011 international dollars, and the 55 economies with both 1935 and 1936 observations rose from 5,630.8 to 5,962.4 billion, or 5.89 percent. This is a reconstructed PPP estimate, not a contemporary current-dollar national-account total. It excludes countries without 1936 observations and uses later historical estimates for several economies.

**Global trade volume.** The Federico-Tena world export-quantum index (1913=100) rose 4.95 percent, from 105.42 to 110.64, and the League of Nations trade quantum (1929=100) rose from 82 to 85.5, or 4.27 percent, on a provisional, restricted sample. The Federico-Tena-Junguito historical trade dataset provides import and export series at current and constant 1913 prices for many polities through 1938, and the League of Nations World Economic Survey is the key contemporary cross-country source. The qualitative pattern is clear: trade recovered from the trough but remained below a fully integrated pre-Depression system because of tariffs, quotas, exchange controls, clearing agreements, and currency blocs.

**Reserve currency dynamics.** The dollar and sterling were the leading reserve and settlement currencies. The dollar was tied to the US Treasury gold price of 35 dollars per ounce, while sterling operated in a managed post-gold-standard regime. The franc ceased to be a hard gold-bloc anchor after September 1936. Germany, Italy, the USSR, China, and several emerging economies used exchange controls or administratively managed systems, which limited free reserve-currency convertibility.

**Global inflation trends.** Inflation was generally mild in the United States and United Kingdom, stronger in France and Italy, and distorted in controlled economies. France combined devaluation, wage gains, and fiscal stress. Germany's measured CPI was low, but price controls hid rearmament pressure. The Netherlands still experienced deflation before its late gold exit.

**Key commodities.** Gold was officially priced at 35 dollars per ounce in the United States. The EIA historical series reports the US crude-oil first purchase price at about 1.09 dollars per barrel for 1936. US grain prices rose strongly by contemporary Survey of Current Business reporting, with the BLS grain index about 7 percent higher than in 1935. Copper and some other metals improved with industrial recovery, and selected sterling quotations appear in section 3D; copper rose 15.21 percent and English wheat 38.72 percent.

**International capital flows.** Capital moved toward perceived monetary safety, especially the United States and sterling assets, until the Tripartite stabilization reduced some immediate currency-war risk. France and other gold-bloc economies faced capital flight before devaluation. Germany and Italy relied heavily on controls, clearing agreements, and administrative allocation rather than free international capital markets.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The monetary environment was unusually easy in the Anglo-American economies. US short-term rates were very low, and gold inflows expanded monetary conditions. The United Kingdom held Bank Rate at 2 percent, a historic low policy stance, while cheap money supported housing, public debt service, and investment. Germany's financial conditions were controlled rather than conventionally easy: official interest rates and CPI understated the true pressure created by rearmament and foreign-exchange scarcity. Japan's conditions moved toward fiscal dominance after the February 1936 assassination of Takahashi Korekiyo.

There was no Bretton Woods system in 1936. The world was in an interwar mixture of managed currencies, gold remnants, sterling-area arrangements, exchange controls, and bilateral clearing systems. The Tripartite Agreement reduced open competitive depreciation among the United States, United Kingdom, and France, but did not create a rules-based postwar-style system.

Official discount rates at the end of 1936 were 1.5 percent in New York, 2 percent in London, 4 percent in Germany, 2 percent in France after 6 percent at the start of the year, 4.5 percent in Italy, 3.29 percent in Japan, 2 percent in the Netherlands and Belgium, 3 percent in Czechoslovakia and at the Reserve Bank of India, 5 percent in Poland and Spain, 4 percent at the Java Bank and 3.5 percent at the Central Bank of the Argentine Republic. The Federal Reserve raised required reserve ratios by 50 percent effective after the close of 15 August, and the Treasury began sterilizing new gold after 23 December. The Federal Reserve estimate of about $3.1 billion of capital movement toward the United States covers 1934 to 1936 cumulatively.

Equity markets were strongest where recovery expectations were highest. The Dow Jones Industrial Average closed 1936 with a 24.82 percent annual gain, while a total-return S&P series reports a 33.92 percent gain. Several European markets also recovered in local terms, and reconstructed national returns appear in section 6A. Government bond yields remained low in the United States and United Kingdom, while Italy and some continental economies had higher yields reflecting fiscal, currency, or political risk.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

1936 had no EEC, ASEAN, WTO, or Bretton Woods institutions. Trade policy was fragmented. The United States continued reciprocal trade-agreement liberalization within a still-protectionist world. The British Empire operated with imperial preference. Germany used exchange controls and bilateral clearing to prioritize raw materials and rearmament. Italy's Ethiopian war and sanctions strengthened autarkic policy. Spain's civil war disrupted Iberian trade and foreign exchange. Poland restricted foreign payments from April and later suspended debt transfers, Brazil kept multiple exchange channels, and the Australian-Japanese commercial dispute was settled on 27 December.

Geopolitically, the world economy was becoming militarized. Germany remilitarized the Rhineland in 1936 and launched the Four Year Plan. Japan's military influence increased after the February 26 Incident. The USSR was planning under growing security concerns. China faced Japanese pressure and was trying to centralize currency and finance before full-scale war. These were economic as well as political events because they redirected investment, imports, labor, and public finance.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

There was no OPEC in 1936. Oil pricing was shaped by US production, state-level proration, private company behavior, and international concession systems. The US crude-oil first purchase price was around 1.09 dollars per barrel. Gold was fixed officially at 35 dollars per ounce in the United States, making gold inflows a monetary issue as much as a commodity issue.

Agricultural staples were recovering from Depression lows. Grain prices rose in 1936, aided by demand recovery and supply conditions. Export economies such as Canada, Australia, Argentina, Brazil, and Indonesia benefited to varying degrees. Metals and industrial raw materials gained from manufacturing and rearmament demand, especially in economies linked to steel, machinery, and armaments. The United States drought and weak European cereal harvests tightened grain supply. Annual sterling quotations from the Federico-Tena commodity price database for selected grades were as follows.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Employment improved but remained historically weak in several advanced economies. The United States unemployment rate in JST was 16.9 percent, the United Kingdom 9.4 percent, Canada 13.45 percent, the Netherlands 18.85 percent, and Australia 11.0 percent. These rates are not directly comparable because definitions, relief work, labor-force measurement, and social-insurance registration differed by country. Germany's low measured unemployment reflected rearmament, labor service, conscription, repression, and controls rather than a normal market recovery.

Demographically, the largest economies by total GDP were often not the richest per person. China and India ranked high by output because of population scale, while the United States, United Kingdom, Netherlands, Australia, Canada, and Belgium had much higher GDP per capita. Urbanization continued in industrial centers, but rural population shares remained very high in China, India, Indonesia, Brazil, Mexico, and the USSR. Migration was constrained by Depression-era restrictions and political barriers. World population in the Federico-Tena reconstruction on 1991 borders rose 1.35 percent, by 29.3 million, to 2,204.8 million.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States was the largest economy in the MPD 1936 ranking by a wide margin. The dominant macro story was recovery from the Depression rather than completion of recovery. Real output grew rapidly, equity prices rose strongly, commodity prices firmed, and New Deal spending continued, but unemployment remained exceptionally high. The policy mix was expansionary by interwar standards: gold inflows, low short-term interest rates, public works, relief employment, and financial stabilization all supported demand. The 1936 election year also gave fiscal policy a political dimension, as relief and public investment were central economic issues.

Microeconomically, autos, electrical equipment, construction materials, retailing, and consumer durables benefited from stronger household demand, while farm regions were affected by drought and commodity-price volatility. Banking was healthier than during the early 1930s, but credit demand was still not normal. Equity performance made 1936 look buoyant, yet with hindsight the economy was vulnerable to the 1937 tightening shock caused by fiscal retrenchment, monetary sterilization, and changes in required reserve ratios.

Industrial production in the League of Nations index (1929=100) rose 16.53%, from 75.6 to 88.1, faster than MPD GDP, while the BEA chained-dollar series shows real GDP up 12.88%. The Federal Reserve raised required reserve ratios by 50% effective after the close of 15 August and left the New York discount rate at 1.5%, and Treasury sterilization of new gold began after 23 December.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:**

Automobiles and durable goods led cyclical manufacturing; public works and housing supported construction; farm income improved unevenly; banking failures were far below crisis levels; consumer debt revived but remained constrained by Depression memories; no single national corporate ranking for 1936 revenues is used here because standardized global data were not available. Real consumption per person rose 9.49% in the Macrohistory reconstruction while bank loans grew 1.09%, and new-building contract floor space in 37 states rose from 28 to 46.

**Economic narrative:**

Germany's 1936 growth was powerful but deeply distorted by rearmament, controls, and hidden finance. The regime used public works, military orders, exchange controls, bilateral clearing agreements, and Mefo-bill finance to pull resources into heavy industry. The key structural shift was the Four Year Plan launched in 1936, which pushed autarky, raw-material substitution, military production, and state direction of investment. Measured CPI inflation was subdued because prices and wages were controlled, not because demand was balanced. The micro result was a guns-over-consumer-goods economy: steel, chemicals, machinery, aircraft, transport equipment, and synthetic materials gained, while consumer choice and import availability narrowed.

The League of Nations industrial index rose 13.09% against MPD GDP growth of 8.81%. **Micro/sectoral highlights:**

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

Spain's civil war was the most acute realized economic crisis of 1936. It destroyed ordinary national accounting, split financial institutions, and redirected resources toward war. The gold-bloc crisis was the main financial-system near-crisis, resolved partially through devaluation and the Tripartite Agreement. Italy's sanctions and Ethiopian-war costs produced fiscal and trade stress. China's currency reform stabilized one problem but did not remove the geopolitical risk that materialized in 1937.

Germany and Japan were the clearest systemic vulnerabilities building in plain sight. Rearmament generated growth and employment, but it also created import needs, fiscal pressures, labor controls, and strategic behavior that could not be reconciled with peaceful open trade. The Soviet Union's rapid industrialization created output gains and defense capacity, but also administrative distortions, consumer shortages, and political risks from purges.

With hindsight, the United States also carried a policy-mistake risk that materialized in 1937. Fiscal retrenchment, monetary sterilization, and changes in required reserve ratios helped produce a recession after the 1936 boom. This was not fully obvious from 1936 equity-market optimism, but the economy's high unemployment and dependence on policy support made it vulnerable.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1936 vs. 1935)

The main structural break from 1935 to 1936 was monetary. In 1935, the gold bloc still mattered as a policy constraint. By late 1936, France had devalued and the Tripartite Agreement had created a looser stabilization arrangement among the United States, United Kingdom, and France. This reduced the immediate pressure for competitive devaluation but confirmed the end of the old gold-standard regime.

Growth accelerated or stayed strong in much of the MPD top 20. The United States grew nearly 10 percent by the MPD real estimate, Germany nearly 9 percent, Brazil roughly 9.6 percent, Czechoslovakia roughly 8.2 percent, and Mexico roughly 7.8 percent, while Soviet official industrial output rose 30.30 percent in the League of Nations index. Spain was the stark exception, with an estimated contraction of almost 24 percent. Italy also contracted, reflecting war, sanctions, fiscal stress, and autarkic pressures.

The top-20 composition changed at the margin. Mexico entered the top 20, while Sweden exited. Spain fell from 11th to 14th. Brazil rose relative to Australia and Czechoslovakia. The top five remained the United States, Former USSR, China, Germany, and United Kingdom, but the meaning of their GDP differed sharply because the United States and United Kingdom were market economies, Germany was a controlled rearmament economy, the USSR was a non-market planned economy, and China was fragmented and only partly industrialized.

The sectoral delta was toward heavy industry, defense, construction, and commodities. Housing and consumer durables remained important in Anglo-American recovery. Rearmament gained weight in Germany, Japan, USSR, United Kingdom, France, Italy, and Czechoslovakia. Commodity rebound improved conditions in Canada, Australia, Brazil, Indonesia, and Mexico. Labor institutions shifted most dramatically in France.

Matched measures show the shift. MPD growth slowed by 2.61 pp in the United States but accelerated by 1.29 pp in Germany, 0.68 pp in the United Kingdom and 6.35 pp in France. French consumer prices moved from an 8.53 percent fall to a 6.67 percent rise, while Dutch prices still fell 4.50 percent. Official discount rates fell by 4 pp in France, 1.5 pp in the Netherlands, 0.5 pp in Italy and Czechoslovakia and 0.36 pp in Japan. The League of Nations index of world manufacturing and mining rose from 106.3 to 123.1, and from 93.7 to 105.0 excluding the USSR, and the reconstructed world trade quantum rose 4.95 percent.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

**Highest-confidence categories.** The highest-confidence data are the MPD 2023 GDP per capita and population series for the broad historical ranking, the JST R6 macro-financial series for advanced economies, the US oil-price series from EIA, the US gold-price context from Federal Reserve history, the US equity returns from historical index compilers, and the text and dating of the Tripartite Agreement. Even here, the GDP ranking is a real PPP historical reconstruction, not a 1936 contemporary accounting table.

**Moderate-confidence categories.** Advanced-economy inflation, unemployment, long rates, public debt, current accounts, and exchange rates are reasonably usable where JST provides data, although Belgium's external series is a merchandise trade balance. However, unemployment definitions vary widely, fiscal data can omit off-budget or hidden liabilities, and exchange-rate series can be hard to compare during devaluation years. Germany's Mefo finance and Spain's civil-war finance are especially difficult to reconcile with ordinary public-finance tables.

**Low-confidence categories.** Soviet GDP, China GDP, colonial GDP for India and Indonesia, civil-war Spain, and developing-country fiscal or unemployment indicators are lower confidence. For the USSR and other non-market systems, output was not measured by market prices and official reporting had political incentives, so the Soviet estimate carries substantial, unquantified uncertainty. For China, fragmented territorial control, silver-to-fabi monetary reform, and incomplete statistical administration make national indicators fragile. For colonies, statistics often measure trade and public finance better than household welfare or labor conditions.

**Major gaps.** Data not available for an independent official 1936 world GDP ranking from World Bank WDI or Penn World Table; both standard sources begin later for GDP comparisons. Data not available for a reliable global 1936 corporate ranking by revenue or market capitalization; standardized annual equity returns for all major world exchanges; comparable unemployment across all top 20 economies; R&D spending by country; global current-account aggregation; and many developing-country fiscal ratios.

**Weakest claims.** The weakest quantitative claims are the non-market and crisis-economy GDP estimates, especially Former USSR, China, and Spain. The strongest qualitative claims are the monetary-regime break, the rearmament shift, the Spanish Civil War shock, the French Popular Front labor shift, the Japanese fiscal-military policy break after February 1936, and the broad late-Depression recovery pattern.

**Indicator sources and definitions.** Policy rates are official discount rates in force at the end of the year as published in the Federal Reserve Bulletin of January 1937 (rates as of 28 December), not annual averages; Australia, Brazil, China and Mexico have no official rate shown and the Soviet State Bank rate is administered. Exchange rates are Federal Reserve annual averages of New York buying rates in US cents per local unit, so a rise means local appreciation; Macrohistory local-currency-per-dollar values are given as context. CPI, unemployment, debt, current-account and budget entries for advanced economies come from the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database (Release 6), and equity returns, long yields and housing capital gains from it and its rate-of-return extension; these data are used under the CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 licence. The Zurich rent index is taken from Historical Statistics of Switzerland Online under the CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 France licence. Budget balances are Data not available in the Value column because fiscal-year accounts and coverage are not reconciled with calendar-year GDP; the arithmetic ratios are given as context. Belgium's external series is a merchandise trade balance, Italy's unemployment entry is a linked index and France's unemployment covers only industrial wage and salary earners.

**Source conflicts.** MPD 2023 growth for the United States is 9.87 percent against 12.88 percent in the BEA chained-dollar series. The United States CPI comparator differs between the Macrohistory series (+2.24 percent in 1935) and the Bureau of Labor Statistics CPI-U (+2.2 percent). Japan's Macrohistory short rate (5.04 percent) does not reconcile with the official 3.29 percent discount rate. The United Kingdom budget arithmetic (+0.97 percent) is not shown as a value because its revenue series and perimeter are unresolved. The League of Nations trade quantum (+4.27 percent) and the Federico-Tena export quantum (+4.95 percent) differ in coverage. The 56-economy MPD total of 5.98 trillion includes one economy without a 1935 observation, so growth is computed on the 55 matched economies.

**Territorial definitions.** GDP growth for the former USSR is withheld because the national-income source behind the MPD record has not been matched to the territory of its population series; the raw record change is +7.94 percent. India corresponds to undivided India, and Indonesia to the MPD Indonesia series covering the Dutch East Indies.
