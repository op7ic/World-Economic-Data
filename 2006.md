# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 2006

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

2006 was the last full year of the mid-2000s global boom before the U.S. subprime shock became a visible systemic crisis. World output expanded rapidly, with the IMF projecting global real growth at about 5.1 percent for the year in September 2006, while World Bank WDI/WITS put world GDP at roughly USD 51.86 trillion in current dollars. The IMF's April 2007 estimate of completed-year growth was 5.4 percent, and the October 2026 IMF WEO series gives 5.290 percent on PPP weights and 3.962 percent at market exchange rates. Merchandise trade growth was exceptionally strong: the WTO estimated real merchandise exports rose about 8 percent, while the IMF's later statistical tables put world trade in goods and services volume growth at about 9.2 percent. The basic story was synchronized expansion, high commodity prices, abundant global liquidity, and large but uneven financial leverage.

The center of gravity continued shifting toward emerging Asia. China ranked fourth by current-dollar GDP in the World Bank series, overtaking several advanced European economies in nominal scale and recording double-digit real growth. India remained smaller in current-dollar terms but grew rapidly and deepened its services, telecom, finance, and infrastructure expansion. Resource exporters such as Russia, Brazil, Canada, and Australia benefited from high oil, metals, and agricultural prices, while current account surpluses in China, Germany, Japan, Russia, the Netherlands, Sweden, Switzerland, and oil exporters fed the global savings imbalance.

At the same time, the vulnerabilities that later defined 2007 and 2008 were already visible. The Federal Reserve raised the federal funds target to 5.25 percent on 29 June, the ECB raised its main refinancing rate to 3.50 percent in December, the Bank of Japan ended quantitative easing in March and zero interest rates in July, and other central banks tightened. Easing was not universal: Brazil cut the Selic to 13.25 percent and Bank Indonesia cut its BI Rate to 9.75 percent by December. U.S. housing activity weakened sharply, and early stress in subprime mortgages was noted in IMF analysis; U.S. regulators issued final guidance on nontraditional mortgage products on 29 September. Credit spreads remained compressed and equity markets rose in many countries, showing how financial markets still treated the downturn as contained.

Microeconomically, 2006 was a year of corporate scale, energy profits, bank listings, and cross-border consolidation. ICBC completed a record global IPO, Arcelor and Mittal created a steel giant, AT&T acquired BellSouth (closing on 29 December), and NYSE and Euronext agreed to merge. Technology, telecommunications, finance, energy, autos, steel, mining, and construction were key transmission channels. Consumers in the U.S., U.K., Spain, and Australia relied heavily on housing wealth and credit; consumers in China, India, Russia, Brazil, and Turkey were pulled into widening domestic markets.

The top-20 ranking in section 4 is a nominal, current-dollar, market-exchange-rate ranking based on World Bank WDI/WITS data. On the Maddison Project Database 2023 measure in constant 2011 international dollars, China ranks second, India fourth and Russia sixth.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 2006

**1. The global expansion reached late-cycle speed.** The economic significance of 2006 is that growth was still broad-based, but it was no longer early-cycle recovery. The United States grew moderately while housing slowed; the euro area accelerated after years of weak domestic demand; Japan continued its post-deflation recovery; China, India, Russia, Brazil, and Turkey expanded quickly. Forward linkage: the strong 2006 data encouraged confidence, but the same late-cycle conditions made the 2007 credit shock more dangerous.

**2. The world economy became more commodity constrained.** Oil averaged about USD 64.29 per barrel on the World Bank crude oil average series, up from about USD 53.39 in 2005. The IMF reported oil prices up about 20.5 percent in U.S. dollar terms and nonfuel commodities up about 28.4 percent, with metals up about 56.5 percent. In the IMF's October 2026 benchmark series, LME copper averaged US$6,731 per metric ton, up 83.1 percent. World Bank commodity notes reported record or multi-year highs in copper, zinc, crude oil, gold, sugar, rubber, and other commodities at different points in the year. Forward linkage: these prices drove capital spending in mining and energy and strengthened resource exporters, but also pressured inflation and household purchasing power.

**3. Monetary normalization spread across advanced economies.** The Fed, ECB, Bank of England, Bank of Canada, RBA, Riksbank, and other central banks either tightened or held policy rates at restrictive or less accommodative levels. The Bank of Japan's July rate hike from zero to 0.25 percent was historically important because it marked the exit from the zero-interest-rate policy after years of deflation pressure. Several emerging economies moved the other way: Brazil and Indonesia eased. Forward linkage: tighter funding conditions tested leveraged borrowers, especially U.S. housing and structured credit.

**4. Global imbalances became central rather than peripheral.** The United States ran a very large current account deficit, while China, Germany, Japan, Russia, oil exporters, and several northern European economies ran surpluses. In the BIS's contemporary compilation, China's current account surplus was US$239 billion and Russia's US$96 billion. China also operated a managed exchange rate, allowing gradual renminbi appreciation while reserve accumulation continued. Forward linkage: these imbalances shaped exchange-rate politics, reserve management, and the flow of savings into dollar credit markets before the crisis.

**5. Trade globalization was strong, but multilateral governance stalled.** The WTO estimated real merchandise exports grew about 8 percent in 2006, but the Doha Development Agenda negotiations were suspended on 24 July after gaps among negotiators remained too wide. Forward linkage: trade kept growing, but global trade policy shifted further toward bilateral, regional, and sectoral bargaining.

**6. Finance and corporate control were globalizing rapidly.** Banks, exchanges, and industrial companies used cheap financing and high valuations to consolidate. The ICBC IPO signaled China's arrival in global capital markets; the NYSE-Euronext agreement signaled exchange consolidation; Arcelor-Mittal signaled global steel consolidation. Forward linkage: corporate globalization continued, but the leverage and risk transfer mechanisms used in 2006 became stress points in 2007-2008.

**7. Housing cycles diverged, but the U.S. cycle turned.** U.S. residential investment weakened and subprime mortgage stress began to receive explicit attention. Spain, the U.K., Canada, Australia, the Netherlands, and some emerging markets still had strong property or construction cycles; the BIS reported nominal residential price increases of 9.1 percent in Spain and the U.K. and 9.4 percent in Canada, against 5.9 percent in the United States and no change in Germany. Forward linkage: the U.S. housing downturn became the first major channel through which financial fragility emerged.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The United States tightened first and hardest among the largest economies. The FOMC raised the federal funds target to 5.00 percent in May and 5.25 percent on 29 June, then paused as housing cooled. The separate primary-credit (discount) rate rose to 6.25 percent; Federal Reserve discount minutes record implementation on 29 June at most Reserve Banks, 30 June at St. Louis and 6 July at Kansas City. The yield curve flattened and inverted at points, showing markets expected slower growth or future easing. U.S. equities recovered from a spring correction and the S&P 500 price index gained about 13.6 percent for the year, while total return was about 15.8 percent.

The euro area moved from accommodative toward neutral. The ECB lifted the main refinancing rate to 3.50 percent in December after multiple increases during the year: the 7 December decision applied the 3.50 percent minimum bid rate from the operation settled on 13 December, with the deposit and marginal lending facility rates at 2.50 and 4.50 percent from that day. Germany's recovery was especially strong, and euro-area credit growth remained robust. The Bank of England lifted Bank Rate to 5.00 percent at its 8-9 November meeting and maintained it in December; the November decision was taken by a 7-2 vote, with two members preferring to hold at 4.75 percent. The Bank of Japan ended quantitative easing in March and ended zero rates in July, two separate decisions. From the 14 July announcement the uncollateralized overnight call rate was guided to around 0.25 percent and the basic loan rate became 0.4 percent, but Japanese policy remained highly accommodative by global standards.

Other dated decisions show that policy did not move in one direction. The People's Bank of China raised the one-year benchmark lending rate to 5.85 percent from 28 April and 6.12 percent from 19 August. The Reserve Bank of India's LAF repo rate reached 7.25 percent from 31 October, and the cash reserve ratio rose to 5.25 percent from 23 December. The Bank of Canada held its target for the overnight rate at 4.25 percent on 5 December, with the Bank Rate 25 basis points higher at 4.50 percent. The RBA's 8 November release announced the Board's decision of the previous day to raise the cash rate to 6.25 percent. Brazil's Copom cut the Selic target to 13.25 percent on 29 November, effective 30 November. Bank Indonesia cut its BI Rate by 50 basis points on 5 October, 7 November and 7 December, ending the year at 9.75 percent, easing after the 2005 inflation shock. The Bank of Thailand's 14-day repurchase rate reached 5 percent in June and was held there at year-end.

Financial markets remained confident. BIS analysis for the period emphasizes strong profits, buybacks, mergers and acquisitions, low credit spreads, and strong risk appetite. There was a May-June correction in risky assets, particularly emerging-market equities and commodities, but it was short-lived. This was a classic late-cycle market pattern: investors recognized inflation and credit risks but continued to price them as manageable.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

Trade volumes were one of the brightest indicators of 2006. Asia led export volume growth, with China continuing to gain manufacturing share. The United States, Europe, Japan, and emerging markets all participated in the upswing. The suspension of the WTO Doha Round negotiations on 24 July was the year's most important trade-policy failure. It did not stop trade growth in 2006, but it showed that global liberalization through a single multilateral agreement had become politically harder.

Geopolitical shocks had direct economic channels. The Russia-Ukraine gas dispute in January briefly affected European gas supply and showed the strategic risk embedded in pipeline energy dependence. The Middle East remained central to oil risk premia. Iran-related concerns and other supply risks supported oil and gold; the IMF's March 2007 Article IV consultation with Iran identified tensions over the nuclear program as an explicit risk. OPEC responded to falling oil prices late in the year with production cuts, including a 1.2 million barrel per day cut taking effect in November. Announcements, production quotas and actual supply are not interchangeable measures. In December, Thailand imposed a 30 percent reserve on short-term capital inflows, effective 19 December in the Bank of Thailand's printed measures table, with a relaxation for listed equities from 22 December, a capital-account shock independent of the U.S. housing cycle.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Energy was central. Crude oil prices reached new highs in mid-2006, then fell in the autumn as demand concerns increased and inventories improved. OPEC's late-year cuts aimed to stabilize the market. EIA annual spot averages were about US$65.16 per barrel for Brent and US$66.05 for WTI; Banco de Mexico's report gives a WTI average of US$66.2, a different vintage and averaging convention, and the World Bank's US$64.29 three-crude average is not either grade. Annual averages are distinct from intraday peaks, the OPEC basket or posted prices. Natural gas politics were visible in Europe through the Russia-Ukraine dispute. Coal prices were relatively stable compared with oil and metals, but energy-intensive industries faced elevated input costs.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor markets improved in most advanced economies, but definitions remain non-comparable. The country unemployment rates in section 4 are ILO modeled estimates from the World Bank WDI, not a homogeneous set of national surveys. U.S. unemployment averaged about 4.6 percent. Japan's rate was around 4.1 percent (4.2 percent ILO modeled). Germany still had high unemployment but was improving as export strength and labor-market reforms began to show results. Spain and Italy had lower unemployment than in earlier years but still had structural joblessness. China and India continued absorbing labor into manufacturing, construction, services, and urban employment, though official unemployment measures do not capture underemployment well: China's registered urban unemployment rate was 4.1 percent, a measure that excludes much rural and migrant underemployment, against an ILO modeled 4.4 percent. China's NBS reported urban real disposable-income growth of 10.4 percent and rural real net-income growth of 7.4 percent, different income concepts for different populations.

Demographically, advanced economies faced aging, while emerging Asia still had rising working-age populations or large labor reservoirs. Migration mattered economically: eastern European labor mobility into the U.K., Ireland, and other EU economies after the 2004 enlargement continued to shape wages and services, while remittances and emigration were significant for Mexico, India, China, Turkey, Russia's neighbors, and parts of eastern Europe. Mexico's remittance inflows were US$23.054 billion in Banco de Mexico's preliminary figures, a flow measure rather than a count of migrants. A matched origin-destination migration flow panel: Data not available.

**Economic narrative:**

The dominant U.S. story was a transition from credit-fueled housing expansion to housing drag. Consumption remained resilient and corporate profits were strong, but residential investment weakened. ### 5B. Corporate landscape

The corporate landscape was dominated by oil, retail, autos, finance, and heavy industry. The Fortune Global 500 2007 ranking, based on 2006 revenues for company fiscal periods rather than a uniform calendar year, placed Wal-Mart first, Exxon Mobil second, Royal Dutch Shell third, BP fourth, Toyota fifth, Chevron sixth, ING seventh, Total eighth, General Motors ninth, and ConocoPhillips tenth. The mix tells the story of 2006: high oil prices, global retail scale, autos under pressure, and finance expanding rapidly.

Major corporate events included AT&T's acquisition of BellSouth, a transaction valued at USD 67 billion at announcement by the U.S. Department of Justice and widely reported at USD 86 billion at closing depending on valuation basis; Mittal's acquisition of Arcelor, creating the largest steelmaker; ICBC's IPO of about USD 21.9 billion, then the largest global IPO; and the NYSE-Euronext merger agreement, which was an agreement in 2006 rather than a completed merger. Other important transactions included Freeport-McMoRan's announced purchase of Phelps Dodge, Boston Scientific's acquisition of Guidant, and Tata Steel's pursuit of Corus, announced in 2006 and completed in 2007.

AT&T's 2006 annual report dates the BellSouth closing to 29 December and reports shareholder consideration of about US$86.9 billion at the closing share price of US$35.75, while purchase accounting used a five-day announcement-window average of US$27.32 plus transaction costs and other adjustments, giving an accounting purchase price of US$66.798 billion. Announcement, closing and accounting valuations are therefore not interchangeable, and AT&T's reported 2006 income includes only post-closing BellSouth results.

ICBC's results announcement separates A-share proceeds of about RMB 46.64 billion from H-share proceeds of RMB 126.58 billion, the latter including RMB 24.67 billion of net proceeds from secondary state-share sales collected for the Ministry of Finance and Huijin, so the offering headline is not all fresh capital retained by the bank. Its year-end net regulatory capital of RMB 530,805 million against risk-weighted assets plus market-risk adjustment of RMB 3,779,170 million gives the 14.05 percent capital adequacy ratio; accounting equity was RMB 471,001 million and equity attributable to shareholders RMB 466,464 million.

Goldman Sachs' fiscal-year (November) accounts show that rising balance sheets did not always mean higher reported leverage: total assets rose from US$706,804 million to US$838,201 million, but shareholders' equity rose from US$28,002 million to US$35,786 million, so reported accounting leverage fell from 25.2 to 23.4 times and adjusted leverage from 17.9 to 16.1 times. Neither is a regulatory capital ratio, and falling year-end leverage does not establish lower intra-year, contingent or market risk.

Bankruptcies and failures were not yet the defining story, but early stress existed in U.S. mortgage finance. The bigger story was balance-sheet expansion, structured finance, private-equity leverage, and international listings. With hindsight, 2006 was the year when risk was being transferred and hidden rather than eliminated. In Thailand, the banking system reported 2006 operating profit of 160.7 billion baht and net profit of 73.6 billion baht, with higher fourth-quarter provisions linked by the Bank of Thailand to IAS 39 adoption from December.

### 5C. Innovation & technology

The economic technology story was not one single breakthrough but diffusion. Mobile telephony expanded rapidly in China, India, Brazil, Turkey, and Russia. Broadband penetration rose in advanced economies. Semiconductors, consumer electronics, LCD panels, telecom equipment, and software services drove global supply chains. India strengthened its IT and outsourcing role; China moved from low-end assembly toward more complex electronics and capital goods.

Digital platforms were still early relative to the 2010s. Google, Apple, Microsoft, Yahoo, Amazon, eBay, Samsung, Nokia, Ericsson, Sony, and telecom carriers were major actors. The iPhone had not yet launched, so the smartphone platform shift was still just ahead. R&D spending was concentrated in the U.S., Japan, Germany, Korea, Switzerland, Sweden, and large multinationals.

Infrastructure megaprojects mattered in China, India, the Gulf outside this top-20 list, and parts of Europe. Ports, airports, highways, power networks, and urban real estate absorbed large capital flows. Saudi Arabia's central bank governor, presenting SAMA's annual report in September 2007, described development projects approved in 2006 and early 2007 across energy, transport, utilities, education and health; approval is not completion. Comparable global R&D expenditure, patent counts and infrastructure commissioning dates for 2006: Data not available.

### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

The global labor market was split. Advanced economies saw improving employment but wage restraint in many sectors. Germany's wage moderation improved competitiveness. The U.S. labor market was tight by unemployment measures, but manufacturing employment remained pressured. Japan's labor market improved, yet wages stayed weak.

Emerging economies absorbed workers into cities, construction, manufacturing, services, and retail. China and India had enormous labor reallocation from agriculture to urban sectors, but official unemployment data understate underemployment. Migration shaped labor supply in the U.K., Spain, Canada, the U.S., Russia, and Gulf economies outside this top-20 ranking. Union power was generally weaker than in earlier decades, though France, Italy, Korea, and parts of Latin America maintained visible labor mobilization. ### 6B. Fixed income

The U.S. Treasury curve flattened and inverted during parts of 2006 as the Fed reached 5.25 percent and markets priced slower growth. German Bund yields rose with ECB tightening. Japanese government bond yields rose from very low levels but remained low globally. Credit spreads were tight, especially in investment grade, high yield, leveraged loans, and structured credit. This was one of the key warning signs with hindsight: the market was being paid little for taking increasing leverage and maturity risk. BIS spread measures use month-end option-adjusted corporate spreads over government bonds, while CDS-basis measures use Libor and on-the-run five-year contracts, so they are not one generic spread; exact complete yield curves and spread endpoints are Data not available.

The BIS's 17 November 2006 release reported end-June OTC derivatives notional amounts of US$370 trillion and gross market value of US$10 trillion. Notional amounts are contractual reference quantities, not bank assets or expected losses, and gross market values are replacement values before netting and collateral; these are June stocks, not December totals.

Sovereign debt stress was limited among the top 20 in 2006. Emerging-market spreads generally compressed after recovering from the mid-year sell-off. Brazil, Russia, Turkey, and Mexico all benefited from better macro frameworks than in the 1990s, but Turkey remained sensitive to global risk appetite.

### 6C. Currencies

The dollar weakened against the euro during the year after earlier strength. The yen remained relatively weak and was used in carry trades. Sterling was firm, reflecting U.K. rates and financial inflows. Commodity currencies such as the Canadian dollar and Australian dollar were supported by resource prices. China's renminbi appreciated gradually under the post-2005 managed-float framework, but not enough to end trade tensions.

In WDI official annual averages, the euro averaged EUR 0.796 per dollar (units per dollar down 0.9 percent from 2005, about USD 1.26 per euro), the yen JPY 116.3 (up 5.5 percent), sterling GBP 0.543 (down 1.2 percent), the renminbi CNY 7.973 (down 2.7 percent), the Canadian dollar CAD 1.134 (down 6.4 percent), the Brazilian real BRL 2.175 (down 10.6 percent), the won KRW 955.3 (down 6.7 percent) and the Turkish lira TRY 1.428 (up 6.3 percent). A fall in local currency units per dollar indicates a stronger local currency. Annual-average changes are not December-to-December returns or trade-weighted indices, and the reciprocal of an average euro rate is not generally the average dollar-per-euro rate.

Emerging-market currencies experienced a sharp but temporary stress episode in May-June. Turkey was one of the most affected among the top 20, while Brazil and Russia were cushioned by commodities and reserve accumulation. Thailand's December capital-inflow measures, outside the top 20, showed that capital-account controls could return abruptly; the measures did not impose identical restrictions on all FDI, trade payments and existing positions.

### 6D. Alternative assets

Gold averaged about USD 604 per troy ounce and reached about USD 725, reflecting oil prices, inflation concerns, geopolitical risk, and dollar weakness. Real estate was the most important alternative asset class for households. U.S. housing turned down; Spain, the U.K., Australia, Canada, and parts of emerging Asia were still strong. Commercial real estate benefited from easy credit and private-equity flows. Commodities became a larger institutional asset class through index products and commodity-linked strategies.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

Active risks in 2006 included U.S. housing deterioration, subprime mortgage credit quality, high oil prices, geopolitical energy disruptions, inflation pressure, the Doha Round suspension, emerging-market capital-flow volatility, and compressed credit spreads. The Russia-Ukraine gas dispute was a live energy-security shock. The May-June sell-off in equities, commodities, and emerging markets was a near-miss that showed risk appetite could reverse quickly.

With hindsight, the most important vulnerability was the U.S. mortgage and structured-credit chain. At the time, many observers saw subprime as a contained segment. Later evidence showed that mortgage securitization, wholesale funding, bank and broker-dealer leverage, rating-agency assumptions, and global demand for AAA structured products created systemic channels. Housing deterioration was observable during 2006, but a systemic crisis was not a realized 2006 outcome. The June FOMC statement recognized housing cooling. The interagency guidance on nontraditional mortgage product risks was proposed on 29 December 2005, issued in final form on 29 September 2006, published in the Federal Register on 4 October and transmitted by supervisory letter SR 06-15 on 10 October; it addressed payment shock, negative amortization and layered underwriting risk in nontraditional products, and was neither a ban on subprime credit nor a new capital rule. The BIS annual report released in June 2007 put end-2006 subprime delinquency at about 13 percent, with subprime loans at 21 percent of new lending against 14 percent of outstanding mortgages, flow and stock shares with different denominators; lender failures and HSBC's provisioning announcement were 2007 developments.

Another hindsight risk was European bank exposure to global credit. Banks in Germany, Switzerland, the U.K., Belgium, the Netherlands, and France looked profitable in 2006, but several had large structured-credit and wholesale-funding exposures. Spain's risks were more domestic: real estate, cajas, construction employment, and external deficits.

Outside the top 20, Thailand's December capital-control shock and the correction in Saudi Arabia's equity market were notable stress episodes. Iran's March 2007 IMF consultation estimated fiscal 2005/06 growth at 5.5 percent and projected 5.8 percent for 2006/07 (fiscal years beginning 21 March); December 2006 twelve-month inflation was 15.9 percent, and the projected non-oil central government deficit of 17.75 percent of GDP is a non-oil measure, not the overall budget balance. The authorities reduced state-bank lending returns and set private-bank rates in March 2006, and a proposed privatization of 80 percent of state companies over ten years was an intention rather than completed 2006 asset sales.

Risks that did not materialize in 2006 included a hard landing in China, a generalized oil-supply crisis, a collapse in the dollar, a major sovereign debt crisis among the top 20, or a global recession. Instead, the year ended with markets optimistic and most macro indicators strong.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (2006 vs. 2005)

Compared with 2005, 2006 was faster globally and more late-cycle financially. World GDP growth remained above trend, and merchandise trade growth accelerated. In the IMF's October 2026 series, PPP-weighted world growth rose from 4.713 to 5.290 percent and goods-and-services trade volume growth from 7.807 to 9.133 percent. Commodity prices moved higher, especially oil and metals, although oil fell from its mid-year peak by autumn; the IMF copper benchmark rose 83.1 percent and wheat 30.4 percent. China became more visibly central to world demand, not only as an exporter but as a commodity and capital-market force.

The main policy shift was monetary normalization. The Fed moved from 4.25 percent at end-2005 to 5.25 percent by mid-2006. The ECB moved from 2.25 percent at end-2005 to 3.50 percent by end-2006. The Bank of Japan left zero rates. The Bank of England, RBA, Riksbank, and many emerging-market central banks tightened or remained restrictive. India's repo rate reached 7.25 percent by year-end, up 1.00 percentage point. Monetary paths nevertheless diverged: Brazil and Indonesia eased. Liquidity was still ample, but the cost of funds rose.

The main structural break was in U.S. housing. In 2005 the housing boom still looked like a driver of household wealth and construction employment. In 2006 it became a drag on GDP and the first clear source of credit deterioration. This did not yet dominate global markets, but it changed the risk map. Many other national property indices still rose.

In trade policy, 2006 was worse than 2005 because the Doha Round suspension signaled an institutional failure. In corporate finance, 2006 was more aggressive than 2005: larger IPOs, larger M&A, more exchange consolidation, stronger private equity, and broader leveraged finance. ICBC's IPO changed ownership, capital and financial-service distribution, but its secondary state-share proceeds were not new issuer cash. Higher assets at Goldman Sachs did not mean higher year-end accounting leverage. Mergers announced in 2006 and closed in 2007 remain distinct from the completed BellSouth acquisition.

Economic power continued shifting. The United States remained dominant, but China moved rapidly up the nominal GDP ranking and became more central in trade, commodities, and capital markets. India gained visibility as a services and domestic-demand growth story. Russia and Brazil benefited from the resource cycle. Germany regained strength within Europe, with MPD growth accelerating by 3.0 percentage points. China's contemporary preliminary growth of 10.7 percent differs from later WDI and MPD values; a later revision of growth is not itself a 2006 economic event.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

Highest-confidence data in this assessment are current-dollar GDP ranking values for countries with explicit 2006 GDP values on World Bank WITS summary pages, global GDP from World Bank WITS, global growth and trade from IMF and WTO, major central-bank policy rates from official central-bank releases, and commodity annual averages from World Bank Pink Sheet and IMF primary commodity price data.

Medium-confidence data include country-level inflation, unemployment, current-account balances, debt, and fiscal balances. These are usually available from IMF WEO, OECD, Eurostat, World Bank, and national statistical agencies, but vintages and definitions differ. Unemployment is especially non-comparable: the ILO modeled estimates used in section 4, registered urban unemployment in China, household-survey unemployment in advanced economies, and informality-heavy labor markets in India, Mexico, Brazil, and Turkey do not measure the same economic condition. Policy-rate values for Korea, Mexico, Russia and Turkey, and the Swiss setting, are approximate year-end values rather than dated decision records. India's year-end LAF repo rate was 7.25 percent, effective 31 October; the 7.50 percent rate took effect on 31 January 2007. For the Netherlands, Switzerland, Sweden and Belgium, and for Korean government debt, the indicators are approximate values not taken from the IMF WEO, apart from the dated ECB rate and WDI euro exchange rate.

Lower-confidence or explicitly incomplete items include exact 2006 reserve-currency shares, some country budget-balance measures, some government debt measures for emerging economies, annual oil production totals, and full comparable sectoral employment by country. These are marked as Data not available where no defensible number is available; gold's annual average and peak are approximate. Fiscal series differ in perimeter and period: India's IMF series are April-March fiscal years covering the center and states; Mexico's IMF perimeter includes public corporations; Australia's calendar-year fiscal estimates average two fiscal years; and Japan's IMF debt is on an unconsolidated basis.

The top-20 GDP ranking is nominal, current-dollar, market-exchange-rate based. It is not PPP GDP and not real GDP. Exchange-rate movements can change nominal rankings even when real output rankings do not change. For non-market economies this caveat would be larger, but the 2006 top 20 does not include the Soviet Union or pre-transition centrally planned economies. China's GDP figures are official national-account data in WDI/IMF, but analysts still debate local-government reporting incentives and later revisions. Government ratios use nominal GDP denominators and are never computed from PPP levels.
