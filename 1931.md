# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1931

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1931 was the year when the Great Depression became a fully global monetary, banking, trade, and political crisis. The downturn was no longer only a US equity-market and industrial collapse. It had become a worldwide contraction transmitted through the gold standard, falling commodity prices, bank runs, tariff retaliation, exchange controls, and debt-deflation. In 1931, Britain left gold, Germany imposed controls after a banking crisis, Japan prepared and then executed a reflationary break with gold, and the United States defended gold while its banking system deteriorated.

The dominant economic force was deflation. Falling prices raised real debt burdens, reduced profits, forced inventory liquidation, and caused households to defer spending. The countries most damaged were those combining gold-standard rigidity, foreign short-term liabilities, export dependence, and fragile banking systems. Germany, Canada, the United States, Poland, Argentina, the Dutch East Indies, Australia, and many commodity exporters were especially exposed. Countries that later recovered earlier, especially Japan and Britain, did so partly because abandoning gold created scope for currency depreciation, easier money, and changed expectations.

The year also showed that the world economy was not a single synchronized machine. The MPD 2023 historical PPP estimates put the United States first, followed by China, the former USSR, India, Germany, and the United Kingdom. This ranking differs from nominal-dollar rankings and from modern GDP lists. China and India ranked highly because of population scale. The Soviet estimate is especially uncertain because it describes a planned, non-market economy during forced industrialization and collectivization.

Microeconomically, 1931 was a year of broken balance sheets. Banks failed or were rescued, firms cut investment, farms and commodity producers faced extreme price declines, and consumers shifted from discretionary spending to survival and debt service. The corporate landscape was not defined by IPOs or expansionary mergers; it was defined by bank rescues, forced restructurings, state intervention, collapsing equity valuations, and the beginning of a more interventionist economic regime.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1931

**1. The gold standard moved from stabilizer to crisis amplifier.** The international gold-exchange standard required deficit or reserve-losing countries to defend parity through tighter money, higher rates, fiscal restraint, or deflation. In 1931, that mechanism became destabilizing. Britain's September suspension of gold payments was the clearest regime shift. Germany's July crisis produced capital controls and a de facto break from normal convertibility. Japan's December departure from gold set up one of the earliest recoveries among major economies. The forward linkage was decisive: countries leaving gold earlier generally gained more monetary flexibility and recovered earlier.

**2. Banking crises became macroeconomic crises.** The Austrian Credit-Anstalt crisis, German Danatbank collapse, banking holidays, and US bank failures showed that financial institutions were not merely passive victims of the depression. Bank distress cut credit, destroyed deposits, disrupted payments, and forced governments into emergency action. Germany's 1931 crisis remains debated: one interpretation emphasizes bank fragility, another emphasizes currency and fiscal-political credibility. The shared conclusion is that bank and currency crises reinforced one another.

**3. Debt-deflation punished farmers, homeowners, and leveraged firms.** Falling prices increased real debt burdens. This was especially destructive in agriculture, real estate, construction, and capital-intensive industry. Wheat, coffee, rubber, sugar, cotton, and other commodity markets weakened sharply. The burden fell on farmers in Canada, Australia, India, Argentina, Brazil, and the United States, as well as on plantation economies such as the Dutch East Indies.

**4. Trade protection and shrinking trade worsened the slump.** Smoot-Hawley was enacted in 1930, but its effects were part of the 1931 environment. Retaliation, quotas, exchange controls, and import compression reduced international trade further. The issue was not only tariffs; weak demand and payments stress reduced trade even when formal barriers were unchanged. The forward linkage was the gradual move from a liberal pre-1914 trade-finance order toward managed trade, blocs, and bilateralism.

**5. The policy regime began shifting toward state intervention.** 1931 was before the US New Deal and before many later recovery programs, but the direction was visible. Governments guaranteed banks, imposed controls, debated public works, destroyed or stored agricultural surpluses, managed exchange, and intervened in wages, prices, and budgets. In planned economies, especially the USSR, state intervention was not a response to the market collapse but the organizing principle of production itself.

**6. Economic crisis had political consequences.** The German crisis fed radicalization. Spain's Republic faced economic stress during political transition. Argentina and Brazil were already under post-1930 political regimes that moved toward intervention. China faced the Manchurian crisis. These developments were not only geopolitical; they changed investment, trade, credit, labor organization, and fiscal priorities.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

No official, universally accepted 1931 world GDP exists. MPD 2023 provides country GDP per capita and population estimates, which can be multiplied to derive real PPP GDP in 2011 international dollars. For the 56 MPD country rows with both GDP per capita and population in 1931, the covered-economy sum is approximately I$5.275 trillion. On the 55-country common set available in both 1930 and 1931, this covered-economy GDP fell by about 3.7 percent. This is not a formal world aggregate, because many countries lack complete 1931 rows and because PPP historical reconstruction is not the same as contemporaneous national accounting.

Global trade volume and value contracted under the combined pressure of depression, tariffs, exchange controls, collapsing prices, and weak demand. In the Federico-Tena World Trade Historical Database, the world export volume index (1913=100) fell from 123.28 to 108.77, or 11.77%, while nominal world exports fell from $26,443.9 million to $18,556.4 million, or 29.83%; price-deflated exports fell from 23,097.5 to 20,379.6 million 1913 dollars. The League of Nations' interwar trade reviews remain the primary period source, but values, quantities, and prices must be treated separately because deflation made nominal trade declines look even larger. The Federico-Tena world population reconstruction on 1991 borders rises from 2,043.9 million to 2,072.0 million, or 1.38%.

Reserve-currency dynamics were unstable. Sterling had been a central reserve currency, but Britain's September 1931 gold suspension weakened confidence in the old system. The US dollar remained on gold, and the French franc remained part of the gold bloc, but this did not imply general monetary stability. Gold flowed toward countries perceived as safer, while countries under reserve pressure were forced into higher rates, controls, or devaluation. The Bank for International Settlements estimated the foreign-exchange holdings of 26 central banks at roughly 11 billion Swiss francs at the beginning of 1931; reserve-currency shares for the world as a whole are Data not available.

Global inflation trends were strongly deflationary in industrial and commodity economies. Exact global CPI is Data not available. Commodity prices weakened broadly. Gold was fixed in the United States at $20.67 per fine troy ounce. Oil was in oversupply, especially after the East Texas field boom, where regulation, proration, and illegal production became central issues. Agricultural staples such as wheat, coffee, sugar, cotton, and rubber were under severe price pressure.

International capital flows moved away from weaker debtors and toward perceived safe centers, but even safe centers were unstable. German and Austrian banking distress, sterling pressure, and US bank failures show that capital did not simply flow smoothly to safety; it frequently froze, fled, or was trapped by controls. The Bank for International Settlements estimated in 1932 that about 5 billion Swiss francs of external emergency advances were extended and more than 30 billion of short-term debt was liquidated during 1931, partly by transfer between foreign creditors rather than net repatriation.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The interest-rate environment was dominated by gold defense and confidence shocks. The Federal Reserve raised discount and acceptance-buying rates in the fall of 1931 to slow gold outflows. Britain left gold in September, which later enabled lower rates. Germany's banking crisis led to bank holidays, capital controls, and abnormal credit conditions. France held to gold and remained more orthodox. Japan left gold in December and moved toward the Takahashi policy mix of exchange depreciation, fiscal support, and monetary ease. Year-end official rates captured the reversals: the New York discount rate ended at 3.5% after a low of 1.5% in May, Bank Rate at 6% against 3% a year earlier, and the Reichsbank rate at 7% after a peak of 15% on 1 August.

Exchange-rate regimes were in transition. Bretton Woods did not exist. The world was still under the interwar gold-exchange standard, but 1931 shattered the credibility of universal convertibility. Sterling floated after September. Germany imposed controls. Japan left gold in December. The United States stayed on gold until 1933. France and several others remained in the gold bloc.

Stock markets were deeply depressed. US broad-equity total returns were strongly negative in 1931, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average suffered one of its worst annual losses. Long-run rate-of-return reconstructions show nominal total returns of -40.30% in the United States, -34.18% in Germany, -31.90% in France and -20.13% in the United Kingdom, against gains in Japan and Australia; exchange-specific index levels for most markets are Data not available. Bond markets were divided between government securities, which could benefit from safety demand, and corporate or sovereign-risk instruments, which suffered from default and credit concerns.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The trade regime was increasingly protectionist. Smoot-Hawley remained a major symbol and mechanism of US protection, but the broader story included retaliatory tariffs, quotas, exchange restrictions, and managed trade. Trade contraction was caused by both policy barriers and collapsing demand.

Geopolitics mattered. The Manchurian Incident in September 1931 changed East Asian trade, investment, and security expectations. The Hoover Moratorium temporarily addressed German reparations and intergovernmental debts, but it did not restore confidence. The proposal, released on 21 June, covered one year of intergovernmental payments from 1 July, subject to agreement; the London Protocol followed on 11 August, and private debts were excluded. Exchange restrictions followed in Germany on 15 July and Czechoslovakia on 3 October. European integration as understood after 1945 did not exist. Regional economic blocs were beginning to form informally through currency areas, colonial systems, and trade preference debates.

Oil was marked by overproduction, especially in the United States. The East Texas field produced more than 121 million barrels of legal output in 1931 according to the Texas historical account, and regulatory proration became a defining issue. **Economic narrative:**

The United States remained the largest economy in the world, but 1931 was a year of deepening depression rather than ordinary cyclical weakness. MPD-derived real PPP GDP fell by about 6.4 percent from 1930, and the fall in commodity prices, construction, autos, steel, banking balance sheets, and household wealth kept aggregate demand weak. The gold-standard constraint mattered: the Federal Reserve defended gold reserves and confidence rather than acting as an aggressive lender of last resort. The banking panics that began in 1930 intensified through 1931 and 1932, tightening credit for businesses and households.

The domestic policy mix was fragmented. Federal public works and farm relief existed, but they were not yet the large New Deal programs of 1933 onward. Tariff protection after Smoot-Hawley remained part of the trade environment and interacted with foreign retaliation and collapsing import demand. The main mechanism linking macro to micro was balance-sheet distress: falling prices raised real debt burdens, banks cut lending, inventories were liquidated, and consumers postponed purchases of durables.

The banking emergency intensified rather than merely continuing the 1929 market fall. The Federal Reserve counted 2,298 bank suspensions against 1,345 in 1930, with deposits in suspended banks rising from $864.715 million to $1,691.510 million; 276 banks reopened during 1931, so suspensions were not all permanent failures. All-bank loans fell from $38.135 billion to $31.305 billion between end-year dates. The New York discount rate was cut to 1.5% on 8 May and then raised to 2.5% on 9 October and 3.5% on 16 October, combining easing with gold defense. Hoover's intergovernmental-debt proposal was released on 21 June, conditional on congressional and foreign agreement, and excluded private obligations.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:**

Autos, steel, rail equipment, construction, and farm supply chains contracted sharply. Large firms such as General Motors, Ford, US Steel, AT&T, and utilities remained central to the corporate landscape, but 1931 revenue and market-capitalization rankings are Data not available. Farm households faced falling crop prices and debt pressure, while urban households faced unemployment and lower hours. Real estate and mortgage credit were weak. Vehicle manufacture, excluding most assembly plants, fell from 3.356 million to 2.390 million in the League of Nations production series. In the Macrohistory and rate-of-return reconstructions, real consumption per person fell 3.85%, total bank loans 12.55% and nominal wages 7.43%, and the nominal house-price index fell 15.74%. Newspaper asking-price indices fell 6.15% in New York City and 1.60% in Boston, while asking rents fell 8.22% in New York and rose 0.47% in Boston.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The active crises of 1931 were systemic. The Credit-Anstalt crisis opened the acute Central European financial phase; its May 1931 disclosure concerned losses from 1930, and the rescue guarantees did not make its assets sound. The Government Savings Bank of New South Wales suspended payment on 22 April. Germany's Danatbank failure and banking holiday created a combined bank, currency, and political crisis. Britain's exit from gold shocked the reserve-currency system. US bank failures deepened the domestic contraction. Japan's exit from gold marked a different path, one that proved expansionary later.

Near-misses included further pressure on the US dollar, broader gold-bloc instability, and possible disorderly sovereign defaults. Some risks materialized later: the US left gold in 1933, Germany moved further toward autarky and dictatorship, and global trade became more managed and bloc-based. Hindsight also shows that the USSR's agricultural disruption would become a human catastrophe in 1932 and 1933, but that full outcome was not yet visible in standard economic statistics in 1931.

Policy mistakes visible in retrospect include excessive commitment to gold parity, procyclical fiscal tightening, failure to stabilize banking systems quickly, trade protection, and underestimation of debt-deflation. At the time, many officials still viewed balanced budgets, gold convertibility, and wage-price adjustment as credibility-enhancing rather than contractionary.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1931 vs. 1930)

The 1930 to 1931 shift was a regime break. In 1930, the depression was severe but many policymakers still expected orthodox adjustment to work. By the end of 1931, the gold-exchange system had fractured, Central European banking had broken, sterling had left gold, and Japan had begun a reflationary turn. The common-country MPD covered-economy sum fell by about 3.7 percent from 1930 to 1931, but the distribution was uneven. Matched measures confirm the break: world export volume fell 11.77%, US bank suspensions rose from 1,345 to 2,298 and deposits in suspended banks from $864.7 million to $1,691.5 million, US all-bank loans fell from $38.1 billion to $31.3 billion, the German industrial index fell from 92 to 76, and Italy's fiscal balance moved from a surplus of 151 million lire in 1929-30 to a deficit of 504 million in 1930-31.

The GDP ranking changed in economically meaningful ways. The United States stayed first but contracted. China stayed second because of population scale. The former USSR rose from fourth to third as planned industrial growth continued in the estimate. India moved from sixth to fourth as Germany and Britain fell more sharply. Germany fell from third to fifth after a major banking and currency crisis. Britain fell from fifth to sixth but gained later policy flexibility after leaving gold. Switzerland entered the top 20 as Sweden slipped out.

New risks emerged in 1931: exchange controls, competitive devaluation, banking holidays, capital flight, political radicalization, and managed trade. Some risks partly resolved: Britain's gold-defense constraint ended after September, and Japan's late-1931 break with gold set up recovery in 1932. Other risks intensified: US banking weakness, German political instability, commodity-exporter distress, and deflation in gold-bloc countries.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

The top-20 GDP table has medium confidence as a Maddison-style real PPP ranking, but it is not an official 1931 nominal GDP ranking. The method is: MPD 2023 Full data sheet, GDP equals GDP per capita times population, with population in thousands, converted to billions of 2011 international dollars. The ranking was cross-checked against the MPD release documentation, the Dataverse file page, OWID's Maddison GDP documentation, and the MaddisonData R library schema.

High or medium statistical confidence applies to broad output estimates for the United States, United Kingdom, Germany, France, Japan, Italy, Canada, the Netherlands, Belgium, Australia, Switzerland, and Sweden. Even there, unemployment, CPI, debt, current-account, and budget indicators are not always directly comparable to modern statistics.

Lower confidence applies to China, India, the former USSR, Indonesia, Brazil, Poland, Czechoslovakia, Argentina, and other economies where colonial administration, fragmented sovereignty, non-market accounting, informal production, or later border definitions complicate measurement. Soviet and other non-market estimates carry substantial, unquantified uncertainty. The former USSR entry is particularly non-comparable because output was planned and prices were administered.

Historical data conflicts appear in three places. First, nominal current-dollar GDP rankings differ from PPP real rankings. Second, country borders and colonial units differ from modern countries; Indonesia corresponds to the Dutch East Indies, and India corresponds to undivided India, including the princely states. Third, some macro indicators existed in national statistical publications but are not standardized globally for 1931.

Indicator sources and definitions. Policy rates are official discount rates in force at the end of 1931 as compiled by the Federal Reserve Board, not annual averages; India, Canada, Argentina and Brazil had no central bank, and no official policy rate is shown for Australia, so the rates of commercial or state banks appear only in Notes. Exchange rates are Federal Reserve Board calendar-year averages of New York noon buying rates in US cents per foreign unit, which understate the late-year depreciations; the Czechoslovak annual figure printed in the January 1932 Bulletin (29.6186 cents) is a misprint, so the December monthly means are used instead. CPI, unemployment, debt and current-account ratios for most advanced economies come from the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database (Release 6) and equity returns and house prices from its rate-of-return extension; these data are used under the CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 licence. United Kingdom indicators use the Bank of England millennium dataset, and United States CPI and unemployment use the Bureau of Labor Statistics and Lebergott series. Budget balances are Data not available for most economies because fiscal years and coverage are not reconciled with calendar-year nominal GDP.

Source conflicts. United States unemployment is 15.9% in both Lebergott and the Macrohistory Database, but the 1930 values differ (8.9% against 8.7%). United Kingdom unemployment is 14.88% in the Bank of England dataset against 15.1% in the Macrohistory Database, and public debt 180.68% against 184.83% of GDP. Swiss GDP growth is +1.59% in MPD 2023 but -4.17% in the Maddison 2010 series, so even the sign of the Swiss change is uncertain. The Japanese November rate rise is dated 5 November by the Bank of Japan and 4 November by the Federal Reserve Board; the Italian September increase is dated 27 September by the Bank of Italy and 28 September by the Federal Reserve Board. The German standstill is dated 19 August, 1 September or 17 September depending on whether negotiation, coverage or announcement is meant. Italian equity prices fell 31.41% in the Macrohistory reconstruction against 34.99% in the Bachi index. French unemployment in the Macrohistory Database covers only wage earners in manufacturing, mining and construction and is therefore not shown as a national rate. The Macrohistory unemployment entry for Italy is a linked index rather than a rate and is not shown as one. The Belgian external balance in that source is a merchandise trade balance and is not shown as a current account.

GDP growth for the former USSR is withheld because the national-income source behind the MPD record has not been matched to the territory of its population series; the raw record change of +1.98% is shown only in Notes. The 1930 report shows a former-USSR growth rate of +5.81% from the same records, which carries the same limitation. Levels and growth in this report and the 1930 report come from the same MPD 2023 vintage, and the 1931 levels are consistent with the 1930 levels and the stated growth rates.

The weakest claims are sectoral rankings, corporate rankings, cross-country unemployment comparisons, current-account balances, budget balances, and exchange-specific financial-market returns. For those categories, "Data not available" is used unless a sourced series exists. The strongest claims are the MPD-derived GDP ranking, the gold-standard regime changes, the German and Austrian banking crisis sequence, Britain's September 1931 gold suspension, Japan's December 1931 policy turn, and the broad depressionary character of trade, credit, and commodity markets.
