# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1996

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1996 was a year of synchronized but uneven recovery. The world economy regained momentum after the 1995 slowdown, with the United States expanding briskly, Japan growing but still trapped by weak banking and asset-price legacies, Western Europe restrained by fiscal consolidation and high unemployment, and several large emerging economies recovering or sustaining high growth. The World Bank WITS country summaries place world GDP at about $31.872 trillion in current U.S. dollars, and the IMF World Economic Outlook (October 2026) puts world real growth at 3.866% at PPP weights (3.368% at market weights), up from 3.273% in 1995. The top of the nominal ranking was still dominated by the United States, Japan, Germany, France, the United Kingdom and Italy, but China, Brazil, Korea, India, Russia and Mexico were already large enough to shape global demand and trade flows.

The most important surprise was not a crisis, but a deceleration in world trade after the extraordinary trade boom of 1994 and 1995. WTO reporting for 1996 placed world merchandise export volume growth at about 4 percent, with merchandise export value near $5.1 trillion and commercial services export value near $1.2 trillion. This was still solid by early-1990s standards, but it marked a clear slowing from the prior two years. Dollar appreciation, weaker Asian trade growth, sluggish Western European demand and weaker prices for office and telecommunications equipment, steel and non-ferrous metals all mattered. Oil was the major commodity exception, with crude prices rising strongly and lifting fuel export values.

Monetary conditions were generally supportive in the major advanced economies. The Federal Reserve eased early in the year and then held policy broadly steady as growth strengthened. The Bank of Japan kept the official discount rate at 0.5 percent, an extraordinary level for the time, because the banking system and domestic demand remained fragile. The Bundesbank lowered key rates in April, while the United Kingdom cut official rates early in the year but raised its rate by 25 basis points to 6% on 30 October. Financial markets interpreted this mix as favorable for risk assets in the United States and much of Europe. U.S. and European equity markets rose sharply, while Japan's Nikkei declined, reflecting the still unresolved balance-sheet recession.

The year also looks different with hindsight. In 1996, policymakers could see strong capital inflows, rapid credit growth and external deficits in parts of emerging Asia, but the systemic Asian financial crisis had not yet materialized. Korea, Thailand, Indonesia and other economies still looked like high-growth success stories to many observers. Russia's fiscal arrears, ruble-corridor dependence and government-bill financing problems were visible, but the 1998 sovereign and currency crisis was not yet inevitable. Brazil's Real Plan continued to restrain inflation, but it also produced a policy mix of high rates, capital inflows and external vulnerability. 1996 was therefore a late-cycle year in several emerging-market financial structures, even though the shocks would arrive later.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1996

**Theme 1: Trade globalization slowed but did not reverse.** The WTO reported that world merchandise export volume rose about 4 percent in 1996 after two unusually strong years. In value terms, world merchandise exports reached about $5.1 trillion and commercial services exports about $1.2 trillion. The slowdown was analytically important because it showed that the post-Uruguay Round trade boom was not a straight-line acceleration. Dollar appreciation lowered the dollar value of many traded goods, Western Europe's weak demand restrained imports, and Asian trade growth slowed sharply. The countries most affected were Japan, Korea, the European Union economies, Canada and major exporters of electronics or metals. The forward linkage was direct: the world entered 1997 with trade still expanding, but with Asia's trade and financing structure more fragile than headline growth suggested.

**Theme 2: The United States entered a more convincing expansion phase.** The United States combined strong real growth, low unemployment, contained consumer inflation and a powerful equity-market rally. The micro foundations were not just cyclical. Telecommunications deregulation, software investment, falling computer prices, internet commercialization and corporate restructuring fed the idea of a "new economy." Yahoo's IPO, Lucent's separation from AT&T, Apple's agreement to acquire NeXT, and the announced Boeing-McDonnell Douglas merger all reflected sectors in transition. This mattered historically because 1996 helped move productivity and technology from a specialist topic into the center of macro debate. The forward linkage was the late-1990s U.S. boom, the rise of internet valuations and, later, the dot-com crash.

**Theme 3: Western Europe prioritized monetary union over demand stimulus.** European economies faced the Maastricht convergence criteria, high unemployment and muted domestic demand. Germany, France, Italy, Spain, Belgium, the Netherlands and Sweden all had to manage fiscal positions, inflation and exchange-rate credibility with the planned euro in view. Italy re-entered the Exchange Rate Mechanism on 25 November 1996, a symbolic sign of convergence pressure. The policy mix favored disinflation and fiscal restraint, with central bank easing where credibility allowed. The result was lower inflation and stronger credibility, but also social tension and weak labor markets in several countries. The forward linkage was the 1999 euro launch, but also a long debate over whether the convergence process imposed excessive short-run unemployment costs.

**Theme 4: Japan's banking and balance-sheet problems remained unresolved.** Japan's real economy improved from the weakest post-bubble years, but the structure of recovery was fragile. The Bank of Japan kept the official discount rate at 0.5 percent. Equity prices failed to participate in the U.S. and European rally, and banks still faced non-performing loans and weak collateral values. The most affected groups were banks, construction, property-linked firms and households exposed to uncertain employment and wealth conditions. This mattered because Japan was still the world's second-largest economy by nominal GDP and a major creditor economy. The forward linkage was the 1997 consumption tax increase, renewed recessionary pressure and continued banking stress.

**Theme 5: Emerging-market recoveries masked financial vulnerabilities.** Mexico rebounded sharply from the 1995 peso crisis, Brazil's Real Plan continued to reduce very high inflation, China kept near double-digit growth, Korea expanded rapidly, and India grew strongly after earlier liberalization. These successes were real, but the financing mix was often vulnerable. Korea's chaebol leverage and current-account pressure, Brazil's high-interest defense of stabilization, Mexico's still fragile banking system and Russia's fiscal arrears all represented different forms of balance-sheet risk. With hindsight, 1996 was a year when investors still rewarded many emerging-market assets, while underlying risk accumulated.

**Theme 6: Transition economies remained statistically and institutionally difficult to read.** The World Bank's World Development Report 1996 focused on the transition from plan to market. Russia's official GDP still contracted in 1996, inflation remained high and fiscal cash-flow problems were severe. Measurement was difficult because barter, arrears, non-payment, unofficial activity and changing national-account methods distorted the picture. This theme affected Russia most among the top 20 economies, but also mattered across Eastern Europe and former Soviet republics. The forward linkage was the split between relatively successful Central European convergence and the deeper fiscal and banking problems that persisted in Russia and several other post-Soviet economies.

**Theme 7: Commodities created asymmetric effects.** Oil prices rose materially in 1996, supporting fuel exporters and raising costs for importers, while non-oil commodity trends were mixed. Gold averaged roughly $388 per troy ounce in London data, while EIA annual spot prices averaged US$20.64 per barrel for Brent and US$22.12 for WTI. The WTO noted that fuel export values rose more than 10 percent because of higher crude prices, while some traded technology and metals categories slowed from the 1995 boom. The result was a modest terms-of-trade improvement for oil exporters and a less favorable backdrop for some industrial metals and equipment exporters. The forward linkage was that energy remained manageable for advanced-economy inflation in 1996, but commodity cycles were still powerful enough to change regional export income.

Inflation was contained in the advanced economies but remained high in some emerging and transition economies. The United States and most of Western Europe experienced low to moderate consumer inflation. Japan had near-zero inflation. Brazil, Mexico and Russia were still disinflating from much higher inflation regimes, with Russia especially high. China continued to bring inflation down from the mid-1990s surge while maintaining rapid growth.

Key commodities were mixed. EIA benchmark data show annual spot averages of US$20.64 per barrel for Brent and US$22.12 for WTI, and WTO reporting, based on IMF spot prices, states that crude oil prices rose about 19 percent in 1996. Gold averaged about $387.87 per troy ounce in London market data. IMF commodity price series show copper falling 21.8% to US$2,293.4 per tonne, while wheat rose 11.5% to US$197.2, maize 33.3% to US$164.5 and Thai rice 5.4% to US$338.1 per tonne (see 3D).

International capital flows moved mainly from advanced economies toward emerging markets and transition economies, while the United States continued to attract portfolio capital into equities and bonds. UNCTAD and WTO-linked reporting show that foreign direct investment had become a major structural force by the mid-1990s, with global FDI stocks and flows much larger than in the 1970s. The direction of capital flows was historically important because it contributed to both productive investment and financial vulnerability in economies with weak banking supervision or currency mismatches.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The U.S. Federal Reserve lowered the intended federal funds rate on 31 January 1996 from 5.50 percent to about 5.25 percent, alongside a separate discount-rate reduction to 5 percent, and then generally held policy steady as growth improved. The stance was not crisis-easy, but it was supportive enough for equity markets and credit conditions. U.S. financial conditions were helped by low inflation, strong corporate profits and enthusiasm about technology and communications sectors.

The Bank of Japan maintained the official discount rate at 0.5 percent. This was an extraordinary stance for a major advanced economy in the 1990s and reflected weak credit demand, banking-sector stress and continued property-price damage from the earlier asset bubble. Japan's low-rate environment did not produce the same equity-market response as in the United States because private balance sheets and bank asset quality remained impaired; with impaired collateral and banks, a low discount rate did not guarantee vigorous credit demand.

The Bundesbank cut the discount and Lombard rates by 50 basis points in April 1996 (announced 18 April, effective 19 April), to 2.50 percent and 4.50 percent respectively, while keeping repo operations central to liquidity management. The easing helped Europe, but the overall policy setting was constrained by the need to maintain exchange-rate credibility ahead of monetary union. The Bank of England cut official rates twice in the first quarter, according to its quarterly bulletin, as U.K. growth and inflation conditions allowed some easing, but raised its rate by 25 basis points to 6% on 30 October. The European Monetary Institute's chronology records the Banque de France's intervention-rate cut to 3.15% (decided 17 December, effective 20 December), Spain's official 10-day repurchase rate at 6.25% after a 13 December reduction, and Sweden's repo rate cut to 4.1% on 17 December. The Reserve Bank of Australia lowered its cash-rate objective to about 7% on 31 July, 6.5% on 6 November and 6% on 11 December.

Exchange-rate regimes were diverse. The United States, Japan, Canada, Australia and the United Kingdom operated floating currencies. Much of continental Europe operated inside the Exchange Rate Mechanism or close to it, with EMU convergence as a central policy anchor. China kept a tightly managed exchange-rate regime following the 1994 exchange-rate unification. Mexico had moved to a float after the 1994 peso crisis. Brazil managed the real under the Real Plan. Russia operated a ruble corridor, but fiscal weakness undermined credibility.

Equity markets diverged. The S&P 500 delivered a strong positive price return, German and French equities rose sharply, and the DAX and CAC 40 both had strong years in compiled market-return sources. Japan's Nikkei 225 ended 1996 lower than it began, with an annual decline of about 2.55 percent. The contrast captured the macro divergence: U.S. and European investors rewarded disinflation, restructuring and eventual EMU, while Japanese investors remained concerned about banks and domestic demand.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The WTO's early years shaped the rules environment. The Uruguay Round had recently expanded multilateral disciplines, and the 1996 Singapore Ministerial began debates on investment, competition policy, transparency in government procurement and trade facilitation. NAFTA continued to deepen North American integration, with WTO reporting that NAFTA intra-regional exports grew slightly more than 10 percent in 1996. MERCOSUR trade was also dynamic, although Brazil's import growth slowed from its earlier surge. Turkey's customs union with the European Union entered operation on 1 January 1996, covering industrial goods and processed-agriculture components rather than all services and farming; a coal-and-steel agreement signed on 26 July entered force on 1 August with exceptions and transition schedules. Indonesia's February and June national-car measures, which conditioned tax and tariff advantages on local content and favored production arrangements, generated WTO disputes.

European integration was a central economic event, not only a political project. Fiscal targets, exchange-rate discipline and convergence of inflation and bond yields influenced policy across Germany, France, Italy, Spain, Belgium, the Netherlands and Sweden. Italy's return to the Exchange Rate Mechanism on 25 November and the broader commitment to the future euro affected capital markets, interest rates, privatization and fiscal choices.

Geopolitical constraints also shaped economic flows. The Iraq oil-for-food framework began to affect limited oil-market supply and humanitarian trade channels. U.S.-China trade tensions existed but did not stop rapid Chinese export growth. Cross-strait tension affected Taiwan's political risk perception, while Russia's post-Soviet transition remained tied to international financial support and investor confidence. ### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil was the most important commodity story of 1996. EIA annual spot averages were US$20.64 per barrel for Brent and US$22.12 for WTI, different grades and locations, and prices rose materially during the year, and WTO reporting attributed a more than 10 percent rise in fuel export values to the crude-price increase. This supported oil exporters and resource-linked equity sectors, while creating a mild cost headwind for importers.

Metals and equipment-related trade were weaker. WTO reporting identified office and telecommunications equipment, iron and steel, and non-ferrous metals as categories where export-value growth slowed sharply after exceptionally strong 1995 conditions. IMF benchmark prices show copper averaging US$2,293.4 per tonne, down 21.8% from 1995.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor-market performance diverged sharply. The United States combined rapid job growth with unemployment slightly above 5 percent. Japan's unemployment was low by Western standards but high relative to Japan's postwar experience. Continental Europe had much higher unemployment, especially Spain and France, reflecting weak demand, labor-market rigidities, restructuring and fiscal restraint. Unemployment data are not directly comparable across countries because survey methods, registration systems and informal-sector coverage differ; the country tables use ILO modeled estimates, which are not twenty harmonized national surveys, and urban registered unemployment, labor-force surveys and informal livelihoods remain different concepts.

Demographics mattered in different ways. Japan and several European countries were already entering more visible aging pressure. China and India had large working-age populations and continued urbanization. China saw large-scale rural-to-urban migration feeding export manufacturing and construction. Mexico's labor market was reshaped by NAFTA-linked manufacturing and migration links to the United States. Russia faced demographic stress alongside economic contraction and social disruption. **Economic narrative:**

The U.S. economy was the central growth engine among advanced economies. Private consumption, business investment and technology spending supported strong real growth, while unemployment remained low and inflation stayed moderate. The Federal Reserve's early easing, followed by policy stability, reinforced a favorable financial backdrop. Equity markets were buoyant and the technology, telecom and media sectors began to command much more investor attention.

Microeconomic change was unusually important. The Telecommunications Act of 1996 represented a major communications-law overhaul. Lucent's IPO and separation from AT&T, Yahoo's IPO, Apple's agreement to acquire NeXT, and the announced Boeing-McDonnell Douglas merger all showed how telecom, internet, software and aerospace were being reorganized. Household consumption was supported by employment and wealth gains, but this also laid the foundation for later equity-valuation excess.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Technology and communications were the most dynamic sectors; aerospace consolidated; autos remained large but less central to growth; finance benefited from capital-market activity; housing was healthy but not the defining macro risk. Banking-sector health was broadly stronger than in the late-1980s and early-1990s stress period. The Macrohistory Database records a U.S. equity price gain of 20.9% (total return 23.4%) and a bond total return of -1.3%; Philadelphia Fed historical asking-price estimates show house prices up 1.8% in Boston and 0.7% in Chicago but down 3.2% in Los Angeles.

### 5B. Corporate landscape

The country examples in the sector matrix are illustrative, not a verified ranking of sector performance, and a ranking of the top global companies by revenue or market capitalization for 1996 is Data not available. Available historical corporate evidence indicates that the mid-1990s global corporate landscape was dominated by U.S. automakers and oil majors, Japanese trading houses and manufacturers, European energy and telecom firms, and financial institutions. No ranked list is given.

Major corporate events were clear. Deutsche Telekom's IPO on 18 November 1996 was one of the largest privatizations in European history and a milestone for continental capital markets; the company had become a stock corporation in January 1995, and the IPO gave it access to equity finance for network modernization and international expansion, while federal-budget proceeds came only with the third issue in 2000. Lucent's IPO and subsequent AT&T distribution created a major independent telecommunications-equipment firm. Yahoo's IPO showed that public markets were beginning to value internet business models aggressively. Apple's agreement to acquire NeXT returned Steve Jobs to Apple's orbit and created the software foundation that later became central to Apple's operating-system strategy. Boeing's announced acquisition of McDonnell Douglas signaled large-scale aerospace consolidation, completed in 1997. In pharmaceuticals, the Ciba-Geigy and Sandoz combination that created Novartis was announced on 7 March 1996; announcement, shareholder approval and operational combination were separate steps, and 1996 restructuring accruals totalled CHF 4,126 million.

Privatization and deregulation were especially important mechanisms. Telecom privatization, bank restructuring and utilities reform affected Europe and emerging markets. In Brazil and Mexico, privatization and financial repair were linked to stabilization. In Russia, privatization had already created concentrated ownership but weak governance, which made microeconomic incentives difficult to interpret.

### 5C. Innovation & technology

Internet commercialization accelerated in 1996. The macro importance was still small relative to total GDP, but the capital-market signal was large. Yahoo's IPO, the rise of web portals, enterprise software investment and falling computer prices all contributed to technology optimism. Telecommunications deregulation in the United States and telecom privatization in Europe supported network investment and competition.

Semiconductors were more cyclical. Korea and Taiwan were major beneficiaries of the global electronics supply chain, but the semiconductor cycle weakened in 1996 and contributed to pressure on Korean corporates. Japan remained technologically deep but financially constrained. Sweden's telecom-equipment industry, led by Ericsson, was globally important as mobile networks expanded.

Biotechnology and healthcare innovation continued, with Switzerland, the United States, Germany and the United Kingdom important in pharmaceuticals and medical research. Infrastructure megaprojects and construction were most visible in China and parts of Asia, while European infrastructure investment was shaped by EU integration and fiscal constraints. ### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

The main labor-market pattern was divergence. The United States had low unemployment and strong job creation. Western Europe had low inflation but high unemployment. Spain and France were especially challenged. Japan's unemployment was low internationally but high relative to its own social norms. Sweden was recovering from an earlier crisis but still had high unemployment for its model.

Skill demand shifted toward technology, finance, business services and export manufacturing. The United States benefited from software and telecom-related skill formation. India began to build the software-services base that would become much more important later. China absorbed rural labor into urban manufacturing and construction. Mexico's labor demand shifted toward NAFTA-linked manufacturing.

Unions and labor movements mattered most in Europe, where fiscal consolidation, privatization and labor-market reform were politically contested. Wage moderation was a strength in the Netherlands and parts of Germany, but high unemployment created persistent social costs.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The most important systemic risk visible with hindsight was emerging Asia's financial fragility. Korea's corporate leverage, semiconductor pressure and short-term foreign borrowing were significant. Thailand, Indonesia and other economies outside the top 20 also had currency and banking vulnerabilities; Thailand's WDI current-account deficit was 8.027% of GDP, and the BIS described weak exports, property and stock-price declines and fragile finance there. In 1996, many of these risks were recognized in fragments, but the scale of the 1997 crisis was not yet obvious.

Japan's unresolved banking problem was an active macro risk. Low rates helped, but they did not force rapid balance-sheet repair. The risk was not a sudden panic in 1996, but prolonged stagnation and renewed recession if fiscal support faded or taxes rose. That risk materialized more clearly in 1997 and 1998.

Russia was another active vulnerability. Output was still contracting, inflation was high, fiscal arrears were serious and the government increasingly depended on fragile financing structures. With hindsight, this was part of the path toward the 1998 crisis, but the timing and exact trigger were not knowable in 1996.

European convergence carried political and social risks. Fiscal consolidation and high unemployment created social tension, while markets increasingly assumed that the euro would proceed. A failure of convergence would have produced major currency and bond-market disruption. That risk did not materialize, but the social cost debate remained.

The BSE crisis in the United Kingdom and Europe was a sectoral shock with broader trade and regulatory implications. It affected beef markets, consumer confidence in food safety, and European policy coordination. It is an example of a non-financial shock that can create measurable economic consequences through regulation, trade restrictions and consumer behavior.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1996 vs. 1995)

Global growth improved from the 1995 slowdown, with IMF WEO world real growth rising from 3.273% to 3.866% at PPP weights and world CPI inflation falling from 16.192% to 9.507%, but trade growth slowed sharply from the exceptional 1995 pace; IMF goods-and-services trade volume growth slowed from 9.547% to 6.481%. WTO reporting showed world merchandise export-value growth decelerating from nearly 20 percent in 1995 to about 4 percent in 1996. Volume growth also slowed, especially in Asia and Western Europe, although trade still grew faster than output in several regions.

The United States strengthened, while Japan remained fragile. U.S. labor markets improved and equities rallied. Japan's low-rate policy continued, but banking and property problems limited the recovery. Western Europe moved further into euro-convergence mode, with lower inflation and fiscal restraint but persistent unemployment.

Emerging-market conditions were more favorable than in 1995, especially in Mexico, which rebounded strongly after the peso crisis. Brazil's Real Plan continued to anchor disinflation. China and India remained fast-growing large economies. Russia remained the clearest large-economy contraction among the top 20.

Commodity conditions changed materially because oil prices rose. Fuel-export values increased, while several non-oil traded goods categories slowed from their 1995 strength. Gold was relatively stable compared with the stronger moves in equities and oil.

New risks that emerged or became clearer included Asian corporate and banking leverage, Brazil's stabilization-related external vulnerability, Russia's fiscal-financing fragility, and technology-equity valuation enthusiasm. Risks that partly resolved included the acute phase of Mexico's 1995 crisis and some European currency uncertainty as EMU credibility improved.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

Data confidence is highest for nominal GDP rankings drawn from World Bank WITS for countries included in that system. The main ranking uncertainty is Taiwan, which is economically significant but not always included in World Bank country lists. Including Taiwan places it in the top 20; excluding it brings Argentina into the list. This report treats Taiwan as an economy and explicitly flags the definitional issue.

Data confidence is high for broad macro narratives in the United States, Japan, Canada, Australia and Western Europe because primary central-bank, OECD, World Bank, IMF and WTO sources are available. Confidence is moderate for China, India, Brazil, Mexico and Korea because official series exist but informal activity, financial-sector detail or definitional issues are more important. Confidence is lowest for Russia among the top 20 because transition accounting, barter, arrears and informal activity were large. Dated policy-rate actions from the Federal Reserve, Bank of Japan, Bundesbank, Bank of England, Reserve Bank of Australia and the European Monetary Institute chronology are high-confidence observations; the Maddison real growth rates are long-run reconstructions, and the IMF WEO fiscal and world series are retrospective estimates in current membership and aggregation conventions, not information published in 1996.

**Vintage and unit differences.** The GDP levels, populations and per-capita figures in the ranking are the WITS current-dollar values and rounded population estimates; they are not rebased. The growth column uses the Maddison Project Database 2023 real series, whose levels are in constant 2011 international dollars and are not comparable with the current-dollar ranking, which is why the Maddison PPP cohort is shown only as a separate comparison in section 4. The world GDP level in the text (US$31.872 trillion) is the WITS world summary; the current WDI vintage gives US$32.065 trillion and the IMF WEO US$32,355.0 billion. The rounded growth, inflation and unemployment estimates for Switzerland, Sweden and Belgium, and Taiwan's rounded inflation and unemployment, come from the earlier WDI/OECD/IMF-type compilation and are not on the Maddison basis.

**Material conflicts.** Real growth differs materially across series for China (Maddison 6.603% against 9.968% in WEO, 9.973% in WDI and the National Bureau of Statistics' preliminary 9.7%), Russia (Maddison -1.389% against WDI -3.755%, WEO -3.581% and the BIS partly estimated -6%), Mexico (Banco de México's original 5.1% against WDI 6.218% and Maddison 5.973%), Brazil (Maddison 3.508% against WDI 2.209% and the BIS 2.9%), Germany (Maddison 1.864% against about 1.04% in WEO and WDI) and the Netherlands (Maddison 4.164% against about 3.42%). Turkey's provisional national-accounts growth of 7.4% differs from the WEO (7.007%) and Maddison (8.483%) values, and Korea's contemporary BIS growth and current-account estimates (about 7% and -4.5% of GDP) differ from the current WDI values. These are revision and reconstruction contrasts, not arithmetic errors, and are not averaged. Mexico's annual-average CPI (34.4%) and its December-to-December INPC inflation (27.7%) are different measures, not a contradiction. India's BIS price entry (5.9%) refers to wholesale prices, not CPI. The Nikkei 225's decline of about 2.55% and the Macrohistory Database Japanese equity composite's 16.1% price gain measure different things. Agreement between WDI and IMF series is not independent corroboration, since both draw on national sources.

Unemployment rates are not directly comparable. The tables use ILO modeled estimates, which are not directly observed national surveys; advanced-economy survey measures, registered unemployment systems, official urban unemployment in China, open unemployment in Mexico and labor-market conditions in India's informal economy measure different things. Fiscal data are IMF WEO general-government series with source-specific perimeters: India's growth and fiscal ratios follow an April-March fiscal year and cover central and state government, Thailand's fiscal year runs October-September, and Mexico's perimeter includes public corporations. Debt ratios are stocks divided by source annual GDP, never Maddison PPP output. WDI exchange rates for Russia and Turkey are in later redenominated units and are not shown as 1996 quotations.
