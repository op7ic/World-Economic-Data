# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 2001

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

2001 was defined by a synchronized slowdown that exposed the fragility of the late-1990s technology, telecom, credit and equity-market boom. The United States entered recession after the long expansion that began in 1991; Japan remained trapped in deflation and banking weakness; continental Europe slowed sharply; and Mexico, Canada, Korea and much of export-oriented Asia absorbed the shock from weaker US demand and the collapse in the electronics cycle. The terrorist attacks of September 11 intensified financial-market stress and hurt aviation, tourism, insurance and confidence, but the downturn had already begun: the Federal Reserve's July 2001 Monetary Policy Report already described weak business investment and financing difficulties. The more durable macro story was the end of the 1990s investment boom and the repricing of technology and telecom assets.

The NBER announced on 26 November 2001 that US activity had peaked in March 2001. The November 2001 trough was only determined in July 2003, so the short eight-month recession is a retrospective description; firms and policymakers at the end of 2001 did not know when the weakness would end. Annual GDP can rise while an intra-year recession occurs, which is why US real growth for the year was still slightly positive at about 1.0 percent.

Policy responses were unusually aggressive. The US Federal Reserve cut the federal funds target from 6.5 percent in late 2000 to 1.75 percent by December 2001. The European Central Bank cut rates more cautiously, ending the year with the main refinancing (minimum bid) rate at 3.25 percent. The Bank of England reduced its repo rate to 4.0 percent in November. The Bank of Japan shifted in March to an early form of quantitative easing by targeting current-account balances at the central bank rather than the overnight call rate. Easing was not universal: the Bank of Thailand raised its 14-day repurchase rate in June before cutting it in December. Fiscal policy also turned easier in the United States and several other economies through tax cuts, emergency spending, banking support or countercyclical stabilizers.

The other defining structural event was China's accession to the World Trade Organization on 11 December 2001. In the narrow accounting of 2001, China was already one of the largest nominal-GDP economies and continued to grow rapidly while most advanced economies slowed. In economic history, however, WTO accession mattered more as a forward linkage: it accelerated supply-chain integration, reinforced foreign direct investment into Chinese manufacturing, and reshaped global trade, corporate sourcing, wage bargaining and industrial policy debates through the rest of the decade. Separate crisis sequences ran in Argentina, where December withdrawal restrictions and a default announcement preceded the end of convertibility in January 2002, and in Turkey, where the lira was floated in February and several banks were resolved.

The top-20 nominal GDP ranking for 2001, using World Bank WDI current-US-dollar GDP series cross-checked against IMF WEO definitions, was still dominated by the United States, Japan and Western Europe, but with China already nearly level with France in current dollars and ahead of Italy. Exchange rates mattered heavily: dollar strength, euro weakness, the Argentine currency board and depreciations in Brazil, Turkey and Australia changed rankings even when real output dynamics pointed in other directions. The ranking is therefore a nominal, market-exchange-rate ranking, not a measure of purchasing power or welfare. On the Maddison Project Database 2023 constant-2011-international-dollar measure, China ranked second, India fourth and Indonesia, Turkey, Iran and Thailand entered the top twenty.

Microeconomically, 2001 was a year of forced balance-sheet adjustment. Telecom equipment, internet firms, airlines and energy trading were hit hard; households remained comparatively resilient in some economies because of lower interest rates and housing support; banks and insurers faced credit, market and event-loss pressure; and technology shifted from speculative internet infrastructure toward devices, software platforms and mobile data. Enron's collapse, the blocked GE-Honeywell merger, the launch of Apple's iPod, Microsoft Windows XP and NTT DoCoMo's commercial 3G (FOMA) service in Japan signaled that the corporate landscape was being reset rather than simply shrinking.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 2001

**1. The post-dot-com investment bust became a macro recession.** The fall in equity valuations after 2000 moved from financial markets into capital expenditure, inventories, corporate profits and employment. The most affected economies were the United States, Japan, Germany, Canada, Mexico, Korea, Taiwan and Singapore, because technology hardware, semiconductors, telecom equipment and North American trade were central to the slowdown. In the Federal Reserve's February 2002 estimates, US real spending on equipment and software fell 8.5 percent in 2001 and communications equipment more than 30 percent. The forward linkage was the early-2000s productivity debate: some analysts argued the late-1990s productivity acceleration remained real, while others stressed that overinvestment in telecom networks, internet firms and enterprise technology had pulled demand forward.

**2. Monetary policy shifted from anti-inflation to recession insurance.** Inflation pressure faded as commodity prices weakened and output gaps widened. The Federal Reserve moved fastest; the ECB and Bank of England also eased; the Bank of Japan adopted quantitative easing as deflation persisted. Instruments differed: euro-area members shared one refinancing rate, Japan changed its operating target to a quantity of reserves, Mexico retained its corto framework, and emerging-market crisis defenses were not equivalent to advanced-economy recession insurance. The significance is that 2001 helped normalize the idea that central banks would respond rapidly to market dysfunction and demand shocks. With hindsight, the low-rate environment after 2001 also contributed to debates over housing leverage and risk pricing in later years.

**3. China entered the WTO and global supply chains entered a new phase.** WTO accession did not instantly transform output in December 2001, but it created a stronger legal and institutional framework for multinational sourcing, export platforms, foreign direct investment and tariff commitments. China, East Asian suppliers, commodity exporters and consumer markets in North America and Europe were most affected. Thailand and Indonesia were still managing post-crisis bank and corporate balance sheets at the same time. The forward linkage was profound: the 2000s trade boom, the reallocation of manufacturing, rising Chinese current-account surpluses later in the decade, and political debates over import competition all trace partly to this inflection.

**4. The United States remained the cyclical center of the world economy.** The US recession, September 11 shock, technology correction and policy easing transmitted through trade, capital markets and confidence. Mexico and Canada slowed through NAFTA channels; East Asian export producers suffered from weaker electronics demand; Europe faced reduced external demand and falling business confidence. Financial infrastructure proved resilient: Fedwire and CHIPS kept operating on 11 September and afterward, the discount window remained open, and the major US stock exchanges reopened on 17 September after four lost trading days. The forward linkage was a more security-centered fiscal and geopolitical environment, with defense and homeland-security spending rising while airlines and tourism underwent restructuring.

**5. Argentina's convertibility regime reached its breaking point.** Argentina's recession deepened, bank deposits came under stress, cash-withdrawal restrictions were imposed on 1 December, a default was announced on 23 December, and the fixed one-peso-to-one-dollar regime was abandoned in early January 2002. The immediate country impact was severe: unemployment, sovereign risk, banking restrictions and political instability rose sharply. Turkey's February currency and banking crisis was a parallel case of exchange-rate commitments, bank funding and sovereign credit under strain. The broader significance was a warning about hard pegs, foreign-currency liabilities, fiscal credibility and the social cost of adjustment when nominal flexibility is absent.

**6. Corporate governance and accounting risk moved to the center of market analysis.** Enron's November restatement and December bankruptcy changed investor perception of earnings quality, off-balance-sheet vehicles, auditor incentives and energy trading business models. Although the largest wave of legal and regulatory consequences came in 2002, the economic theme began in 2001: capital markets no longer treated complex corporate structures and pro forma earnings as technical details.

**7. Commodities reflected weak global demand, not a supply boom.** Oil prices fell from 2000 levels as the world economy slowed: in IMF primary-commodity data, Dated Brent averaged US$24.71 per barrel, down 14.32 percent, and LME copper fell 12.92 percent. OPEC discussed output restraint, but weakening demand dominated. Gold was low by later standards, but received some safe-haven support after September. Some agricultural prices, such as US hard red winter wheat and maize, rose modestly. Commodity exporters such as Russia and Canada were affected differently: Russia still benefited from post-1998 competitiveness and earlier oil gains, while Canada faced US-linked industrial weakness.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

World nominal GDP in current US dollars was about USD 33.69 trillion in World Bank WDI data. The IMF World Economic Outlook database of October 2026 gives a world nominal GDP of US$34,085.3 billion and current-PPP GDP of 52,576.3 billion international dollars. Its PPP-weighted world real growth is 2.484 percent, down from 4.799 percent in 2000; market-exchange-rate-weighted growth is 1.700 percent, down from 4.306 percent. World CPI inflation on the IMF aggregation was 4.684 percent. These are later retrospective estimates rather than contemporaneous releases, which described global growth around the low-to-mid 2 percent range depending on aggregation method. The US dollar remained the principal reserve and invoicing currency; the euro was still in its pre-cash phase until notes and coins arrived in January 2002 and traded below parity for much of 2001. Inflation was generally subdued in advanced economies, with outright deflation in Japan and high but falling inflation in some emerging markets.

Trade was hit harder than GDP. The IMF's retrospective goods-and-services trade volume grew only 0.580 percent. The WTO's 2 May 2002 release put the fall in merchandise trade volume at 1 percent in its opening statement and 1.5 percent later in the text; merchandise export value fell 4 percent to about US$6 trillion and commercial-services exports fell 1.5 percent in value. Merchandise-only volume, goods-and-services volume and current-dollar values measure different things and are not contradictory.

Commodity prices were mostly cyclical. The World Bank's commodity review reported falling crude prices late in the year as demand weakened and OPEC struggled to coordinate supply restraint. Average oil prices were below 2000 levels; copper was weak with industrial production; gold traded at historically low levels by later-decade standards, near the high USD 200s per troy ounce. Agricultural staple conditions varied by crop and region, so the benchmark prices in section 3D are crop-specific rather than a single global food narrative.

Capital flows favored liquidity and safety. US Treasuries, highly rated sovereign debt and cash instruments benefited after September, while emerging-market risk was uneven. China continued to attract foreign direct investment linked to manufacturing and WTO accession expectations: China's National Bureau of Statistics reported US$46.8 billion of actually utilized FDI in 2001 and year-end foreign-exchange reserves of US$212.2 billion. Argentina faced capital flight and market exclusion; Turkey, outside the top 20 by nominal GDP in 2001, was in crisis and under IMF-supported stabilization. Russia benefited from recovery after its 1998 crisis and from commodity revenue, but still carried transition-economy statistical and institutional risks. ### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The United States entered the year with a high policy-rate setting relative to the weakening economy. The Federal Reserve cut repeatedly. On 17 September it cut the federal funds target to 3 percent and the discount rate to 2.5 percent, warning that the effective funds rate could temporarily trade below target while liquidity was supplied; on 11 December it set the target at 1.75 percent and the discount rate at 1.25 percent. The ECB eased less aggressively: its 8 November decision set the minimum bid rate on main refinancing operations at 3.25 percent from the operation settled on 14 November, with the deposit and marginal lending facility rates at 2.25 and 4.25 percent from 9 November. The Bank of England's repo rate was cut by 50 basis points to 4.0 percent on 8 November. Japan's overnight call rate was effectively near zero, and the Bank of Japan's 19 March 2001 decision replaced the call-rate operating target with a target of about JPY 5 trillion of current-account balances (against about JPY 4 trillion in February), committing to continue until core CPI inflation became stably zero or positive. The Bank of Thailand's 14-day repurchase rate rose from 1.5 to 2.5 percent on 8 June and was cut to 2.25 percent on 25 December, a reminder that easing was not uniform.

Exchange rates reflected the dollar's cyclical and safe-haven strength. The yen weakened during the year, to an annual average of JPY 121.5 per US dollar in WDI official data. The euro remained below parity for much of the year, averaging EUR 1.117 per dollar (about USD 0.90 per euro). The Argentine peso was still officially fixed at one-to-one with the US dollar in 2001, but the peg had lost credibility by year-end. The Chinese renminbi was effectively stable at an average of CNY 8.277 per US dollar. Turkey floated the lira on 22 February. Many emerging-market currencies were under pressure where external financing was fragile.

Equity markets were broadly negative. US technology shares continued to fall, and broad US indices declined for a second consecutive year. European indices weakened as telecom and bank shares repriced. Japan's equity market remained under pressure from deflation and banking concerns. Bond markets rallied during episodes of risk aversion, although yield curves steepened in some economies as policy rates fell faster than long rates.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

The most important trade event was China's WTO accession on 11 December 2001. In North America, NAFTA remained the dominant trade framework, but the downturn in US manufacturing hurt Mexico and Canada. The euro area was preparing for euro cash introduction in January 2002, which mattered for payments, retail pricing and cross-border market integration. September 11 changed aviation security, insurance, logistics and defense-related procurement and added costs to cross-border transport. Sanctions and conflict-related disruptions were not the main macro driver in 2001, but geopolitical risk premia rose after September. Bilateral shipment and sector-content data for NAFTA transmission: Data not available.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor markets worsened in cyclical sectors, with layoffs and reduced hiring the main transmission mechanisms in industrial economies. US unemployment rose from the very low levels of 2000 to an annual average of 4.7 percent. Japan's unemployment reached a historically high 5.0 percent. Europe had structurally higher unemployment but uneven country outcomes: the UK and Netherlands were relatively resilient, while Germany and parts of the euro area slowed. China and India still had large agricultural and informal labor shares, so standard unemployment rates captured only a narrow part of labor-market stress; China's 3.6 percent urban registered rate is not a nationwide labor-force-survey measure. Survey, registered and modeled unemployment rates are not interchangeable, and Indian fiscal-year data complicate comparisons further. Urbanization, demographic aging in Japan and Europe, and large youth cohorts in India and parts of Latin America remained structural forces. **Economic narrative:**

The US economy moved from late-cycle expansion to recession. The downturn was led by falling business investment, technology retrenchment, inventory correction and weaker profits. September 11 added a discrete shock to confidence, aviation, insurance, tourism and security costs, but it did not create the entire recession; the Federal Reserve's 17 September statement itself acknowledged weakness before the attacks. Policy turned strongly countercyclical through monetary easing, tax measures and emergency spending.

The federal budget remained in surplus for fiscal year 2001, but the surplus fell from US$236.2 billion in FY2000 to US$128.2 billion (receipts US$1,991.4 billion, outlays US$1,863.2 billion, per OMB historical tables), with an on-budget deficit of US$32.4 billion offset by an off-budget surplus of US$160.7 billion. **Micro/sectoral highlights:** Technology hardware, telecom equipment, internet advertising and venture-backed firms contracted sharply; in the Federal Reserve's February 2002 estimates, real business spending on equipment and software fell 8.5 percent, communications equipment more than 30 percent, computers and peripherals 9 percent and software 2.5 percent. Airlines and travel suffered after September. Housing and consumer credit were supported by lower interest rates, refinancing and vehicle-finance incentives: single-family housing starts were about 1.27 million and the constant-quality new-home price index rose about 1.5 percent. Enron's bankruptcy changed the market's view of energy trading, earnings quality and auditor risk. Microsoft released Windows XP, while Apple introduced the iPod, signaling that consumer technology remained innovative even as the broader tech bubble deflated.

**Economic narrative:**

Japan remained the main advanced-economy deflation case. Banking weakness, bad loans, weak private investment and falling prices restrained recovery. NTT DoCoMo began commercial FOMA third-generation mobile service in October 2001, a product milestone even as telecom investment overall slumped.

### 5B. Corporate landscape

The largest companies by revenue still included oil majors, automakers, retailers and diversified industrial firms; a complete, consistently measured top-company revenue or market-capitalization ranking for 2001 is Data not available, and Enron's revenue-recognition practices show why unqualified sales rankings for that year are hazardous. The year's corporate story was less about revenue leadership and more about balance-sheet credibility.

Enron's bankruptcy revealed accounting and governance vulnerabilities. Its Form 8-K filed on 8 November 2001 restated 1997-2000 net income from an initially reported total of US$2,680 million to US$2,089 million, a reduction of US$591 million, and separately identified a US$1.2 billion reduction in shareholders' equity. The figures were unaudited, and neither measures cash losses or the eventual creditor loss. A second 8-K filed on 17 December reports Chapter 11 petitions filed on 2 December, with further subsidiaries filing on 3-6 December, and distinguishes a conditional US$1.5 billion debtor-in-possession commitment from interim court-authorized borrowing of up to US$250 million.

The European Commission's prohibition of GE's proposed Honeywell acquisition showed that EU competition policy could block a major US-centered merger. HP announced its plan to acquire Compaq, a sign of consolidation in hardware. Swissair and Sabena failures showed how aviation weakness, leverage and post-September demand shocks interacted. Accenture's 2001 public listing showed that consulting and outsourcing business models retained investor interest even during a technology downturn. Announcement, completion, bankruptcy filing and liquidation are distinct events in each of these cases.

### 5C. Innovation & technology

The innovation picture was contradictory. Venture funding and public-market valuations collapsed, and in the Federal Reserve's February 2002 estimates US real business spending on equipment and software fell 8.5 percent, with communications equipment down more than 30 percent, computers and peripherals down 9 percent and software down 2.5 percent. Yet important platforms emerged. Apple introduced the iPod in October. Microsoft launched Windows XP. NTT DoCoMo launched commercial FOMA 3G mobile service in Japan in October 2001. Wikipedia began in 2001, initially small but significant in hindsight for digital knowledge production. The initial human genome sequence publications of February 2001 had long-term implications for biotechnology and pharmaceutical research. The economic implication was that the internet and digital technology thesis survived, while many firms and capital structures did not.

### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Skill-biased demand did not disappear, but hiring slowed in software, telecoms and finance. Manufacturing layoffs rose in the United States and Europe. Informal labor absorbed shocks in Mexico, India, Brazil and China, making unemployment rates less comparable than in advanced economies; China's registered urban unemployment, IMF unemployment series and population reconstructions serve different purposes. Most major equity markets declined in 2001. The sector rotation was clear: technology, telecom and media underperformed; defensive sectors, selected consumer staples, health care and high-quality sovereign bonds performed better. Trading was disrupted after September: four scheduled US equity trading days (11-14 September) were lost, and the major exchanges reopened on 17 September. Fedwire and CHIPS continued operating throughout, the Federal Reserve stated on 11 September that the discount window was available, and central-bank liquidity provision and exchange reopening limited systemic market failure.

Government bonds benefited from weaker growth and monetary easing. US 10-year Treasury yields were around 5 percent on average, with a steepening curve as short rates fell quickly. German and UK long yields were in the high-4 percent range. Japanese government bond yields remained very low by international standards, around the low-1 percent range, reflecting deflation and weak nominal growth. Credit spreads widened for lower-quality corporate borrowers, telecom issuers and firms with accounting or liquidity concerns. Argentina's default announcement in December was the main sovereign credit event among large economies.

### 6C. Currencies

The dollar was generally strong. In WDI official annual averages, the euro averaged EUR 1.117 per dollar (units per dollar up 3.1 percent from 2000), the yen JPY 121.5 (up 12.8 percent), sterling GBP 0.695 (up 5.1 percent), the Brazilian real BRL 2.350 (up 28.4 percent, a loss of about 22 percent of its dollar value) and the Australian dollar AUD 1.933 (up 12.1 percent). The euro remained below parity for much of the year before the 2002 cash changeover. The yen weakened as Japan's deflationary problems persisted. The Argentine peso's official one-to-one rate survived most of 2001 but broke immediately after. The Chinese renminbi stayed stable around 8.28 per dollar. Turkey's lira was floated on 22 February; the WDI series for Turkey is expressed in the later redenominated lira, not the million-plus-lira quotations of 2001. Sweden's krona weakened; Australia's dollar was also weak, depressing current-dollar GDP even as the domestic economy was not in deep recession. A rise in local currency units per dollar indicates a weaker local currency; it is not by itself an investor return.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The active crises of 2001 were the US recession and September 11 shock, Japan's deflation/banking trap, Argentina's convertibility collapse, Brazil's energy and currency stress, Turkey's crisis outside the top-20 ranking, the telecom debt overhang, airline-sector distress and Enron's governance collapse. They had different causes and time paths. The near-miss was systemic financial-market failure after September; coordinated liquidity provision and market infrastructure resilience helped prevent that, though liquidity could not eliminate the economic losses.

**Argentina.** The IMF chronology dates cash-withdrawal restrictions to 1 December 2001, with a weekly cash limit of Arg$250 on sight accounts, and the default announcement to 23 December; convertibility was abandoned in early January 2002. The IMF Independent Evaluation Office's 2004 report later criticized aspects of programme diagnosis and crisis management; that is a retrospective assessment rather than the consensus of policymakers in 2001.

**Turkey and emerging Asia.** The Central Bank of Turkey's annual report dates the float of the lira to 22 February, after acute liquidity and bank-payment stress, and records the transfer of Ulusalbank to the Savings Deposit Insurance Fund on 28 February and of Iktisat Bankasi on 15 March. Indonesia's 13 December letter of intent to the IMF reported arrangements to resolve the solvency problems of Unibank and Bank Internasional Indonesia and kept asset recovery by the Indonesian Bank Restructuring Agency, corporate-debt restructuring and state-bank privatization at the center of its programme. Thailand's Asset Management Corporation (TAMC) was restructuring transferred bad loans.

**Hindsight-labeled vulnerabilities.** The low-rate response, while appropriate to recession risk, helped create later debates over housing leverage, search for yield and mortgage credit standards. China's WTO accession, seen in 2001 as a liberalization and integration milestone, later became a central part of trade, labor-market and geopolitical debates. Euro-area divergences were already visible: Spain's domestic-demand boom, Germany's weak demand, Italy's productivity problem and Belgium's debt legacy sat inside one monetary policy framework.

Policy mistakes visible in retrospect include underestimating corporate governance risk in complex listed firms, insufficient stress testing of hard pegs with debt overhangs, and overconfidence in telecom demand forecasts. Not all risks materialized immediately: global banking crisis did not occur in 2001, China did not suffer a WTO-entry shock, and the US recession was short by NBER chronology. Later housing-credit problems, the 2002 accounting legislation and subsequent telecom and airline failures are forward links rather than 2001 events.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (2001 vs. 2000)

The main structural break from 2000 to 2001 was the shift from boom to balance-sheet repair. In 2000, the world economy still reflected late-cycle technology investment, high equity valuations and strong trade growth. In 2001, business investment fell, equity markets declined again, trade nearly stalled, and central banks moved decisively toward easing. The policy regime shifted from inflation vigilance to recession insurance. In the IMF's retrospective data, PPP-weighted world growth slowed by 2.315 percentage points (from 4.799 to 2.484 percent) and market-weighted growth by 2.606 points (from 4.306 to 1.700 percent); the gap reflects different weights. Oil and copper benchmarks fell by 13 to 15 percent while wheat and maize prices rose.

The second delta was the trade-system inflection from China's WTO accession. In 2000, China's entry was still anticipated; in 2001, it became institutional reality. The full economic consequences arrived later, but the forward-looking behavior of firms, provinces, investors and trading partners began to change immediately. Japan changed its monetary operating target in March, Turkey floated in February, and the Federal Reserve's easing accelerated after September.

The third delta was country-specific crisis risk. Argentina moved from chronic stress to collapse. Turkey, not in the top 20 by nominal GDP after depreciation, moved through a major IMF-supported crisis. Brazil faced higher risk premia from domestic electricity shortages and Argentina contagion. Russia continued improving from its 1998 crisis, showing that the post-crisis rebound had become durable.

The fourth delta was fiscal. The US federal unified surplus fell from US$236.2 billion in FY2000 to US$128.2 billion in FY2001, a decline of US$108.0 billion, as recession, tax cuts and security spending took effect. Japan's general government debt rose by 8.3 percentage points of GDP to 126.8 percent, and Brazil's by 5.1 points to 67.3 percent, while strong growth lowered Spain's ratio by 3.8 points.

The fifth delta was corporate confidence. In 2000, the dot-com collapse looked like a valuation problem. In 2001, it became a solvency, debt and governance problem, with telecom defaults, airline distress and Enron. Investors began paying more attention to cash flow, leverage, pensions, off-balance-sheet vehicles and auditor quality. The attacks aggravated the investment downturn rather than providing its single cause.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

GDP ranking confidence is high for the top countries when using World Bank WDI current-dollar series, but the interpretation is narrower than many readers expect. Current-dollar nominal GDP rankings are affected by exchange rates, inflation, statistical revisions and conversion factors. They are not PPP rankings. The France-China ranking is especially close in 2001, so small revisions can affect order. The Korea-Brazil and Sweden-Belgium bands are also sensitive to revisions and exchange rates. The Maddison Project Database 2023 ranking in constant 2011 international dollars produces a different top twenty (adding Indonesia, Turkey, Iran and Thailand); its levels are shown in the country headers only as a comparison and are not mixed with the WDI current-dollar levels. Government ratios use their own nominal denominators and are never computed from the PPP levels.

Country statistical reliability varies. United States, Japan, Canada, Australia, Switzerland and most Western European national accounts are high-confidence. Mexico, Korea and Brazil are moderate-to-high confidence for national accounts, but labor-market informality or survey definitions complicate unemployment comparisons. China, India and Russia have official data, but structural issues are important: China has administrative and local-reporting questions, India has large informal and agricultural sectors and fiscal-year reporting, and Russia's transition economy had informal activity and valuation issues. These are not Soviet-bloc-style gaps, but they do require caution.

Unemployment is the weakest comparable metric in Section 4. Registered unemployment, ILO unemployment, urban unemployment and survey unemployment are not equivalent. China, Mexico, India, Brazil and Russia are especially difficult to compare with the United States, Canada or the UK. Policy-rate comparisons also require caution because China used benchmark and administrative credit tools, Mexico used the corto operating framework, Argentina had a currency-board regime, and Japan was near zero with a quantity target. Policy-rate values for China, Canada, Korea, Brazil, Russia, Switzerland and Sweden are approximate year-end settings rather than dated decision records.

Fiscal indicators have medium confidence. Gross debt, net debt, central-government debt and general-government debt differ materially. Budget balances differ by fiscal year and institutional coverage; IMF series for India map April 2001-March 2002 to 2001. Current-account balances are generally more comparable than unemployment, but revisions and GDP-denominator changes matter. For Switzerland, Argentina, Sweden and Belgium, and for Korean debt and fiscal balance, no IMF WEO values are shown and several indicators are given as approximate values.

**Conflicting data.** Real growth estimates differ materially by source and vintage: China 6.334 percent (MPD), 8.319 percent (IMF WEO) and 7.3 percent (NBS preliminary); Germany 2.752 percent (MPD) against 1.633 percent (IMF); Mexico +0.830 percent (MPD) against -0.451 percent (IMF); Australia 3.154 percent (MPD), 2.584 percent (IMF) and 2.03 percent in an earlier WDI vintage. Thailand's central bank expected growth of about 1.5 percent in February 2002 and the official March 2002 release showed 1.8 percent, against 3.444 percent in the current IMF series. Japan's gross general government debt is 126.8 percent of GDP in the IMF WEO, lower than earlier estimates of 140 percent or more. World nominal GDP is US$33.69 trillion in WDI and US$34,085.3 billion in the IMF WEO. Population levels in the ranking table are WDI-style estimates and differ from MPD populations (for example India 1,071.5 million against 1,036.3 million in MPD). The WTO's May 2002 release gives both -1 and -1.5 percent for merchandise trade volume. The US federal surplus is US$128.2 billion in OMB historical tables and about US$127 billion in the Federal Reserve's February 2002 estimate. Iran's 2001/02 growth was 4.8 percent in the IMF's 2002 Article IV notice but differs materially in later series. Agreement between WDI and IMF series is not independent corroboration, since both draw on national sources.

The weakest evidence in this document is in broad global sector ranking, annual equity-index return approximations, long-bond yield levels, real-estate conditions by city and some micro-level corporate generalizations outside the most documented events. Where exact sector data or market figures could not be verified, the text uses qualitative language or labels data as approximate. Important data gaps for 2001 include globally comparable informal employment, sector-level profit data for many emerging markets, consistent housing-price series across major cities, comparable corporate debt by sector across countries, exact equity total returns, an exact gold benchmark, global employment and migration flows, comparable R&D and patent data, and complete venture-capital valuation data outside the United States and Europe. For agricultural staples, country-level weather and crop data should be used rather than a single global statement.
