# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1987

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1987 was an expansion year with unusually visible financial fragility. World GDP was about US$17.47 trillion current US$ World Bank/FRED. IMF reported continued output and trade growth: industrial-country real output rose more than 3 percent, world trade volume rose about 5.5 percent, and inflation in advanced economies stayed low versus the 1970s and early 1980s. The BIS's preliminary estimate of world merchandise trade volume growth was about 4¾%, which differs in coverage from the IMF's.

The main surprise was the October stock-market crash. On Black Monday, 19 October 1987, the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 22.6 percent in one day, and the shock moved through global markets. It did not cause an immediate global recession or banking collapse, but it changed views of market plumbing, portfolio insurance, program trading, and central-bank liquidity support. The crash was not a world annual return: it was a sequence of market-specific daily and weekly changes, interruptions and financing pressures. The BIS's contemporaneous comparison shows the S&P 500 falling 20.4% on 19 October, while the Korean representative index rose 0.5% over the crash week; different trading calendars, benchmarks, capital controls and settlement arrangements mattered, and neither observation describes the return of a global portfolio. Contemporary officials still regarded the crash's eventual real-economy consequences as uncertain at year-end, and continued expansion is not proof that the crash had no cost.

Exchange-rate management was the other core force. After the post-Plaza dollar depreciation, the February 1987 Louvre Accord attempted to stabilize major currencies. The 22 February Louvre statement contained policy commitments, including a Japanese discount-rate reduction effective the next day, not evidence that every fiscal undertaking was implemented immediately. The dollar subsequently strengthened temporarily, then weakened again. The Federal Reserve raised its basic discount rate in September and provided reserves generously in October without reversing that posted discount-rate increase. The United States still had large fiscal and trade imbalances; Japan and Germany remained surplus economies; markets were sensitive to policy coordination.

Growth was uneven. Korea, China, Spain, Turkey, and parts of the advanced economies grew strongly. Latin America remained constrained by debt and inflation. The Soviet bloc showed weak growth and rising reform pressure. Oil recovered from 1986 lows but stayed far below early-1980s levels, helping importers and straining oil-dependent fiscal systems. Original Chinese reporting gives preliminary GNP growth of 9.4%, which is not the same concept or vintage as Maddison GDP growth of 8.38%. Mexico's central bank reports 159.2% December-to-December inflation and a primary public-sector surplus, neither interchangeable with annual-average CPI or an overall government surplus.

Microeconomic evidence is stronger when corporate transactions are unpacked. NTT's first share sale, listing and opening market price were separate events; JR East's incorporation did not instantly create privately owned equity. Housing finance, consumer durables, margin calls and collateral constraints transmitted macroeconomic conditions to households and firms.

The report cross-checks market-economy nominal GDP against WDI nominal data where available. Soviet-bloc and non-market estimates are low-confidence.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1987

**1. Exchange-rate coordination moved from depreciation to stabilization.** The Louvre Accord followed the Plaza period and showed that policymakers feared excessive dollar weakness. The United States, Japan, Germany, France, the United Kingdom, and Canada were most affected. Louvre's commitment to stability around prevailing exchange levels was conditional on adjustment policies, and the BIS records substantial intervention in both directions during the year; coordination was a process, not a new fixed exchange-rate system. The forward linkage was Japan's domestic easing and the late-1980s asset boom, together with the credibility problem of coordinating domestic policy and external balances.

**2. Black Monday exposed modern market-structure risk.** The crash was not just a fall in prices; it revealed how futures, portfolio insurance, trading rules, and settlement liquidity could transmit stress. Cash equities, derivatives and banks had different trading and payment timetables, closing one market could move selling elsewhere, and institutions that were solvent on a net basis could still need cash before receipts arrived. Fed liquidity helped contain the shock. Later circuit breakers trace to this episode.

**3. Inflation stayed low, but asset inflation became harder to see.** Advanced-economy goods-price inflation was subdued, helped by policy credibility and low oil, although WDI annual-average CPI inflation rose in 1987 in the United States, Germany, France and the United Kingdom. This encouraged confidence, but in Japan especially it hid the growth of financial and property excess.

**4. East Asia gained industrial weight.** Japan remained a giant, Korea grew extremely fast, China continued reform-era expansion, and Asian production networks deepened. Trade frictions, especially in semiconductors, showed that export-led growth was politically contested. Japanese easing and domestic demand, Korea's expansion and Chinese household-income gains occurred under different financial systems; a common "Asian bubble" explanation would conceal major differences in market access, ownership and credit allocation.

**5. Socialist and state-led systems faced reform pressure.** China deepened market-oriented reforms; the USSR tried enterprise autonomy; Poland contracted. With hindsight, these were signs of institutional stress, though systemic collapse was not a settled 1987 expectation.

**6. Debt overhang split developing economies.** The IMF reported an improved aggregate developing-country current account, but growth slowed. Brazil, Mexico, and Argentina remained constrained by external debt, inflation, and limited access to voluntary bank lending. Mexico's external surplus improved while inflation accelerated, and Brazil's suspension of interest payments reflected continuing debt stress; a developing-country aggregate current-account improvement did not establish improved financing for every debtor.

**7. Regional integration became a firm-level planning issue.** The Single European Act entered into force in July 1987, and Canada and the United States agreed to CUSFTA in October. Firms began planning around larger integrated markets before all legal effects were fully implemented. Japanese railway incorporation and telecommunications share sales likewise changed incentives before final ownership outcomes were visible.

**8. The year's expansion did not insure households against financial losses.** The Federal Reserve's 1987 annual report records slower consumer spending, reduced equity wealth after the crash and weaker housing starts. Interest-rate declines could offset part of the shock, but did not retroactively remove balance-sheet losses.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The U.S. Federal Reserve raised the discount rate to 6.0 percent on 4 September 1987, from 5.5 percent, then supplied liquidity after the October crash. The October response involved reserve provision and lower money-market pressures rather than a cut in that administered rate. Long-term U.S. interest rates had risen before the crash; the Federal Reserve's own graph uses a 30-year constant-maturity U.S. government bond yield, not a 10-year yield. Japan's discount rate was cut to 2.5 percent in February: the Bank of Japan's historical event table gives 3% at the end of 1986 and 2.5% from 23 February 1987. The Louvre statement announced that next-day change; it did not make every participant cut the same rate on 22 February. Retrospective BOJ/BIS work treats 1987 as the start of the bubble period. Germany remained central to anti-inflation credibility and exchange-rate coordination.

The BIS describes rising short rates in Europe and Canada during summer and early autumn, followed by easing after the crash; Germany reduced its discount rate in December. The world used managed floating, not Bretton Woods. The Louvre Accord attempted to limit further dollar depreciation. Credit was available to high-grade borrowers, speculative in Japan, and constrained for many indebted developing countries.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Demographics explain much of the difference between Maddison real-output rankings and current-dollar rankings: China and India were much larger in real-output scale than in market-exchange-rate GDP. Population figures in the country headers come from Maddison, not from WDI or UN series. Labor statistics are not directly comparable across market economies, informal economies, and command economies; definitions differ particularly between registered unemployment, urban surveys and command-economy employment, and a low recorded rate does not measure underemployment, shortages or labor hoarding. Employment continued shifting toward services in the United States and United Kingdom, toward export manufacturing in Korea and China, and away from low-productivity agriculture in parts of Asia.

China's household surveys reported nominal urban living-expense income per person of 916 yuan, real growth 1.7%, and rural net income of 463 yuan, real growth 5.3%; the same release said 21% of urban households experienced declining real income. These are different income concepts, not disposable-income national accounts. Mexico's survey of 57 manufacturing classes recorded annual employment down 3.4%, although its fourth-quarter year-on-year change turned positive. Aggregate GDP recovery therefore did not imply uniform industrial hiring.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The realized black swan was Black Monday. It raised fears of a 1929-style collapse, but liquidity support and no banking panic limited immediate macro damage. The policy legacy was market-plumbing reform and clearer central-bank liquidity roles.

The crash created a liquidity and information crisis as well as a valuation loss. Delayed executions and stale quotes obscured market conditions. Margin calls protected clearinghouses but demanded cash before gains on other positions were credited. The Federal Reserve's later staff reconstruction by Mark Carlson reports delayed transfers, settlement-bank credit and operational interruptions; it is retrospective evidence, not a 1987 forecast. The Federal Reserve's 1987 annual report independently confirms generous reserve supply after the crash.

The policy response did not imply that all institutions were sound, that trading never stopped, or that every central bank used the same instrument. Hong Kong's prolonged closure and guarantee-corporation support were especially consequential. The BIS also warned that closing markets could redirect selling to still-open centers. U.S. circuit-breaker reforms recommended or implemented later belong to later dates, not to the crash week. There is no single settled causal explanation: valuation, international imbalances, interest rates, portfolio strategies, liquidity and trading capacity interacted, and regulators disagreed about the weight of program trading and appropriate trading halts. Contemporary concern about consumption and investment is distinct from the later observation that a global depression did not follow.

Other risks were possible exchange-rate coordination failure, developing-country debt stress, Japan's asset bubble, and partial reform failure in socialist economies. Debt overhang, inflation and property-credit exposure were additional vulnerabilities, and Mexico's late-year pact was not already a completed disinflation success. The Japan and Soviet-bloc risks are partly hindsight: visible, but their later scale was not fully knowable in 1987; Japan's later asset collapse and Soviet institutional breakdown are not inevitable outcomes demonstrable from a 1987 snapshot.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1987 vs. 1986)

Oil shifted from collapse to partial recovery: the U.S. first-purchase price rose 23.10% to $15.40 per barrel. The composition of trade growth changed as well: the BIS estimated manufactured-goods trade volume up 5% and agricultural trade up 4%, whereas oil and other mineral trade contracted about 1%. These are trade-volume changes, not sectoral value-added returns. Exchange-rate policy shifted from forcing dollar depreciation to stabilizing the dollar, then toward liquidity support after the crash. Financial psychology shifted from strong equity-market optimism through late summer to recognition of market-structure fragility after October. Regional integration became more concrete because the Single European Act entered into force and CUSFTA was agreed. Reform pressure became more visible in China, the USSR, and Poland.

The year-end Fed discount rate stood higher than at the end of 1986 (6% against 5.5%), while Japan's discount rate was lower (2.5% against 3%); different instruments and time windows explain why "tightening" and "easing" can both describe parts of the year. WDI world real growth rose from 3.31% to 3.75%. Annual-average CPI inflation rose in the United States from 1.89% to 3.66%, while Brazil's rose to 228.34% and Mexico's to 131.83%.

In the Maddison top 20 there were no entries or exits; China moved above Japan and the Former USSR in the real-output ranking, and Argentina and the Netherlands exchanged eighteenth and nineteenth places. The ranking comparison does not certify disjoint historical sovereignty or equal precision across market and planned economies. Changes in rates, ratios and fiscal balances in the country tables are percentage-point changes, and exchange-rate changes are in currency units; changes are given only where both same-source observations exist, without interpolation.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

High-confidence data include WDI nominal GDP, FRED world GDP, IMF aggregate output and trade descriptions, Federal Reserve Black Monday documentation, and EIA oil-price data. Dated original records include the BIS crash-week comparison, the Federal Reserve and Bank of Japan discount-rate actions, the Louvre statement, the NBS communiqué, Banco de México's annual report and the NTT and JR East chronologies. Medium-confidence data include Maddison for market economies. Low-confidence data include the Former USSR and Poland, where non-market output estimates may carry uncertainty of roughly plus or minus 20 to 30 percent. Maddison, Macrohistory Database and IMF WEO values are database observations; they do not establish economic truth, independence or full comparability. Current WDI and WEO data can reflect later national-account revisions and do not automatically displace contemporaneous accounts.

Nominal and real-output rankings conflict because exchange rates, price levels, controls, and non-market valuation matter. China and India are much larger in Maddison real output than in WDI current-dollar GDP. Japan and West Germany are larger in WDI current-dollar rankings than in real-output comparisons. The Former USSR has no clean market-rate equivalent.

**Vintage differences.** GDP levels in section 4 are Maddison Project Database 2023 real GDP in 2011 international dollars, with WDI current U.S. dollars in brackets; the two units are not comparable, and growth rates are labelled by source (Maddison Project Database 2023 for country growth, WDI for world growth, IMF Annual Report 1988 and BIS 1988 for contemporary aggregates). The world GDP level in the text (about US$17.47 trillion) comes from an earlier WDI/FRED vintage; the current WDI vintage gives $17.5328 trillion for 1987 and $15.3978 trillion for 1986. Ranking-table GDP, population and per-capita values are rounded Maddison figures; the unrounded records give, for example, 8,432.567 billion 2011 international dollars and 242.804 million people for the United States.

**Material conflicts.** The Maddison Germany series is a reconstructed combined territorial record, not the Federal Republic alone; the WDI current-dollar GDP, the BIS current account and most German indicators describe the Federal Republic, and a Federal Republic current-account balance is not a ratio to combined-territory GDP. China's preliminary official GNP growth of 9.4% is a different concept and vintage from Maddison GDP growth of 8.38%, and its retail-price and living-cost indices are not the WDI CPI. Mexico's December-to-December inflation (159.2%) differs from the WDI annual-average CPI (131.83%), and its primary surplus is not an overall government balance; Banco de México dates the solidarity pact to 16 December, while later summaries sometimes use 15 December. Contemporary BIS current-account balances for the United Kingdom (-$3.0 billion) and Canada (-$7.3 billion) are not reconciled with later WDI ratios, and Spain's BIS surplus of $0.8 billion conflicts in sign with the negative WDI ratio. The IMF's approximately 5.5% world trade-volume growth and the BIS's preliminary 4¾% merchandise estimate differ in coverage. The Dow's 22.6% one-day fall and the S&P 500's 20.4% fall in the BIS comparison are different indices. BIS representative indices for several markets, including Hong Kong, are not named. NTT's English offering table dates the listing "February 9, 1986", while other dates on the same page and the company's share history give 1987. Macrohistory Database equity series for Germany and the Netherlands do not satisfy the total-return identity in the published columns, and its Japanese and U.S. capital gains are not matched to named index endpoints. WDI CPI data show inflation rising in 1987 in the United States, Germany, France and the United Kingdom, so the advanced-economy picture is one of low rather than continuously falling inflation. Agreement between WDI and IMF series is not necessarily independent corroboration, since both draw on national sources.

Weakest areas are policy rates outside the United States and Japan, debt ratios for developing and planned economies, several current accounts and fiscal balances, exchange rates for economies with later redenominations, city real estate, corporate rankings outside the United States, and sectoral employment. Many exist in scattered national or IMF/OECD/BIS sources but are not given in a harmonized 1987 form in the sources cited. Government debt and fiscal balances mix IMF WEO general-government, WDI central-government and Macrohistory Database perimeters, labelled in each table: Japan's debt is unconsolidated, Canada's coverage differs, Argentina's metadata describe central government, and Spain and the Netherlands use Maastricht-style debt excluding other payables.
