# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1915

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1915 was the first full year in which the world economy operated under the conditions of World War I. The defining economic fact was not a single business cycle turn, but the conversion of major European economies from peacetime market coordination toward state-directed war economies. Finance, trade, labor allocation, shipping, food supply, and industrial production were all pulled into the war effort. The global gold standard had already been severely disrupted in 1914; in 1915 the practical monetary system became a set of emergency regimes, with gold convertibility suspended or restricted in many belligerent countries, capital controls expanding, and neutral financial centers gaining relative importance.

The largest global reallocation was away from civilian consumption and toward public procurement, shipping, munitions, food supply, and strategic materials. Britain and France relied increasingly on imports and external finance, especially from North America. Germany and Austria-Hungary faced tightening blockade pressure and import substitution problems. Russia had not yet suffered its full output collapse, but the weaknesses of transport, procurement, food distribution, and monetary finance were becoming visible. Italy entered the war in May 1915, adding another major European economy to the mobilization system.

The United States was the most important non-belligerent beneficiary. It was still neutral, but its export industries, banks, railroads, farmers, and manufacturers increasingly served Allied demand. Gold inflows and Allied borrowing strengthened the dollar and the American banking system, while also raising concerns about domestic credit expansion and inflation. Japan also gained from the war through export growth, shipping demand, and reduced European competition in Asian markets. China, India, Argentina, Brazil, Canada, Australia, and other producers were pulled into war trade channels in uneven ways.

GDP rankings for 1915 are necessarily estimates. No contemporary global system of national accounts existed. The figures below use Maddison-style reconstructed real GDP and population series, cross-checked against later economic history literature and official series where available. They should not be read as official 1915 GDP. For pre-1990 non-market or imperial economies, and for colonial economies such as British India and the Dutch East Indies, uncertainty is high. Growth rates follow the Maddison Project Database 2023, which shows real GDP rising 2.20% in the United States, 6.92% in the United Kingdom and 8.02% in Japan, and falling 5.04% in Germany and 2.04% in France. Where modern-style indicators were not available, this document states "Data not available" rather than guessing.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1915

**1. Total-war mobilization became the dominant macroeconomic regime.** 1914 had been a shock year; 1915 was the year in which governments accepted that the war would not be short. Ministries, treasuries, central banks, and procurement agencies expanded their roles. Britain moved deeper into munitions coordination and short-term Treasury finance. France developed a more mixed economy of state buying and private production. Germany relied on war loans, central bank support, and import control. The forward linkage was the larger command economies of 1916 to 1918.

**2. The classical gold standard gave way to wartime monetary fragmentation.** Before the war, the gold standard and sterling-centered bill market had supported international trade and capital flows. In 1915, gold convertibility, private gold exports, and cross-border payments were heavily restricted in many belligerent economies. Britain kept a managed gold and sterling system, but not the prewar free gold standard. Germany had suspended specie payments in 1914. The United States received gold inflows from Europe, strengthening its reserve position and accelerating the dollar's rise as an international currency.

**3. Neutral economies became strategic suppliers and financial intermediaries.** The United States, the Netherlands, Spain, Argentina, Brazil, and other neutrals experienced a mixture of export opportunity, shipping disruption, inflation, and diplomatic pressure. Japan had declared war on Germany on August 23, 1914, so it was a belligerent rather than a neutral. Neutrality did not mean insulation. It meant exposure to blockades, freight shortages, insurance costs, foreign exchange strain, and pressure to choose trading partners indirectly.

**4. Blockade and shipping constraints reshaped commodity markets.** Britain and its allies used sea power to restrict Central Power imports. Germany and Austria-Hungary faced growing scarcity of food, fertilizer, metals, and imported raw materials. At the same time, ocean freight rates rose and commodity prices shifted. Oil remained a small but rising strategic sector; the US domestic crude first-purchase price averaged $0.64 per barrel in nominal terms, down from $0.81 in 1914. Wheat and other staples were affected by higher demand and freight costs.

**5. Labor markets were militarized and segmented.** Millions of workers were mobilized or redirected to war industries. Women, colonial workers, older workers, and youth entered or expanded participation in industrial and support work. Standard unemployment rates are mostly not comparable or unavailable for 1915. The central labor issue was not peacetime joblessness, but the allocation of scarce labor among armies, farms, transport, factories, and households.

**6. The United States moved from debtor periphery toward creditor center.** The shift was not complete in 1915, but it was visible. European purchases of American food, raw materials, and manufactured goods generated gold inflows. Private American banks arranged large credits for Allied governments. The forward linkage was the postwar rise of New York in global finance and the weakening of London's prewar monopoly over international liquidity.

**7. Food, transport, and inflation risks were already systemic.** Russia, Germany, the Ottoman Empire, and occupied Belgium faced serious distribution problems. These were not only agricultural problems. They were failures of transport, procurement, price controls, fiscal finance, and political legitimacy. With hindsight, 1915 can be seen as an early warning year for the deeper crises of 1916 to 1918.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

**World GDP and growth rate:** Data not available as a reliable contemporaneous world aggregate. Maddison-style reconstructions place world output in the low single-digit trillions of 1990 international dollars, but annual global growth for 1915 is highly uncertain because major combatants, colonies, and non-market economies were reconstructed later with different methods. A defensible statement is that global output was redistributed rather than uniformly reduced: U.S. and Japanese output rose or held up, while parts of continental Europe contracted or shifted heavily toward war production.

**Global trade volume:** The Federico-Tena world trade volume index (1913=100) fell from 86.93 in 1914 to 73.51 in 1915 (-15.44%). The index is a reconstruction that includes coverage gaps and imputations, not a sum of recorded trade. Nominal exports in current borders fell from $15,323 million to $14,720 million (-3.93%), a different measure. Trade flows were reorganized by blockade, military shipping needs, freight shortages, insurance costs, and import licensing. Allied access to Atlantic shipping and American supply was a central advantage. Central Powers increasingly relied on domestic production, occupied territories, neutral intermediaries, and rationing.

**World population:** About 1,819.0 million, up 12.5 million (0.69%) from 1,806.5 million in 1914, in the Federico-Tena January 2026 reconstruction summed from 1991-border country series.

**Reserve currency dynamics:** Sterling remained central to global finance, but wartime controls weakened the old gold-sterling system. The U.S. dollar gained importance because the United States was receiving gold, selling exports, and extending credit. There was no Bretton Woods system in 1915.

**Global inflation trends:** Inflation rose in many belligerent and neutral economies, but comparable CPI series are limited. Macrohistory consumer-price inflation was 25.50% in Germany, 17.65% in France, 15.00% in the Netherlands, 12.50% in the United Kingdom, 11.25% in Australia, 9.53% in Spain, 7.00% in Italy, 1.81% in Canada and 1.00% in the United States, while Japan recorded -6.40%. Inflation was driven by monetary expansion, war borrowing, shipping bottlenecks, higher food prices, and scarcity of tradable goods. Mexico and the Ottoman Empire faced especially severe monetary instability. The United States experienced emerging inflationary pressure from gold inflows and export demand.

**Key commodity prices:** The US domestic crude first-purchase price averaged $0.64 per barrel in 1915 against $0.81 in 1914 (-20.99%) in the Energy Information Administration's annual series, a national price rather than a world market quotation. Gold's official U.S. mint price remained $20.67 per troy ounce, but free gold markets were distorted by controls. Wheat prices were materially higher than in 1914, with contemporary statistical commentary attributing increases to both demand and freight costs. British-market quotations for wheat, copper, coal, refined petroleum and sugar are given in section 3D; transaction-weighted world prices for nitrates, rubber, cotton, jute, and steel-related inputs are Data not available.

**International capital flow direction:** Capital flowed increasingly from the United States and neutral financial channels toward the Allied powers. Britain and France sold assets, borrowed in New York, and mobilized domestic savings. Germany financed itself mainly through domestic war loans and central bank credit. In the Macrohistory Database the US current account moved from -0.46% to +2.90% of GDP and the UK current account from +5.51% to -1.88%. Capital mobility was far below prewar norms.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

**United States:** The Federal Reserve System was new and still developing. Gold inflows from Europe expanded reserves and credit. The modern federal funds rate did not exist. Discount rates at Reserve Banks were the relevant instrument, but they were not equivalent to a modern policy rate. The Reserve Banks' gold settlement fund began preliminary clearings on May 20 and regular weekly settlements on May 27, and settled $1,052.6 million of interbank balances by December 31; this is an interbank settlement flow, not a measure of national payments. The Macrohistory market rate on surplus funds was 1.92%, down from 3.43%.

**United Kingdom:** Bank Rate was 5 percent through 1915 in the Bank of England historical series. In practice, war finance and Treasury bill issuance increasingly influenced money-market conditions; the Macrohistory ordinary short-term rate was 3.66%, a different instrument. The pound remained central, but not under the old free gold standard conditions.

**Germany:** The Reichsbank had suspended specie payments in 1914. War finance relied on Treasury bills, central bank credit, and repeated war loans. The 1914 war loan had a 5 percent coupon; later wartime finance continued the debt-heavy model. A verified 1915 Reichsbank discount rate is Data not available; the Macrohistory market short-term rate was 3.89%.

**France:** The Banque de France and Treasury operated under wartime finance conditions. Gold convertibility and capital movement were constrained. The state expanded purchasing, financing, and allocation functions.

**Japan:** Japan retained more normal financial conditions than European belligerents and benefited from export growth. The Bank of Japan's discount rate on commercial bills was 7.30% throughout 1915, unchanged from July 6, 1914 to April 16, 1916; the Macrohistory compilation's 5.91% for Japan is a different, unreconciled series.

**Stock markets:** All-stock trading on the New York Stock Exchange had resumed on December 15, 1914, and minimum-price restrictions were removed on April 1, 1915. The New York market staged a strong rebound; the Dow Jones Industrial Average ended 1915 at roughly 99.15, with an annual gain of about 81 percent from its depressed 1914 close. London reopened in January 1915, but capital markets were increasingly subordinated to war finance. Berlin bond trading remained severely restricted.

**Bond markets and yield curves:** Standard 10-year yield comparisons are not reliable for 1915. Government borrowing dominated. Britain issued large war loans and short-term Treasury instruments. Germany relied heavily on war loans and Reichsbank-supported paper. Many private capital markets were crowded out by public borrowing.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

World trade in 1915 was geopolitically divided. Britain expanded blockade measures, including restrictions on cargoes considered destined for enemy use. Germany faced declining imports and attempted to respond through substitutes, rationing, and pressure on neutral routes. Allied purchases in the United States became a major political and financial issue. Japan used the war to expand trade and influence in Asia, and concluded treaties with China on May 25. Italy's entry into the war in May 1915 changed European trade and fiscal alignments. Colonial economies were integrated into imperial supply chains through food, raw materials, labor, and finance.

No EEC, ASEAN, IMF, World Bank, or Bretton Woods institutions existed. Regional integration was imperial rather than treaty-based in the later twentieth-century sense. Tariffs mattered, but wartime licensing, blockade, shipping priority, and exchange controls were more important than normal tariff bargaining. Britain's Finance (No. 2) Act imposed duties of 33 1/3% from September 29 on imports of specified motor cars, musical instruments, clocks and watches.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil was strategically important but still smaller than coal in the global energy system. Coal remained the core fuel for heavy industry, shipping, railways, and urban energy. Britain and Germany were major coal economies; French access to coal and steel was damaged by the occupation of industrial regions. U.S. oil production and refining gained importance as transport and mechanization needs rose. Metals, nitrates, rubber, cotton, jute, and grain moved through increasingly militarized supply chains.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor market data are not comparable across countries in 1915. The dominant trends were conscription, war industry expansion, female industrial employment, colonial labor mobilization, and pressure on farm labor. Historical unemployment estimates in the Macrohistory Database were 8.5% in the United States (1914: 7.9%), 5.9% in Australia (3.3%), 4.60% in the Netherlands (3.82%), 3.3% in Germany (7.2%) and 1.1% in the United Kingdom (3.3%), on national definitions that are not harmonized. Urban consumers faced rising food prices in many countries; the Board of Trade estimated that the cost of a fixed working-class budget in British towns of over 50,000 people was about 35% above July 1914 by December 1915, with food up 46%, clothing 35%, fuel and light 30% and rent 2%. Migration was constrained by war, but refugees, prisoners, colonial workers, and internal relocation became economically significant. Productivity statistics are weak, but it is clear that civilian productivity and welfare were often sacrificed to military output and state procurement.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States was the clearest macroeconomic winner of 1915. It remained neutral, but Allied demand for food, raw materials, chemicals, finance, transport, and manufactured goods lifted output and profits. The U.S. economy was not yet a fully mobilized war economy, but it was becoming the supply base for Allied Europe. Gold inflows increased banking reserves and helped transform New York into a more important international financial center.

The Federal Reserve was too new to operate like a modern central bank. It could not easily neutralize gold inflows, and credit conditions were shaped by export demand and bank lending. The main macro risk was that export-led expansion and gold inflows would later turn into inflationary pressure.

Steel output rose 36.74% to 32.15 million long tons, pig iron 28.22% to 29.92 million long tons and iron ore 39.74% to 55.50 million long tons, while the cotton crop fell 30.64% to 11.19 million 500-pound bales on a crop-year basis.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Agriculture benefited from grain demand; the corn crop rose 12.05% to 2,994.8 million bushels. Steel, chemicals, explosives inputs, shipping, railroads, and finance gained; crude zinc output rose 33.40% to 458,135 short tons. New York investment banks arranged large Allied credits. Consumer conditions were mixed: employment and income rose in export industries, but prices began to rise. Barro-Ursua real consumption per person fell 3.37%, Macrohistory bank loans rose 3.01% and the nominal house-price index fell 7.06%. Corporate data by revenue and market capitalization are incomplete for 1915.

**Economic narrative:**

China's aggregate GDP was large because of population, not per capita income. The Republic of China under Yuan Shikai had limited fiscal reach and a fragmented political economy. National accounts, unemployment, and CPI data are not available in modern form. The currency system was silver-based and regionally uneven, although 1914 to 1915 coinage reforms helped standardize silver yuan circulation.

The war had a paradoxical effect. European disruption opened markets for Chinese exports and reduced the trade deficit, but these gains were temporary and uneven. Treaty ports, coastal trade, textiles, foodstuffs, and raw material exports were more directly affected than inland subsistence agriculture.

The treaties signed with Japan on May 25 concerned former German interests in Shandong, railway and port arrangements, 99-year lease extensions and restrictions on the financing of the Hanyehping company. The signed terms did not include all of the original Twenty-One Demands and did not amount to wholesale nationalization.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Export merchants, treaty-port finance, shipping intermediaries, and some light manufacturers benefited. The household economy remained overwhelmingly agrarian. Corporate data are limited. Political fragmentation limited infrastructure and fiscal modernization.

**Economic narrative:**

The United Kingdom shifted from financial panic management to long-war finance. London remained a global financial center, but the Treasury increasingly shaped money markets through bills, war loans, and capital controls. The state expanded its role in munitions, shipping, food supply, and labor coordination. Civilian consumption was squeezed as resources moved into war production.

Britain's strategic economic advantage was access to sea lanes, empire, and American credit. Its weakness was dependence on imports and external finance. The 1915 Allied borrowing relationship with the United States was one of the most important financial developments of the year.

The Munitions of War Act of July 2 restricted strikes and lockouts in specified disputes, provided for arbitration and allowed the designation of controlled establishments subject to profit and workplace rules; it did not nationalize industry. The Finance (No. 2) Act of December 23 set the tea duty at 1s per pound, added 3d per gallon to the motor spirit duty, and introduced an excess-profits duty of 50% above a statutory standard; the tea, motor-spirit and new import duties took effect in September, before enactment.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Shipbuilding, steel, coal, textiles for military supply, munitions, rail, and finance were central. Consumer goods faced shortages and price pressure. Labor relations changed under war regulation, and women entered war-related industrial roles in greater numbers. Barro-Ursua real consumption per person rose 1.53%, Macrohistory bank loans fell 7.41% and the nominal house-price index rose 22.71%.

### 5B. Corporate landscape

Globally comparable top-company rankings by revenue or market capitalization are not available for 1915. The strongest corporate clusters were U.S. steel, railroads, oil, banking, and chemicals; British shipping, banking, mining, and armaments supply; German chemicals, electrical equipment, coal, and heavy industry; French banking and relocated industrial production; Japanese shipping, textiles, and trading companies. Corporate profits depended heavily on war demand, access to raw materials, shipping, and state contracts.

Comparable M&A data are Data not available. IPO data are not globally comparable. Notable failures were often not formal bankruptcies but shutdowns, requisitions, occupation losses, exchange closures, or state redirection of capital. Nationalization in the later twentieth-century sense was less common than wartime control, licensing, requisitioning, and compulsory allocation. In India, the Deutsche-Asiatische Bank went into liquidation after the outbreak of war and is excluded from the exchange-bank returns.

### 5C. Innovation & technology

Innovation in 1915 was less about consumer technology and more about industrial organization, materials science, communications, logistics, chemical production, and standardized procurement. Germany's chemical sector, Britain's munitions organization, U.S. mass production capabilities, Japan's shipbuilding expansion, and Allied logistics all had long-term implications. Patent and R&D spending data are not comparable globally.

Infrastructure megaprojects were constrained by war priorities. Railways, ports, telegraph networks, and shipping capacity were more important than new peacetime construction. War needs accelerated applied engineering and production methods but often at the expense of civilian investment.

### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Labor shifted from civilian sectors into military service, war production, railways, shipping, ports, agriculture, and state administration. Women entered or expanded work in industrial and clerical roles in several countries. Colonial labor and goods were mobilized across empires. Wage data are fragmented; real wages depended on whether nominal pay kept up with food and rent. Union power varied, but labor unrest became more likely where inflation outpaced wages or food distribution failed.

Education and human capital were affected by mobilization and fiscal pressure. In belligerent countries, the opportunity cost of war was large: investment in schools, housing, public health, and civilian capital formation was delayed or crowded out.

### 6C. Currencies

The prewar gold standard was fragmented. The U.S. dollar remained linked to gold and gained reserve strength from inflows. Sterling remained central but was managed under wartime constraints; the Bank of England dataset's annual average fell from $4.9303 to $4.757 per pound (-3.51%). The German mark, French franc, Russian ruble, Austro-Hungarian crown, Ottoman lira, and Mexican currencies faced varying degrees of convertibility restriction, depreciation, or monetary expansion. Annual Macrohistory rates per dollar rose 16.46% for the lira (6.05), 15.87% for the Belgian franc (6.06), 13.55% for the mark (4.86) and 9.30% for the French franc (5.569), while pesetas per dollar fell 3.51% to 5.23 and the guilder moved 0.30% to 2.4696; the Bank of Japan's December demand-draft rate implies 2.0135 yen per dollar (1914: 2.0356). China remained tied to silver conditions and fragmented currency circulation. Many exchange rates cannot be interpreted as free market rates.

### 6D. Alternative assets

Gold's official U.S. price remained $20.67 per troy ounce, but gold markets were restricted. Comparable annual real estate data for major cities are limited to selected index series. In the Philadelphia Fed historical housing indices, New York City asking prices rose 0.24% and asking rents 4.39%, and Boston asking prices fell 1.02% while asking rents rose 1.37%; these are hedonic newspaper-listing estimates sampled mostly in late May. Norges Bank's Oslo inner-city repeat-sales index fell 5.20%, and the Historical Statistics of Switzerland index of Zurich three-room apartment rents fell 2.8% (1914=100). These are separate measures with different units and timing, not a comparable city ranking. Land, food commodities, shipping capacity, warehouse stocks, and foreign exchange claims were economically important alternative stores of value. In Mexico and some war economies, hard money and goods could become more trusted than paper claims.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The largest active crisis was the continuation and deepening of World War I. The "black swan" element of 1915 was not that war existed, but that many governments had still underestimated the duration, cost, and institutional demands of industrial warfare. By 1915, the risk of a short-war assumption had already materialized.

Key systemic vulnerabilities were war finance inflation, transport bottlenecks, food distribution failures, shipping losses, blockade escalation, and capital-market crowding out. Russia, Germany, Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire, Belgium, and Mexico had especially high institutional stress. Britain and France were more resilient because of empire, sea access, and access to U.S. credit, but they were building large debt burdens.

With hindsight, 1915 was an early warning year for later crises: Russia's 1917 breakdown, Germany's worsening scarcity, the Ottoman fiscal and supply crisis, postwar European debt instability, and the permanent shift in financial power toward the United States. These outcomes were not fully knowable in 1915, but the mechanisms were already visible.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1915 vs. 1914)

The year 1914 was defined by outbreak, panic, emergency closures, and first mobilization. The year 1915 was defined by institutional adaptation to long war. The major structural break was the acceptance that ordinary market mechanisms could not allocate food, labor, shipping, finance, and industrial inputs under war conditions.

Compared with 1914, public borrowing expanded sharply; Macrohistory debt ratios rose 12.01 points in the United Kingdom and 11.07 points in Italy. Britain moved deeper into Treasury bill finance and war loans. Germany consolidated a debt-heavy war finance model. France reorganized production after industrial occupation. Italy moved from neutrality to belligerency. The United States moved from crisis recovery to export-led war boom, with growth of 2.20% after -8.26% in the 2023 release. Japan moved further into export expansion and Asian market opportunity. The largest increases in Macrohistory consumer-price inflation were 22.74 points in Germany (from 2.76% to 25.50%), 15.61 points in France and 15.00 points in the Netherlands.

Risks that emerged in 1915 included food unrest, inflationary finance, and the dependence of Allied powers on U.S. credit. Risks that partly resolved included the immediate liquidity panic of 1914 and the uncertainty over whether New York could function as a major wartime financial market. The shift in economic influence from Europe toward the United States accelerated.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

**Reliable or relatively reliable areas:** U.S. nominal and real GDP series, U.S. population, Bank of England Bank Rate, the Bank of Japan discount schedule, some historical oil prices, Dow Jones index values, and selected war-finance institutional facts are relatively well documented. Britain, France, Germany, and Japan have strong economic history literatures, though not always modern annual macro indicators for 1915. Federal Reserve, British statutory and British India statistical records give dated institutional facts.

**Estimated areas:** GDP rankings, GDP per capita, and population for most countries are reconstructed. Maddison-style estimates are suitable for broad ranking but not precise annual policy analysis. Soviet-bloc predecessor regions, China, India, Indonesia, the Ottoman Empire, Mexico, and occupied Belgium have high uncertainty. Error margins of plus or minus 20 to 30 percent are plausible for several entries. Macrohistory, Barro-Ursua and Federico-Tena series are retrospective reconstructions; Federico-Tena world trade estimates missing wartime exports, and world population is a reconstruction with interpolation (the historical-border World26 variant gives 1,818.98 million).

**Conflicting areas:** GDP rankings change depending on whether one uses modern borders, imperial polities, colonies, nominal exchange rates, 1990 international dollars, 2011 PPP dollars, or 2021 international dollars. China, India, Russia, the United Kingdom, and Germany can shift order depending on method. Colonial economies such as British India and the Dutch East Indies are not sovereign states but were major economic units. The 1990-dollar ranges are not consistent with the 1914 point estimates for several economies: Austria-Hungary ($105-120 billion against 83,918 million in 1914), Italy ($100-110 billion against 95,413 million), Canada ($37-41 billion against 32,577 million), Argentina ($28-35 billion against 26,038 million) and Brazil ($24-31 billion against 18,844 million) all lie above the 1914 levels by more than the 2023-release growth for these economies (or for later-border Austria) would imply, and the population ranges for China, the Russian Empire, British India, Germany, France, Italy, Austria-Hungary and the Dutch East Indies differ from the 1914 figures. Growth rates are Maddison Project Database 2023 rates; an estimate of about +3% for the United States differs from the 2023 release's +2.20%, and for Italy and Mexico the older Maddison 2010 series shows growth (+11.86% and +0.67%) where the 2023 release shows contraction. For the United Kingdom, the Macrohistory Database and the Bank of England millennium dataset differ for unemployment (1.1% against 0.95%), debt (39.28% against 63.71% of GDP) and the current account (-1.88% against -1.81% of GDP); the Macrohistory values are shown and the series are not blended. Japan's Bank of Japan discount rate (7.30%) differs from the Macrohistory compilation (5.91%), and its annual CPI fell while the Tokyo December wholesale index rose 18.36%. The Federal Reserve's 1915 report gives net gold transfers through the settlement fund as $95.697 million in its text but $85.697 million in its table; the table figure, which matches the bank components, is preferred. The Dow Jones close of about 99.15 and Shiller's composite (+28.98%) are different index constructions. Belgium's 1915 Macrohistory price figure is an implicit deflator, and the Spanish, Dutch and Australian short-rate series change source in 1915. British India's postal savings balances differ by Rs 159 from the printed deposits less withdrawals.

**Unavailable data:** Comparable unemployment, CPI, central bank policy rates, government debt/GDP, current account/GDP, budget balance/GDP, and exchange-rate series are not available for many 1915 economies, including China, the Russian Empire, Austria-Hungary and the Ottoman Empire. Corporate revenue, market capitalization, M&A, IPO, bankruptcy, household debt, and real estate data are generally not globally comparable.

**Weakest claims:** The exact lower-bound top-20 ranking, GDP growth for the imperial and colonial economies, and sectoral performance rankings for colonial and neutral economies have the weakest evidence. They should be treated as historically informed estimates, not official statistics.
