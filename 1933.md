# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1933

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1933 was the turning point year of the Great Depression, but not a uniform recovery year. The MPD-covered world aggregate totals about 5.04 trillion 2011 international dollars and rises only +0.5% from 1932. That aggregate is not an official world GDP statistic; it is a constructed sum of Maddison Project Database country estimates after excluding obvious successor-state double counting. It is best read as a broad, PPP-style real-output approximation. Industry recovered faster than jobs: the League of Nations weighted industrial index for 21 countries rose from 78 to 88 (1925-1929=100), while its index of industrial employment in 16 countries rose only from 75 to 78 (1929=100).

The defining macroeconomic force was the breakdown of gold-standard orthodoxy. Britain and Japan had already left gold and were recovering faster under cheaper money and depreciated currencies. The United States broke with gold convertibility in practice during 1933 through the bank holiday, restrictions on gold hoarding and export, and reflationary policy. France, the Netherlands, Belgium, Switzerland, and several other gold-bloc countries held to gold and accepted more deflationary pressure. The result was a split global recovery in which countries that changed monetary regimes tended to improve sooner than countries that defended parity.

The second defining force was state intervention. In the United States, the early New Deal created or reshaped institutions for banking, securities markets, agriculture, public works, and industrial policy. In Germany, public works, credit direction, exchange controls, and labor-market coercion accompanied recovery after political rupture. In Japan, the Takahashi policy mix pushed demand and exports. In Italy, the creation of IRI made the state a major industrial owner. In Brazil and Argentina, external pressure pushed exchange controls, commodity support, and early import-substitution patterns.

The year was therefore not just a cyclical rebound. It was a regime-change year in money, trade, finance, and the boundary between state and market. What was visible to contemporaries was crisis management and uneven recovery. What is clearer with hindsight is that 1933 accelerated the move from liberal international economic integration toward currency blocs, managed trade, capital controls, and nationally directed recovery strategies.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1933

**1. Gold-standard rupture and asymmetric recovery.** The economies that had abandoned gold or loosened gold constraints were generally better placed to reflate. Britain and Japan were already benefiting from currency depreciation and easier money. The United States moved decisively away from gold convertibility in 1933, although the formal dollar revaluation came in 1934. The gold bloc remained under pressure. The forward linkage was direct: countries that stayed on gold, especially France, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Switzerland, faced later devaluation or continued stagnation.

**2. Banking stabilization after systemic panic.** The U.S. bank holiday was the most dramatic event, but banking weakness was global. The 1933 U.S. response combined emergency reopening, deposit-insurance legislation, and securities-market regulation. Italy created IRI to handle bank and industrial distress. Germany's banks were pulled further into state direction. The forward linkage was the rise of deposit insurance, bank supervision, capital controls, and state balance-sheet support as normal tools of crisis management.

**3. Trade fragmentation and the retreat from liberal globalization.** World trade remained far below late-1920s levels. Tariffs, quotas, exchange controls, import licensing, imperial preference, and bilateral agreements shaped commercial flows. The London Monetary and Economic Conference failed to restore a common international monetary or trade framework. The forward linkage was a 1930s world economy organized increasingly around sterling, gold, dollar, yen, and exchange-control blocs rather than one integrated capital-and-trade system.

**4. Public works, state ownership, and planning.** 1933 saw fiscal and quasi-fiscal intervention in several large economies. The U.S. launched New Deal agencies, Germany expanded work-creation measures, Italy created IRI, the USSR continued central planning, and Brazil used state intervention in coffee and foreign exchange. These policies differed morally, politically, and economically, but all widened the state's role in directing investment and employment.

**5. Agriculture and commodity distress.** Farm incomes had collapsed before 1933 and remained central to policy. The United States used acreage controls and support measures; Brazil managed coffee; Argentina negotiated meat access; Canada and Australia remained exposed to wheat and wool; India and China were dominated by rural household vulnerability. The forward linkage was a more interventionist agricultural policy tradition and a weakening of pure commodity-export dependence.

**6. Recovery with unreliable and unequal labor-market data.** Unemployment was severe in industrial economies, but cross-country unemployment rates are not comparable. Definitions differed by registration systems, relief rules, insurance coverage, colonial statistics, and non-market economies. The reliable analytical point is that labor distress shaped policy everywhere, from U.S. relief and public works to German coercive labor policy and British regional unemployment debates.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

**World GDP and growth.** MPD-covered aggregate GDP is about **5.04 trillion 2011 international dollars**. The 55 economies observed in both years rise from 5,018.5 billion to 5,043.5 billion, up **+0.50%**. This is a constructed sum of country estimates, not an official world national-account series. It excludes countries without MPD data and excludes successor-state double counting where the USSR and Czechoslovakia aggregates are used. World population in the Federico-Tena reconstruction on 1991 borders rose 1.12% to 2,122.8 million.

**Global trade volume.** The League of Nations put the 1933 world export quantum 2.4% above 1932, after a fall of 15.8% in 1932, while export values in old gold dollars fell 8.9% and gold prices of traded goods 11.0%. The later Federico-Tena world export quantum index instead falls 0.86%, from 95.28 to 94.46. The two series differ in construction, weighting and vintage, and no reconciled single figure is available.

**Reserve currency dynamics.** Sterling, the dollar, gold, and exchange-control currencies coexisted uneasily. Sterling had already left gold and functioned within an emerging sterling area. The dollar's gold convertibility was suspended in practice in 1933. Gold-bloc currencies retained gold commitments. The result was not Bretton Woods, which did not yet exist, but a fragmented interwar monetary system.

**Global inflation trends.** Deflationary pressure persisted in many gold-linked economies, while countries with depreciated currencies saw reflation or less severe deflation. The United States still recorded CPI deflation in 1933, even as expectations shifted after the gold-policy break. Japan moved earlier toward reflation. Gold's U.S. official price remained tied to the pre-1934 dollar framework while private gold holding and export were restricted.

**Capital flows.** International private capital mobility remained impaired by defaults, exchange controls, capital flight, blocked balances, and weak banking systems. Capital moved toward perceived safe centers and toward gold-hoarding before controls, while debtor countries faced refinancing difficulty.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The global interest-rate environment diverged. Britain maintained a cheap-money policy with Bank Rate at 2%. Japan's policy mix was expansionary after leaving gold. The United States moved from banking emergency to monetary experimentation and gold restriction. Germany relied increasingly on controls, directed credit, and work-creation financing. France and other gold-bloc economies were more constrained because parity defense limited aggressive reflation. Official discount rates at the end of 1933 were 2% in New York, 2% in London, 4% in Germany, 2.5% in France, 3.65% in Japan, 3% in Italy, 2.5% in the Netherlands, 3.5% in Belgium and 2% in Switzerland.

Exchange-rate regimes were unstable. There was no Bretton Woods system. Currencies were split between gold adherents, sterling-area floaters or managed currencies, exchange-control systems, silver-standard China, and non-market Soviet pricing. FX movements mattered because depreciation improved competitiveness for Japan and Britain, while gold-bloc overvaluation weakened exporters.

Credit conditions were tight for many private borrowers even where policy rates fell. Banks sought liquidity, borrowers were overleveraged, collateral values had fallen, and international lending remained impaired. Equity markets in some countries improved from crisis lows as reflation expectations rose, but annual index-level data by exchange are incomplete; reconstructed national annual returns are available for most major markets. Government bond markets benefited from cheap money in Britain and from flight-to-safety behavior, but sovereign debt risk remained high in many debtor economies.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

Trade policy in 1933 was shaped by the aftermath of Smoot-Hawley, imperial preference, quotas, foreign-exchange licensing, and bilateral bargaining. The London Monetary and Economic Conference failed to create a common stabilization plan, although it produced a silver agreement, signed on 22 July and effective from 1 January 1934. Argentina's Roca-Runciman agreement with Britain, followed by a supplementary chilled-beef convention signed on 26 September and effective on 7 November, showed how bilateral trade bargains replaced older open-market assumptions.

Geopolitically, Japan's occupation of Manchuria, European political extremism, and imperial trade blocs affected economic flows. Colonial economies such as India and the Netherlands Indies remained integrated into imperial and metropolitan systems. Soviet trade and output were governed by planning rather than market signals. Regional organizations such as the EEC and ASEAN did not yet exist.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

There was no OPEC in 1933. Oil markets were shaped by U.S. production, emerging Middle Eastern concessions, major integrated oil companies, and depression-era demand weakness. League of Nations estimates put world crude-oil output at 197 million metric tons (1932: 180 million) and coal output at 1,006.3 million metric tons (1932: 960.2 million). Recorded electricity output rose from 119 to 125 (1925-1929=100), excluding many industrial self-generators. World gold production rose 2.04% to 24.72 million fine ounces (1932: 24.226 million).

Metals and minerals were depressed by weak construction, industry, and trade. Gold was both a commodity and the anchor of monetary conflict. Agricultural staples were central to political economy: wheat in Canada and Australia, coffee in Brazil, cotton and grains in the United States and India, meat and wheat in Argentina, sugar and rubber in the Netherlands Indies, and rice in China and Japan's Asian sphere. ### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor-market distress was one of the most important global facts of 1933, but comparable unemployment statistics do not exist. Industrial economies had high unemployment, underemployment, short-time work, and relief dependence. Colonial and agrarian economies absorbed distress through lower rural incomes, unpaid family labor, migration, and informal employment rather than official unemployment counts. The League of Nations index of industrial employment in 16 countries, which excludes the USSR and counts short-time workers, rose 4.00%, from 75 to 78 (1929=100).

Demographically, the largest economies by aggregate GDP were often large because of population: China, India, the USSR, and Indonesia ranked highly despite low GDP per capita. Urbanization continued in many countries, but the depression slowed migration and reduced demand for industrial labor. Wage growth generally lagged productivity where deflation and unemployment weakened bargaining power, while union strength and labor policy varied widely.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States was still the largest economy in the Maddison ranking, but 1933 was the hinge year between collapse and managed recovery. The banking panic culminated in the March national bank holiday, after which the Emergency Banking Act, federal deposit insurance legislation, the Securities Act, agricultural support, public works, and industrial-code experimentation changed the institutional structure of American capitalism. Output in the MPD aggregate still fell relative to 1932, which reflects the weak first half of the year and the incomplete early recovery.

At the micro level, finance was the center of the shock. Bank failures, debt deflation, and forced deleveraging damaged households and firms before New Deal interventions stabilized confidence. Agriculture received price-support and acreage-control measures, manufacturing benefited from reflation expectations and public spending, and construction remained weak except where public projects began to matter. The Securities Act of 1933 began the federalization of securities disclosure, while Glass-Steagall separated commercial and investment banking and created a more durable deposit-insurance architecture.

Official series show how the turn unfolded. BEA real GDP fell 1.24% to $877.431 billion in chained 2017 dollars, and nominal GDP was $57.154 billion, while Federal Reserve industrial production rose 18.44% on annual averages. Consumer prices fell 5.1% on average but rose 0.8% from December to December. The national bank holiday began on 6 March and the Emergency Banking Act followed on 9 March. The Banking Act of 16 June created the FDIC, whose temporary deposit insurance began only on 1 January 1934, and the National Industrial Recovery Act of the same day authorized industrial codes and public works. The New York Reserve Bank discount rate rose temporarily to 3.5% during the March panic and stood at 2% from 20 October.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:**

Leading sectors and mechanisms: banking stabilization, federal relief, agriculture, public works, securities regulation, and early industrial recovery. Contracting or impaired areas: private construction, small banks, farm-credit channels, and consumer durables before confidence improved. The League of Nations index of building contracts in 37 states (floor area) fell from 17 to 16, and the Macrohistory nominal bank-loan stock fell 19.09%.

Technological change in 1933 was incremental but economically meaningful. ### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

The central labor-market fact was excess labor, but measurement differs by country. Industrial unemployment was high in the United States, Germany, Britain, Canada, Belgium, Czechoslovakia, and other manufacturing economies. In agrarian and colonial economies, distress appeared as lower farm incomes, rural underemployment, seasonal migration, and unpaid family labor rather than measured joblessness.

Union and labor movements faced very different environments. In democratic systems, unemployment and relief shaped politics. In Germany, independent labor institutions were suppressed, and the civil-service law of 7 April excluded Jews and political opponents from public employment. In the USSR, labor allocation occurred within state planning. In Japan, rural distress and industrial growth existed together: private factory employment averaged 9.57% above 1932 while factory wage rates fell 3.35%. Education and skill formation did not drive short-run 1933 outcomes as much as demand, credit, and exchange-rate policy did.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The active crises of 1933 were banking fragility, unemployment, deflation, sovereign debt stress, trade fragmentation, and political extremism. The U.S. banking panic was the most visible financial near-break. The failure of international monetary coordination at London left the world without a common stabilization plan. Gold-bloc countries risked deeper deflation by defending parity, while countries leaving gold risked capital flight, inflation uncertainty, and trade retaliation.

Systemic vulnerabilities visible with hindsight included the normalization of capital controls, bilateral trade blocs, state-directed credit, and coercive labor systems. These tools sometimes produced short-run recovery, but they also weakened liberal international coordination. Another hindsight risk was the growing role of military procurement and strategic industrial planning in Germany, Japan, Italy, and the USSR. The economic significance is clear, but comparable defense outlays for 1933 are Data not available.

Policy mistakes visible in retrospect include prolonged gold-bloc rigidity, inadequate international lender-of-last-resort coordination, and trade barriers that reinforced global fragmentation. Contemporaries could see deflation and unemployment; they could not fully know the later geopolitical consequences of the 1933 policy split.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1933 vs. 1932)

The largest structural break from 1932 to 1933 was the U.S. regime shift. In 1932, U.S. policy still operated within older gold-standard and balanced-budget assumptions. In 1933, banking emergency, gold restrictions, public works, agricultural intervention, industrial codes, securities regulation, and deposit insurance changed both macro policy and micro institutions.

A second break was the widening gap between reflationary and gold-defending economies. Britain and Japan had already moved away from gold and recovered faster. The United States joined the regime-change camp in 1933. France, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Switzerland remained more constrained. The global economy therefore became more fragmented but also began to move out of the trough in countries willing or forced to abandon gold orthodoxy.

A third change was the normalization of managed trade and exchange controls. The Roca-Runciman agreement, imperial preference, German controls, Latin American exchange allocation, and sterling-area arrangements all signaled a move away from pre-Depression openness. This was not a small cyclical adjustment; it was a structural shift in how trade and payments were governed.

In GDP ranking terms, the MPD 2023 top-20 membership was unchanged from 1932. The United States remained first, China second, the Former USSR third, India fourth, and the United Kingdom fifth. Belgium rose from 17th to 15th, above Canada and Czechoslovakia, while several recoveries were notable: Japan, Brazil, Australia, Germany, France, and Argentina grew faster in the MPD estimates. China, Canada, Czechoslovakia, Spain, Poland, the United States, Italy, and the Netherlands remained weak or contracting in the same construction.

Matched measures show the same uneven turn. The League of Nations weighted 21-country industrial index rose 12.82% (78 to 88) but industrial employment only 4.00% (75 to 78). United States industrial production rose 18.44% while BEA real GDP fell 1.24%, and United States CPI deflation eased from -9.9% to -5.1%. Official discount rates fell by 0.73 pp in Japan, 2 pp in Italy, 1 pp each in Poland and Czechoslovakia and 0.5 pp in New York, and ended unchanged in the United Kingdom, Germany, France, the Netherlands, Belgium, Spain, Switzerland and the Dutch East Indies. World crude-oil output rose 9.44% while the sterling price of crude petroleum fell 7.24%, and the Rio coffee quotation fell 22.44%.

**Non-market and colonial estimates:** the Former USSR, China, India, Indonesia, and other colonial or politically fragmented economies require special caution. The League of Nations country industrial indices are on national bases, such as 1928 for Italy, 1924/1927-1929 for the United Kingdom, net raw-material imports for the Netherlands and officially planned gross output for the USSR; they are not on the 1929=100 basis of the country figures in the 1932 report, so levels must not be compared across the two reports. The weighted 21-country index (1925-1929=100) also comes from a different League of Nations edition, with different coverage, from the world manufacturing and mining index in the 1932 report. The 1932 exchange-rate comparators for Germany, France, Italy, Spain, the Netherlands, Belgium, Canada, Australia and Switzerland come from the Macrohistory Database and differ from the League of Nations New York annual averages in the 1932 report, which had no New York quotation for Australia.

**Territorial definitions.** GDP growth for the former USSR is withheld because the national-income source behind the MPD record has not been matched to the territory of its population series; the raw record change is +4.10%. India corresponds to undivided India; Indonesia is the MPD Indonesia series, which covers the Dutch East Indies and includes Timor; and Germany corresponds to its 1936 frontiers. Levels and growth for 1932 and 1933 come from the same MPD 2023 vintage.
