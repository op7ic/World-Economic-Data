# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1960

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1960 was a late-postwar expansion year with two conflicting signals. On the surface, world output, industrial production, and trade were still expanding rapidly after the 1958 downturn. The World Bank WDI current-dollar series reports world GDP of about USD 1.37 trillion in 1960. IMF reporting for the year emphasized that world exports rose by roughly 12 percent in value from 1959 to 1960, with volume rising by almost as much. Yet this expansion was uneven. The United States entered a recession that began after the April 1960 business-cycle peak and ran into early 1961, while Western Europe and Japan were still growing quickly.

The divergence shows in contemporary estimates. The BIS put real GNP growth at 8.0% in West Germany (the Federal Republic), 7.3% in Italy and 2.6% in the United States; these are contemporary national-account estimates, not the same vintages, territories or concepts as the Maddison Project Database 2023 (MPD) real-GDP reconstructions used for growth in section 4. The UN export-quantum index rose from 139 to 154 on a 1953=100 base, about 10.79%, but that "world" excludes the listed centrally planned economies, including mainland China and the USSR. BIS trade turnover rose from US$208.3 billion to US$231.8 billion, or 11.28%, but turnover combines imports and exports and is not export volume. No single series gives an exactly measured, fully comprehensive world growth rate.

The dominant macro regime was Bretton Woods. Major currencies were fixed or managed against the U.S. dollar, and the dollar was formally linked to gold at USD 35 per troy ounce. This produced low headline inflation in most advanced economies, but it also exposed a growing structural weakness: the United States was simultaneously the reserve-center country and a deficit country. Gold outflows, balance-of-payments pressure, and debates about international liquidity were already visible in 1960, even though the 1971 breakdown of gold convertibility was still in the future. U.S. monetary easing contrasted with European restraint, creating incentives for international capital movements, and London gold quotations briefly reached about US$40 per fine ounce despite the official US$35 parity. This was a confidence shock within Bretton Woods, not its termination.

The year was also a turning point in energy and resource politics. OPEC was created at the Baghdad Conference in September 1960 by Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, and Venezuela. At the time, the institutional move looked modest beside the power of the international oil companies, but in retrospect it marked the start of producer-state coordination that would reshape the oil market in the 1970s. Commodity prices were generally not in a boom. IMF reporting described average primary-product prices as roughly near 1959 levels, with industrial raw materials firmer than food early in the year but weaker by year-end.

At the country level, the United States remained by far the largest economy. The Soviet Union was almost certainly second in real or purchasing-power terms, but its nominal current-dollar ranking is not directly comparable because the ruble was not a convertible market currency. Germany, the United Kingdom, France, China, Japan, Italy, Canada, and India formed the next tier. Japan's high-growth model accelerated under the political and policy setting that produced the Income Doubling Plan, while China was in the Great Leap Forward crisis, with severe disruption to agriculture and household welfare. The contrast between Japan's market-industrial takeoff and China's centrally planned crisis is one of the sharpest microeconomic contrasts of 1960.

China's food and demographic crisis was much more consequential for welfare than aggregate GDP alone reveals. Retrospective NBS and MPD estimates disagree materially about 1960 output growth, and neither administrative retail prices nor headline industrial output measure household deprivation adequately. Other institutional changes had long consequences without guaranteeing their later outcomes: EFTA entered into force on 3 May, the five OPEC founders met at Baghdad on 10-14 September, and Congo's independence on 30 June was followed by conflict and international intervention. OPEC's later quota system and 1970s bargaining power, the 1961 German and Dutch revaluations, and subsequent African political outcomes belong to later years.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1960

**Theme 1: Bretton Woods maturity and early strain.**

**Significance in economic history:** 1960 sat inside the fixed-exchange-rate system that shaped postwar trade, capital controls, and monetary policy. The dollar served as the reserve anchor, while gold convertibility at USD 35 per ounce was the symbolic foundation of confidence.

**Specific data/events in 1960:** The United States faced recession and balance-of-payments pressure at the same time. Monetary policy could not be set only for domestic output because low U.S. short-term rates risked worsening dollar and gold outflows. The Federal Reserve lowered its representative discount rate to 3%; Britain briefly raised Bank Rate to 6%; the Bundesbank raised and then reversed its rate. Capital inflows could undermine domestic restraint in the surplus countries. Canada's weekly market-linked Bank Rate and Australia's controlled lending ceilings show why one generic "policy rate" misdescribes this period.

**Countries most affected:** United States, United Kingdom, Germany, France, Japan, Switzerland, Canada, and major dollar-area commodity exporters.

**Forward linkage:** The liquidity problem, dollar overhang, and gold drain of the early 1960s led to the Gold Pool, wider capital controls, special drawing rights, and ultimately the collapse of dollar-gold convertibility in 1971.

**Theme 2: Divergence between U.S. cyclical weakness and non-U.S. industrial expansion.**

**Significance in economic history:** The postwar growth leadership of Western Europe and Japan became more visible. The U.S. was still dominant, but the recovery economies were catching up through manufacturing productivity, export expansion, and high investment.

**Specific data/events in 1960:** NBER dates show a U.S. recession beginning after the April 1960 peak. IMF reporting, however, shows world exports rising about 12 percent from 1959 to 1960 and trade among manufacturing countries rising especially quickly. The BIS estimated gross investment growth of 13.3% in West Germany, 18.1% in Italy and 19.6% in the Netherlands, against -0.7% in the United States. Equipment demand supported exports and imported inputs even while American industrial momentum weakened; recovery was possible but not assured at year-end.

**Countries most affected:** United States, Germany, France, Italy, Japan, Netherlands, Belgium, Sweden, Canada.

**Forward linkage:** The U.S. slowdown gave way to the Kennedy-Johnson expansion, while Europe and Japan continued high growth into the 1960s. The catch-up process became a defining feature of the decade.

**Theme 3: European trade integration and the EEC/EFTA divide.**

**Significance in economic history:** 1960 deepened Western Europe's institutional split between the European Economic Community, the "Inner Six," and the European Free Trade Association, the "Outer Seven." This shaped tariff policy, production networks, and business location decisions.

**Specific data/events in 1960:** EFTA was established by the Stockholm Convention, signed on 4 January 1960, with the objective of liberalizing trade among member states. IMF reporting noted that EEC countries' purchases from each other rose strongly in 1960. The BIS measured EEC trade turnover up 19.6% and EFTA's up 12.4%; these descriptive differences do not identify a causal tariff effect without controlling for demand and industrial composition.

**Countries most affected:** United Kingdom, Sweden, Switzerland, Austria, Denmark, Norway, Portugal, Germany, France, Italy, Netherlands, Belgium.

**Forward linkage:** The EFTA/EEC divide influenced British trade strategy, later British applications to join the EEC, and the reorganization of European industry around common-market access.

**Theme 4: Energy sovereignty begins to institutionalize.**

**Significance in economic history:** OPEC's creation was the clearest 1960 marker of the long shift from oil-company dominance toward producer-state bargaining power.

**Specific data/events in 1960:** OPEC was created in Baghdad in September 1960 by five founding states. Posted crude prices were still low by later standards, roughly around USD 3 per barrel for leading benchmark crude oils. OPEC's founding was a response to posted-price cuts and an institutional development, not a 1960 embargo. Decolonization also altered fiscal authority, ownership, aid and trade relationships, and Congo's crisis linked political sovereignty to mining revenues, Belgian finance and international security.

**Countries most affected:** Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, Venezuela, United States, United Kingdom, oil-importing Western Europe, Japan.

**Forward linkage:** OPEC's early years were institution-building years. Its full price-setting importance emerged later, especially in the 1970s oil shocks.

**Theme 5: Developmentalism, planning, and import substitution.**

**Significance in economic history:** Many large developing economies used state planning, import controls, industrial policy, and public investment as core growth tools. This was not a single model. India, Brazil, Mexico, Argentina, Turkey, and Spain all used different mixes of stabilization, protection, and infrastructure spending.

**Specific data/events in 1960:** India was in the Second Five-Year Plan period, Brazil was completing the Kubitschek developmental push and Brasilia era, Mexico was inside the "stabilizing development" model, Spain was emerging from the 1959 stabilization program, and Turkey was moving through political and macroeconomic stress after its 1958 stabilization. Mexico's public and private fixed investment expanded, India's machinery program increased industrial capacity but required imported food and inputs, and Brasília embodied Brazil's investment drive. Inflation, foreign-exchange access and debt service constrained these programs differently.

**Countries most affected:** India, Brazil, Mexico, Argentina, Turkey, Spain, South Africa.

**Forward linkage:** These policies produced mixed legacies: rapid industrialization in some cases, but also inflation, external bottlenecks, and later debt vulnerabilities.

**Theme 6: Centrally planned economies: high measured output, low statistical comparability.**

**Significance in economic history:** The Soviet bloc remained a huge share of global industrial capacity, but GDP measurement is particularly difficult because plan prices, administered exchange rates, and hidden quality differences distort comparisons.

**Specific data/events in 1960:** The Soviet Union ranked near the top globally under Maddison/PWT-style estimates, but nominal current-dollar conversion is not reliable. China was still recorded as a large economy in current-dollar series, but the Great Leap Forward caused major economic dislocation. Commodity abundance coexisted with household scarcity: preliminary oil and refined-copper output expanded while wheat and rice estimates fell, and exporter grain stocks and price-support systems coexisted with acute food deprivation in China. Aggregate availability, purchasing power, procurement and distribution were distinct constraints.

**Countries most affected:** Soviet Union, China, Eastern Europe.

**Forward linkage:** Retrospective estimates show that the apparent strength of central planning in the late 1950s and early 1960s masked large inefficiencies. China later moved toward reforms after 1978, while Soviet growth slowed sharply.

The main macro surprise was not a crash but a divergence. U.S. demand softened, imports fell, and the U.S. trade balance improved, while Europe and Japan maintained stronger industrial momentum. Primary-producing countries saw a less favorable pattern: import demand recovered, but export prices did not strengthen enough to prevent trade-balance pressure in many cases.

The UN export-quantum figure depends on its exclusion list and on rounded index precision, and the WDI world GDP aggregate is a later published historical series, not the sum of the country estimates in section 4. Industrial-country consumer inflation was generally modest, but no statistically justified global CPI average exists for 1960.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The United States entered 1960 with policy constrained by both domestic slowdown and external defense of the dollar. The Federal Reserve had tightened earlier and then faced the problem of easing into recession without intensifying gold outflows. The United Kingdom raised Bank Rate to 5 percent in January 1960, reflecting the classic sterling-area tension between growth, external balance, and exchange-rate credibility.

The BIS dated discount table records U.S. cuts to 3.5% on 10 June and 3% on 12 August; West German increases to 5% on 3 June and a reduction to 4% on 11 November; and UK changes to 5%, 6%, 5.5% and 5% on 21 January, 23 June, 27 October and 8 December. The Netherlands stayed at 3.5%. The Bank of Japan's facility schedule set the commercial-bill discount rate at 6.94% from 24 August, while overdrafts carried 8.03%; the JST Japanese short rate of 8.4% is a different instrument from that policy facility.

Germany's Bundesbank operated from a position of strength. The deutsche mark was undervalued relative to Germany's export competitiveness, and the DM was still at roughly 4.20 per U.S. dollar until the March 1961 revaluation. Japan maintained the fixed yen parity of 360 yen per U.S. dollar, which supported export planning and industrial investment. France operated with the new franc from January 1960, replacing 100 old francs with 1 new franc.

Credit quantities and regulation mattered as much as discount rates. Britain's special deposits, West German reserve measures and Australia's November lending restrictions operated through bank balance sheets. Australia's maximum individual overdraft interest rate increased from 6% to 7% on 17 November, with a separate maximum average lending rate. Fixed exchange parities did not eliminate controls, multiple rates, forward premiums or differences between official and parallel quotations.

Bond markets were still mostly domestic, regulated, and policy-influenced. Yield-curve data for many countries are either not available or not directly comparable. Equity-market data are much better for the United States and Japan than for most other markets. The DJIA closed 1960 down about 9 percent, while the Nikkei 225 rose sharply, reflecting Japan's accelerating growth cycle.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

World trade was a central growth engine. IMF reporting shows a strong 1960 increase in world exports, especially trade among manufacturing countries. The EEC countries increased intra-bloc purchases, while EFTA created a rival free-trade framework among the United Kingdom, Sweden, Switzerland, Austria, Denmark, Norway, and Portugal.

EFTA's Stockholm Convention was signed on 4 January and entered into force on 3 May. Its intergovernmental free-trade structure differed from the EEC customs union. The BIS describes widening import liberalization and increased European purchases of American aircraft, machinery, metals and textile inputs, channels through which European demand cushioned the U.S. slowdown.

Cold War structure affected technology trade, capital flows, and aid. Soviet bloc trade was institutionally separate from most market-economy trade, although not completely isolated. Decolonization also mattered economically. 1960 was the "Year of Africa" in political history, with many new African states entering international trade and aid systems. Belgium faced major political and economic uncertainty connected to Congo's independence.

The U.S. restriction on Cuban sugar purchases redirected trade rather than eliminating Cuban output: the BIS identifies increased purchases by the USSR and China, while Mexico benefited from additional U.S. sugar demand. Sanctions, official credits and bilateral arrangements therefore changed destinations as well as total flows. Sino-Soviet technical cooperation deteriorated, but an August 1960 U.S. intelligence memorandum still distinguished reported departures of Soviet technicians from proof of their scale or cause.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil was cheap and abundant by later standards, but bargaining power was changing. OPEC's creation was a structural event, not a 1960 price shock. The immediate oil market remained dominated by international oil companies and posted prices. Producer governments, however, were beginning to coordinate on tax, royalty, and price issues.

BIS preliminary production estimates for 1960 were 1,052 million metric tonnes of crude oil against 978 million in 1959, steel 340 against 305 million, refined copper 4,978 against 4,305 thousand tonnes, wheat 240 against 250 million and rough rice 250 against 260 million tonnes. The implied changes are +7.57%, +11.48%, +15.63%, -4.00% and -3.85%. These are contemporary estimates; Chinese agricultural components in particular are not final harvests. BIS crude-oil quotations differed by market: Bradford, Pennsylvania increased 9.1%, while the selected Venezuelan quote was unchanged, so a single world crude price or a modern Brent-type benchmark would be anachronistic. The EIA U.S. domestic first-purchase average of $2.88 per barrel (against $2.90 in 1959) is a nominal national annual average, not an international grade quotation or year-end price.

Gold was fixed at USD 35 per ounce under Bretton Woods. That price was not a free-market indicator of inflation expectations in the modern sense because official convertibility and exchange controls shaped the market. Gold's official parity coexisted with the London premium. The BIS estimated 33.6 million fine ounces produced outside the USSR, of which South Africa supplied 21.366 million; its estimate of US$1.035 billion of industrial and private uses is a residual, not a direct census of hoarding. Primary commodities were mixed. IMF reporting suggests average primary-product prices for 1960 were roughly near 1959 levels, with weakness later in the year. BIS within-year market changes were London copper -9.6%, Singapore natural rubber -26.6%, Winnipeg wheat +0.6% and London jute +86.2%.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor markets were structurally tight in much of Western Europe and Japan, supporting migration, overtime, and productivity-driven wage growth. West Germany relied increasingly on foreign labor recruitment in the early 1960s. In the United States, the recession weakened labor markets and unemployment rose into 1961. The Bank of Canada described an increase in employment insufficient to match labor-force growth and a longer structural shift toward services and female employment. India's employment-exchange live register increased from 1.42 million to about 1.61 million, but registrations omit much informal and agricultural work and cannot supply a national unemployment rate.

Demographically, the postwar baby boom was still reshaping school demand, housing, and consumer durables in advanced economies. Urbanization continued across Latin America, India, China, and parts of Africa. The microeconomic effect was a shift in consumption toward housing, appliances, transport, and education in richer countries, and toward basic infrastructure and food security in poorer economies. The UN 2024 population estimates put world population at 3,015,470,894 on 1 July 1960, against 2,965,950,351 in 1959, a computed rise of 1.67%.

Retrospective NBS reporting places China's crude death rate at 25.4 per 1,000 in 1960, against 14.6 in 1959, and the birth rate at 20.9 per 1,000. These rates document a demographic emergency but do not by themselves identify a famine excess-death total or its causes. The official retrospective attributes the period to natural disasters; broader historical scholarship also examines procurement, collectivization and policy failures, and no single causal account is treated as settled here.

**Economic narrative:**

The U.S. was still the global economic center, but 1960 was a cyclical transition year. The recession that began after the April peak reflected weaker durable-goods demand, inventory adjustment, and earlier monetary tightening. The downturn was mild compared with later recessions, but it mattered globally because U.S. imports from other manufacturing countries fell and because U.S. external balance concerns constrained policy.

Microeconomically, the U.S. consumer economy was changing. Domestic automobile producers faced foreign competition and a shift toward compact cars. Housing and suburban infrastructure still supported durable-goods demand, but recession-sensitive sectors such as autos, steel, and machinery weakened.

The slowdown began within a year that still showed positive annual output growth. NBER retrospectively dates the cycle peak to April 1960 and the trough to February 1961; that dating is hindsight, not a contemporary forecast. BEA real GDP of 3,500.272 billion chained 2017 dollars against 3,412.421 billion in 1959 gives about 2.57%, distinct from MPD's 2.49% and the BIS contemporary GNP estimate of 2.6%. Federal Reserve industrial-production data give annual growth of 2.26%, and the BLS published annual CPI of 1.7% differs from the 1.49% JST index ratio; the vintages are not mixed.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Autos, steel, consumer appliances, petroleum, aerospace, electronics, and finance remained central. IBM and other computing firms were moving business data processing into large corporations. Commercial aviation continued the jet-age transition. Banking was stable but heavily regulated, and real estate remained tied to suburbanization rather than speculative global capital flows. Micro transmission ran through weaker inventory and investment demand, easier bank reserves and a more favorable export market in Europe. Newspaper asking-price house indices rose 10.03% in Los Angeles and 3.41% in New York City, but fell 0.17% in Chicago (section 6D).

### 5B. Corporate landscape

A globally comparable 1960 ranking of the largest companies by revenue or market capitalization is not available in this assessment. Large corporations by economic importance included General Motors, Standard Oil of New Jersey, Royal Dutch/Shell, Texaco, U.S. Steel, IBM, AT&T, Unilever, Siemens, Volkswagen, Fiat, Sony, Matsushita, Toyota, Hitachi, and major state-linked energy or industrial firms. This list is illustrative, not a ranked table. State enterprises, consolidated multinational groups and listed parent companies cannot be ranked together without specifying currency, consolidation, accounting year and valuation date.

Corporate finance was less globalized than today. Mergers and acquisitions occurred, but the giant cross-border M&A market of later decades did not exist. National champions, regulated utilities, banks, and vertically integrated manufacturers dominated. In developing economies, state enterprises and licensed private firms were major mechanisms of industrial policy.

Three quantified corporate mechanisms stand out. Toyota's production expansion measures physical vehicles, not revenue or profit. Volkswagen's 1960 legal privatization steps preceded the 1961 share offering. Mexico's acquisition of foreign electricity enterprises transferred ownership and required external payments; the central bank reports US$137.3 million of exceptional payments combining utility purchases and early repayment of old foreign debt, not the utility purchase price alone. The BIS also identifies £131 million temporarily held in London by Ford of Detroit pending purchase of the British company's outside minority, an acquisition-related capital flow rather than proof that the purchase closed in 1960. A complete global IPO list, bankruptcy list and consistent industry-concentration measures are not available, and their absence does not mean that no failures or new listings occurred.

### 5C. Innovation & technology

The economic frontier in 1960 was shaped by computing, electronics, jet aviation, chemicals, nuclear energy, and materials science. Mainframe computing moved deeper into banking, insurance, government, and large manufacturing firms. The first laser demonstration in 1960 had long-term implications, although its immediate commercial impact was limited. Integrated circuits were still early, but the pathway toward miniaturized electronics was becoming visible. NIST's institutional history dates Theodore Maiman's working ruby laser at Hughes to May 1960; its later industrial and medical importance is hindsight, and no 1960 commercial revenue effect is claimed.

Infrastructure megaprojects mattered more immediately than laboratory breakthroughs in many countries. Highways, ports, power generation, steel plants, dams, and new cities created demand for construction, heavy machinery, cement, and engineering services. Brasília's 21 April inauguration is a dated infrastructure milestone, although completion of a capital city is not synonymous with completion of every building or ministry transfer. Indian capacity additions in machine tools, electrical machinery, chemicals and consumer appliances provide more immediate signs of technology diffusion into production. Aggregate R&D spending, patent counts and a cross-country megaproject cost panel are not available for 1960.

### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Sectoral employment continued to move from agriculture into manufacturing and services. This shift was fastest in Japan, Italy, Spain, Brazil, Mexico, and parts of Western Europe. In the United States, the recession produced cyclical labor weakness but did not reverse the long-term move toward services and white-collar work.

Human-capital investment expanded through universities, technical institutes, and vocational training. Japan's Income Doubling Plan explicitly linked growth to education and living standards. Europe's social-market and welfare-state models treated skilled labor as a core industrial asset.

India's Economic Survey recorded average cotton-mill employment of 769,000 in January-September against 760,000 in the matched 1959 period, and its registered applicants included many without vocational training or previous work experience; the employment-exchange sample is not the whole labor force. Canada's central bank separated slow industrial employment growth from expanding services and increasing female participation, warning against treating unemployment as only a short cyclical fluctuation. The BIS associated European wage rises with exceptional productivity gains and reduced import restrictions rather than assuming that all wage increases passed through to prices. A harmonized skilled/unskilled wage panel, educational attainment, union membership, strike statistics and migration-flow totals are not available on a comparable basis.

### 6B. Fixed income

Government bond yield data for 1960 are uneven and not directly comparable across countries. The United States and United Kingdom had developed government bond markets, but official rate management, capital controls, and banking regulations shaped yields. In the United Kingdom, short rates rose after Bank Rate moved to 5 percent. In the United States, monetary easing was constrained by concern over gold outflows.

JST long-term rates were 4.12% for the United States, 6.39% for West Germany, 5.40% for the UK, 6.16% for France, 5.01% for Italy, 4.21% for the Netherlands, 4.57% for Sweden and 5.19% for Canada. Their national bond samples and maturities differ, so this is not a harmonized ten-year government curve. Japan's JST entry of 10.1% is not labelled a ten-year sovereign yield because its instrument lineage is unresolved.

The Bank of Canada's annual report supplies a dated curve instead: on 28 December the 91-day bill yielded 3.25%, the September 1965 bond 4.87%, the September 1972 bond 5.22% and the October 1979 bond 5.19%. These are actual remaining maturities, not constant-maturity points. The industrial-bond basket yielded 5.61%, but it uses the nearest first business day of a month, so subtracting a government bond of different duration would not give a matched credit spread. Sovereign default coverage and corporate spread panels are partial.

### 6C. Currencies

The key currency fact was fixed parity. The U.S. dollar was the reserve anchor. The pound was at USD 2.80. The yen was at 360 per dollar. The deutsche mark was about 4.20 per dollar until the 1961 revaluation. The Italian lira was about 625 per dollar. The Mexican peso was fixed at 12.50 per dollar. Many developing economies used exchange controls, multiple rates, or administered parities, so published rates often understate scarcity of foreign exchange.

Official parity, annual-average market rates and parallel quotations are distinct. JST's Japan 380 and Spain 61.5 units per dollar do not contradict the official 360 and 60 when they refer to different markets. The Dutch guilder parity was 3.80 per dollar before the March 1961 revaluation. France's new franc was a redenomination; the Soviet reform and the German and Dutch revaluations took effect in 1961. Canada floated, with an annual official-market average of 0.96977 Canadian dollars per U.S. dollar. WDI's historical observations for Mexico (0.0125), Brazil and Poland (0.0004) are expressed in later currency units and are not contemporary 1960 transaction quotes.

The dollar remained the central monetary reserve currency while sterling balances also increased. The BIS sterling table excludes Ford's temporary £131 million from its adjusted year-end total. Reserve composition shares for the whole world are not available; gold, dollar claims, official reserves and private bank positions are different stocks.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

1. **U.S. recession:** Knowable in real time as industrial and labor weakness. Its global effect came through weaker imports, policy uncertainty, and dollar concerns. North American recession coincided with gold outflows and international interest differentials.

2. **Bretton Woods dollar-gold vulnerability:** Partly visible in 1960. Hindsight shows it was the beginning of a long systemic problem, but the exact 1971 endpoint was not inevitable from the 1960 perspective. The London premium exposed doubts about the reserve system without establishing that a dollar devaluation was inevitable, and tightening and liberalization could conflict when foreign inflows replenished domestic bank liquidity.

3. **China's Great Leap Forward crisis:** Severe disruption was already occurring. The depth of the demographic and welfare consequences became clearer later, and statistical uncertainty remains high. The mortality shock and food distress are documented independently of the disputed GDP path, but an excess-death count or a single-cause explanation cannot be derived from one crude-rate series.

4. **Oil producer coordination:** OPEC did not immediately create a price shock, but hindsight identifies 1960 as a major institutional turning point. A U.S. diplomatic memorandum of 21 November 1960 still considered OPEC's effectiveness uncertain and its secretariat location unresolved; a future oil shock was a possibility, not an observed 1960 outcome.

5. **Decolonization and political risk:** Congo's independence crisis affected Belgium and mining-finance expectations. Wider African decolonization changed aid, trade, and investment relationships. Congo became independent on 30 June and Security Council action on 14 July followed the crisis; mining revenues, ownership and foreign intervention were intertwined. Lumumba's death and later phases of the conflict belong to 1961 or beyond, and no aggregate lost-output estimate is assigned.

6. **Latin American inflation and external constraints:** Brazil and Argentina showed the vulnerability of development models financed by credit expansion and protected markets. Multiple exchange markets complicated investment finance.

7. **European bloc fragmentation:** The EEC/EFTA split created uncertainty about Britain's long-term trade strategy and European industrial organization.

8. **Late credit restraint in Australia:** The November 1960 measures exposed indebted firms and households to refinancing pressure.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1960 vs. 1959)

World trade accelerated sharply relative to 1959, with IMF reporting about 12 percent value growth in exports from 1959 to 1960. However, momentum weakened late in the year as activity slowed in several industrial countries. In the same UN vintage, export-quantum growth accelerated from 7.75% in 1959 (139/129) to 10.79% in 1960 (154/139), about 3.04 percentage points, within its restricted geography.

The United States shifted from recovery into recession. This changed the pattern of world trade: U.S. imports fell, while U.S. exports to some markets improved. Europe and Japan were still expanding, so the global economy did not move in lockstep with the U.S. cycle. MPD's U.S. growth decelerated 4.92 points to 2.49%, while Japan accelerated 4.01 points to 13.12%.

Europe's institutional landscape changed. EFTA was created in 1960, deepening the contrast with the EEC. Intra-EEC trade became increasingly important, while the United Kingdom's position outside the EEC became a larger strategic issue.

Energy politics changed structurally with OPEC's formation. The 1960 oil market itself remained calm, but producer coordination was new and important. Commodity supply expanded faster than some prices: preliminary oil output rose 7.57% and refined copper 15.63%, while the selected London copper quote fell 9.6% during the year.

China's Great Leap Forward moved from ambitious mobilization toward visible economic crisis. The shift was especially severe in agriculture and household welfare. The crude death rate rose from 14.6 to 25.4 per 1,000.

Spain moved from the immediate shock of its 1959 stabilization toward the conditions for the 1960s growth acceleration. Brazil moved toward the end of the Kubitschek era with infrastructure achievements but rising inflation and external stress. India's industrial momentum accelerated in matched January-October comparisons, but its 1959/60 agricultural output weakened. São Paulo and Buenos Aires price indices document different inflation paths, not a single regional average. Institutional breaks also included the new franc's unit change and newly independent African states, while gold-market stress and worsening Sino-Soviet cooperation added risks.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

**High-confidence data:** World Bank WDI/API current-dollar GDP for market economies with national accounts coverage; OPEC founding date and founding members; NBER U.S. business-cycle dates; Bretton Woods institutional rules; EFTA founding date and objective; dated central-bank discount-rate schedules; UN table definitions and exchange attribution; Indian partial-period distinctions; Mexican investment and output observations. Accurate transcription does not make every underlying estimate accurate.

**Medium-confidence data:** Population and GDP per capita figures used in country headers, because they are rounded and in some cases depend on postwar territorial definitions. Latin American GDP and inflation comparisons are medium-confidence because exchange controls and data revisions can change the interpretation. IMF world trade aggregates for 1958 to 1960 and the UN export-quantum and BIS turnover series are medium-confidence because of their coverage exclusions and definitions. MPD territorial GDP growth, JST historical debt and current-account ratios, reconstructed unemployment and national asset returns are conditional on their construction.

**Low-confidence data:** Soviet Union current-dollar GDP, China GDP and sectoral performance during the Great Leap Forward, Eastern bloc GDP rankings, unemployment comparisons across almost all non-U.S. countries, government debt/GDP, budget balance/GDP, and current-account balance/GDP for many countries.

**Historical data conflicts:** WDI current-dollar GDP, Maddison Project historical GDP, and Penn World Table real-output measures answer different questions. WDI current-dollar GDP reflects official national accounts and exchange-rate conversion; Maddison and PWT are designed for long-run real-income comparisons. For non-market economies, current-dollar conversion can be misleading. In this assessment, GDP levels, population and per-capita cells are WDI current U.S. dollars, while growth rates are MPD 2023 real GDP growth (in 2011 international dollars) unless labelled otherwise; MPD levels appear only in parentheses and are not converted into current dollars. Contemporary BIS, Banco de México and NBS estimates differ from MPD growth and are kept separate rather than averaged. Germany's GDP territory is wider than the West German (FRG) series used for its policy data. Soviet and Czechoslovak planning statistics are not market-sector GDP, and city price indices are not national CPI. BLS, WDI, JST and contemporary UN series differ in baskets, precision, vintage and chaining.

**Licensing:** Values marked JST come from the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database (Release 6) and its rate-of-return data, used under the Creative Commons CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 licence; those terms apply to the values themselves.

**Weakest claims in this assessment:** Borderline top-20 ranking near positions 18 to 20; exact sectoral growth claims outside the United States, Japan, and Western Europe; corporate-size comparisons; financial-market returns outside the U.S. and Japan.

**Important missing data:** Comparable 1960 unemployment rates; comparable 1960 policy rates for all top 20; complete global corporate market-cap rankings; standardized government-debt and budget-balance ratios; reliable real-estate prices; high-frequency bond-market yields for most countries; and consistent Soviet bloc national accounts in market terms. There is also no independent same-basis top-20 ranking, no complete global real GDP or inflation aggregate, incomplete fiscal and current-account coverage, no fully matched ten-year bond and credit-spread panel, incomplete exchange closing returns, and no global company ranking, IPO or failure census, or R&D and patent panel. A blank cell in a retrospective database means the value is absent from that source and vintage, not that no statistic existed in 1960.
