# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1956

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1956 was a late-expansion year in the postwar boom. The world economy continued to grow, but the rhythm shifted from the extraordinary reconstruction surge of the early 1950s to a more mature cycle shaped by capacity limits, inflation pressure, and tighter credit. Summing Maddison-based country records while avoiding double-counting of Soviet and Yugoslav successor territories gives approximate world output of $11,386.6 billion in 2011 international dollars and approximate growth of 4.7% from 1955. This is an indicative sum of country records, not a certified world total and not an official global national account; a defensible complete-world GDP level and growth rate are Data not available.

The United States remained the system's dominant economy, but its growth slowed from the very strong 1955 rebound. Western Europe also slowed, although Germany, France, Italy, the Netherlands, Belgium, and Sweden remained in the broad postwar expansion. Japan was entering the high-growth phase that would define the next decade, and its 1956 policy narrative explicitly shifted from postwar recovery to modernization. India began the Second Five-Year Plan, Brazil began the Kubitschek development drive, and Mexico continued its stabilization and import-substitution model.

The year's biggest shock was Suez. The military and political crisis interrupted oil logistics, pressured sterling, and exposed the limits of British and French economic power under the Bretton Woods system. The crisis did not create a global depression, but it showed how payments confidence, oil transit, and alliance politics could interact. Meanwhile, the Hungarian and Polish crises revealed economic tensions inside the Soviet bloc, including consumer shortages and pressure for some decentralization.

At the micro level, several 1956 events look larger in hindsight than they may have appeared at the time. The U.S. interstate highway program, IBM's RAMAC storage system, the first commercial container-ship voyage of the Ideal-X, and the TAT-1 transatlantic telephone cable all pointed toward future transformations in logistics, data processing, communications, suburbanization, and corporate scale. The year therefore combined a maturing postwar macro cycle with early technological and infrastructure foundations for later globalization.

Contemporary BIS/OEEC estimates put Western European real GNP growth at 3.6%, after 6.0% in 1955, and U.S. growth at 2.4%, after 7.2%. Those are contemporary GNP estimates, not today's GDP series. The UN export-quantum index increased from 114 to 124, approximately 8.77%, but excluded the Soviet Union, mainland China and several other centrally planned economies.

Suez was an actual late-year transport and payments shock, not merely a geopolitical precursor to later oil crises. Shipping rates rose and European petroleum supply was constrained. Britain's reserves increased only $13 million over the whole year after exceptional receipts; the BIS estimated a $725 million drain without the oil-company sale proceeds and the IMF drawing. Britain nevertheless recorded a current-account surplus. Reserve pressure therefore cannot be equated with a current-account deficit, nor can emergency conditions extending into early 1957 be assigned wholly to 1956.

Development policy remained geographically diverse. India's Industrial Policy Resolution expanded state responsibility while retaining a defined private-sector role. Brazil legally established the vehicle for constructing its new capital, rather than completing Brasília in 1956. Mexico's central bank documented strong manufacturing, construction and credit expansion alongside weather-damaged agriculture. Indonesia experienced an external deficit and repudiated Dutch debts. These episodes prevent a U.S.-centered monetary narrative from standing in for the entire world economy.

Industrial and financial innovation also mattered. Ford's public share sale, IBM's disk-storage system, transatlantic telephone infrastructure and container experimentation changed financing or operating possibilities, but their eventual productivity effects are hindsight, not measured 1956 global growth contributions. China illustrates the largest statistical warning: the MPD/Wu reconstruction gives 8.49% growth, while the National Bureau of Statistics' 2016 restated national accounts give 15.0%. These are different reconstructions, not independent confirmations of one number.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1956

**Theme 1: The postwar boom cooled but did not break**

The major Atlantic economies were still expanding, but the speed changed. BIS described 1956 as a year in which general economic expansion continued while the rate of advance slowed in much of Western Europe and North America. Its report estimated Western European real GNP growth at 3.6% in 1956 compared with 6.0% in 1955, and U.S. real GNP growth at 2.4% compared with 7.2%. The significance is that 1956 was not a crisis year in output terms, but it was a transition away from rapid postwar rebound toward capacity-management macroeconomics.

Countries most affected included the United States, United Kingdom, France, Germany, Canada, and the Netherlands. The forward linkage was the 1957 to 1958 downturn, when earlier tightening and inventory-cycle weakness became more visible.

**Theme 2: Bretton Woods remained strong, but sterling looked vulnerable**

The global monetary system remained centered on fixed but adjustable parities, dollar reserves, capital controls, and IMF oversight. The U.S. dollar was linked to gold at $35 per troy ounce, and most major currencies were pegged to the dollar. IMF reporting emphasized the improvement of the world payments situation and the role of U.S. official spending abroad in supplying dollars to the rest of the world.

The United Kingdom was the key weak point. Sterling still had reserve-currency functions, but Suez triggered a credibility shock. This linked imperial geopolitics with capital-market confidence. The forward linkage was the continuing reduction of sterling's global role and the clearer dominance of the dollar within Bretton Woods.

**Theme 3: Suez made oil transit and payments confidence a macro issue**

The Suez crisis showed how a transport chokepoint could become a macroeconomic shock even before the 1970s oil-price era. Oil prices did not behave like the later OPEC shocks; the WTI spot series was stable at about $2.82 per barrel during 1956. The shock was more about physical routing, shipping costs, refinery logistics, and sterling pressure than a sustained global posted-price explosion.

Countries most affected were the United Kingdom, France, Egypt, oil importers in Western Europe, and shipping-intensive economies such as the Netherlands. The forward linkage was direct: the 1956 crisis became a reference point for later energy-security planning.

**Theme 4: Developmentalism accelerated in Asia and Latin America**

Japan, India, Brazil, and Mexico illustrate different versions of developmentalism. Japan's model combined fixed exchange rates, high savings, banks, industrial policy, and export-oriented productivity catch-up. India emphasized planning, public-sector heavy industry, and capital goods under the Second Five-Year Plan. Brazil's Kubitschek administration launched a development agenda centered on energy, transport, industry, and the new capital. Mexico pursued exchange-rate stability and state-guided industrialization.

The significance is that 1956 was not only a North Atlantic story. Large developing and middle-income economies were building institutions and industrial structures that shaped later global trade and capital flows.

**Theme 5: Centrally planned economies grew quickly, but stress became visible**

Maddison estimates show fast growth for the USSR, China, and Poland, but these figures have lower confidence than market-economy estimates. BIS noted that centrally planned economies continued expanding, especially in heavy industry, while also facing tensions that required revising production and trade plans. The Hungarian and Polish crises had economic roots, especially living standards, shortages, and heavy-industry priorities.

The forward linkage was the long-running socialist-bloc dilemma: high measured investment and industrial output could coexist with weak consumer welfare, low price information, and political stress.

**Theme 6: Infrastructure and technology planted micro foundations for later globalization**

Several 1956 events were microeconomic inflection points. The U.S. interstate program authorized a 41,000-mile network and created long-term demand for construction, vehicles, logistics, retail, and suburban real estate. The Ideal-X voyage demonstrated containerization's cost-saving potential. IBM's RAMAC storage system changed the economics of business data access. TAT-1 began transatlantic telephone cable service.

The immediate macro effect was limited, but the forward linkage was enormous. These were mechanisms through which later productivity, corporate scale, supply-chain integration, and consumer-market geography would change.

**Theme 7: Expansion slowed unevenly**

North Atlantic output still grew, but passenger-car production weakened sharply in the United States and Britain while rising in France, West Germany and Italy. Investment pressure coexisted with consumer-durable weakness; a universal industrial recession is the wrong description.

**Theme 8: Credit restraint was instrument-specific**

The United States, Britain, West Germany, Canada and the Netherlands tightened discount rates or lending conditions. Canada changed the rule linking Bank Rate to Treasury bills. These instruments cannot be read as modern, comparable overnight-policy targets.

**Theme 9: External balances redistributed monetary pressure**

West German surpluses and reserve gains contrasted with French and Dutch deficits. Canadian foreign borrowing financed investment-related imports. Sterling's late-year difficulties involved capital movements and trade-payment timing as well as Suez.

**Theme 10: State-led industrialization did not mean a single institutional model**

India's mixed-sector resolution, Brazil's NOVACAP financing authority, Mexico's public and private investment and Soviet-bloc planning differed in ownership, funding and household consequences. Authorizations and plan targets are not realized output.

**Theme 11: Transport vulnerability became visible**

Suez aggravated freight costs and petroleum logistics. New container and telephone systems offered different longer-run ways to move goods and information, but were not immediate substitutes for the canal.

**Theme 12: Measured prices concealed different experiences**

Britain's inflation, São Paulo's much faster cost-of-living increase, Mexico's worker basket and Poland's administered-price stability refer to different populations and institutions. Shortages, housing controls and wage arrangements complicate comparisons of household welfare.

Global trade continued to expand. The UN export-quantum index is the best available summary of trade volume, but it is not a harmonized all-country measure because the Soviet bloc and mainland China are excluded. Contemporary IMF and BIS reporting emphasized continued trade and payments improvement compared with the immediate postwar era, while also noting payments disequilibria and the vulnerability of primary-commodity exporters. The top 20 economies accounted for a very large share of world output, led by the United States, USSR, China, Germany, and the United Kingdom.

Reserve-currency dynamics were still those of Bretton Woods: the U.S. dollar was the central reserve asset, gold was officially priced at $35 per troy ounce, and sterling remained important but vulnerable. Inflation was not a runaway global problem, but it was persistent enough that central banks in the United States, United Kingdom, and Germany maintained or strengthened restrictive policies.

Commodity price data are uneven. WTI crude oil was about $2.82 per barrel across 1956 in the FRED historical series. Gold remained officially fixed at $35 per troy ounce. Agricultural production was relatively flat in Western Europe and the United States according to BIS, while industrial production continued to drive output growth.

International capital flows remained heavily controlled compared with later decades. The broad direction was still U.S. dollar outflows through government spending, aid, military procurement, and private investment, alongside intra-European payments liberalization and reconstruction-linked capital formation.

**Regional trade and capital channels.** The trade expansion is visible outside Western Europe: North American quantum rose 100 to 116 and Latin American 106 to 116, while the sterling-area Far Eastern independent-country group fell 111 to 110. Geographic definitions follow the source, not modern regional groupings. Long-term funds flowed toward Canadian resource development; contemporaneous BIS analysis also identified short-term flows into Germany and away from sterling. IBRD calendar 1956 loan authorizations of $507 million differed from disbursements of $289 million. Neither is an aggregate of all international capital flows.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

Monetary policy was generally cautious to restrictive in the major market economies. In the United States, the Federal Reserve discount rate moved from 2.50% at the end of 1955 to 2.75% on 13 April 1956 and 3.00% on 24 August. In the United Kingdom, Bank Rate was raised to 5.5% on 16 February and stayed there for the rest of the year. In West Germany, the Bank deutscher Länder raised its discount rate to 4.5% on 8 March and 5.5% on 19 May, then reduced it to 5% on 6 September. In Japan, the BOJ official discount rate for commercial bills was 7.30% during 1956 and did not rise until 1957. Canada's year-end Bank Rate was 3.92%; from 1 November its weekly value followed the latest Treasury-bill auction rate plus 0.25 percentage point. The Bank of Canada's report dates announcements one day before the effective dates tabulated by BIS.

The exchange-rate system was Bretton Woods: fixed or managed parities, exchange controls in many countries, and limited convertibility. Canada was an exception among major economies because its dollar floated from 1950 to 1962. Many developing and socialist economies had official or multiple exchange rates that are not comparable with market rates.

Credit conditions tightened most visibly in the United States, Britain, and Germany. Hire-purchase restrictions transmitted restraint directly to households: Britain tightened terms in February; Sweden required 40% automobile down payments; the Netherlands tightened instalment conditions. Discount rates, Treasury-bill yields, longer bonds and retail borrowing costs are separate measures.

Stock-market data are best for the United States: the Dow Jones Industrial Average ended 1956 at 499.47, a modest price gain from 1955, while S&P 500 total-return tables show a positive but much smaller result than the 1954 to 1955 surge. Japanese equities were rising enough for the Nikkei 225 to cross 500 in June 1956. Section 6A reports national equity reconstructions and industrial share-price averages for other markets without confusing them with exchange-wide returns.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

GATT remained the key multilateral trade framework, but trade was still shaped by balance-of-payments restrictions, import licensing, and capital controls. Western Europe was liberalizing gradually, while the Soviet bloc maintained managed trade and the developing world used import controls to manage foreign exchange.

The Suez crisis was the major geopolitical economic event. It affected oil transit, shipping, insurance, sterling, and the credibility of British and French power. Egypt's 26 July nationalization of the Suez Canal Company preceded the 29 October Israeli attack and the subsequent Anglo-French intervention; the UN's institutional response and the financial response evolved through November and December. The Hungarian uprising and Polish crisis affected East-West perceptions and the internal economic politics of the socialist bloc.

Regional integration in Europe was moving toward the 1957 Treaty of Rome, but the European Economic Community did not yet exist in 1956. The 21 April Spaak report supplied a basis for common-market and atomic-energy discussions. A new International Wheat Agreement took effect on 1 August, with 8 million metric tons of quotas and a $1.50-2.00 per-bushel range; Britain remained outside. Indonesia's August repudiation of Dutch debts and its new export bonuses illustrate bilateral tensions and exchange controls beyond Europe. ### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Oil remained inexpensive by later standards, with WTI around $2.82 per barrel, but the Suez crisis revealed vulnerability in the transport system. OPEC did not yet exist. Middle Eastern oil production and European import dependence were already important, but pricing power still sat largely with major oil companies and consuming-country systems rather than a producer cartel.

BIS reported world oil production more than 7% above 1955, including Middle East +6.2%, Venezuela +15% and United States +5.4%. These annual production figures do not negate the late-year European supply disruption. From late October 1956 through February 1957, deliveries, European output and stock withdrawals together averaged about 80% of normal European petroleum needs. That is a cross-year emergency interval, not a 20% calendar-1956 production decline.

**U.S. oil-price comparator.** The U.S. Energy Information Administration's domestic crude first-purchase annual average was $2.79 per barrel in 1956, versus $2.77 in 1955, a +0.722% change. These nominal national annual averages are not an international grade quotation, December close, freely traded world spot price or investor holding-period return, and they share one Census/EIA lineage rather than providing independent corroboration of other U.S. price series.

Metals demand remained linked to industrial production, construction, autos, and heavy industry. A comparable global annual copper price for 1956 is Data not available. London copper peaked at £437 per long ton on 19 March; the fall to £236 by end-March 1957 is not a 1956 return.

Agriculture was mixed: output did not strongly drive 1956 growth in the United States and Western Europe, and FAO reporting emphasized the continuing importance of agricultural trade, surpluses, and uneven commodity conditions. U.S. government cotton liquidation altered international supplies: by year-end 6.2 million bales had been sold, and the export quotation around 25 U.S. cents per pound plus freight differed from domestic supported prices. Chicago wheat was around $2.40 per bushel at the year-end/early-1957 boundary, compared with $2.10 at end-1955. These are specific quotations, not interchangeable annual-average commodity indices; comparable annual oil, copper and grain-price panels are Data not available.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

The global labor story was demographic expansion, urbanization, and sectoral movement from agriculture into industry and services. The postwar baby boom continued to reshape household formation and future school demand in North America and parts of Europe. Japan and Western Europe were moving labor into manufacturing and construction. India, China, Indonesia, and much of Latin America still had very large agricultural labor forces. UN World Population Prospects population figures are a later demographic reconstruction, not a simultaneous 1956 census.

Unemployment comparisons are weak. Modern labor-force-survey definitions were not consistently used, and socialist economies did not report unemployment in a comparable market sense; rates in planned economies are not assumed to be zero. Wage growth versus productivity became a central issue in Western Europe. Migration mattered strongly in Canada, Australia, and Western Europe, while internal migration shaped Brazil, Mexico, Japan, and Italy.

Mexico's central bank reported Federal District minimum-wage increases of 21% for urban and 18.8% for rural workers from 1 January, while its worker living-cost annual average rose 4.8%. That does not establish every worker's real-wage increase or a productivity gain. Hungary's uprising began on 23 October and Soviet intervention intensified on 4 November. The often-cited figure of approximately 200,000 refugees covers the crisis and ensuing months, including 1957, and is not a calendar-1956 migration total. **Economic narrative:**

The United States remained the anchor of the 1956 world economy. MPD places its real PPP output near 2.94 trillion 2011 international dollars, far ahead of every other economy. Growth slowed sharply from the very strong 1955 upswing, matching the contemporary view that North America had moved to a more moderate pace. Monetary policy leaned restrictive as the Federal Reserve raised the discount rate twice.

The micro story was a split between still-strong household capitalism and sector slowdowns. Autos softened after the 1955 boom, while housing, consumer durables, retail credit, and suburban infrastructure remained powerful. The Federal-Aid Highway Act, IBM RAMAC, and the first commercial container-ship voyage pointed toward later transformations in construction, computing, logistics, and suburban retail.

BEA observations give nominal GDP of $449.353 billion and real GDP of 3,148.765 billion chained 2017 dollars, up 2.13%, against the MPD estimate of 1.95%. Contemporary BIS U.S. GNP growth of 2.4% is a third vintage and concept, not a failed reproduction of BEA. The Federal Reserve industrial-production annual mean rose from 20.5412 to 21.429825, or 4.33%, while BIS passenger-car output averaged 485,000 a month against 660,000 in 1955, so autos cannot represent all of industry.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Leading sectors: consumer durables, construction, finance, oil, autos, electronics, logistics. Notable 1956 events: Interstate Highway Act, IBM RAMAC, Ideal-X container voyage. The National Interstate and Defense Highways Act was signed on 29 June; its 41,000-mile network and approximately $25 billion federal authorization concerned fiscal years 1957-69, not spending incurred in 1956. The Ford Foundation sold 10,216,381 Ford Motor shares, about 22% of its holdings, for net proceeds of $640,725,445, with public trading from 26 January; this was diversification by an existing shareholder, not new money raised by Ford. RAMAC's original capacity was five million encoded characters. Philadelphia Fed historical asking-price indices rose 3.65% in New York City and 38.74% in Los Angeles. No comprehensive 1956 household confidence or saving-rate panel is available.

**Economic narrative:**

The USSR was the second-largest economy in the MPD ranking, but the number is a reconstruction for a non-market system. The Sixth Five-Year Plan opened in 1956 and still emphasized heavy industry, energy, steel, and military-linked engineering. Output estimates show rapid growth, but plan prices and data opacity make comparison with market GDP uncertain.

The key 1956 innovations with economic implications were business computing storage, container shipping, interstate highways, and transatlantic telephone cable service. ### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

Sectoral employment continued shifting toward manufacturing and services in high-growth economies. ### 6C. Currencies

The currency system was still Bretton Woods. The dollar-gold link at $35 per ounce anchored parities. Sterling's fixed parity came under pressure during Suez: sterling traded around $2.80-2.81 during much of the first half and near its lower support region after the canal nationalization. Canada was unusual because its dollar floated, and it appreciated during the year. Many developing countries and socialist economies used controls or multiple official rates, making simple market comparisons misleading.

Japan's official 360 and parallel 402 yen per USD are not rival measurements of one freely tradable rate. French new-franc-unit reconstructions must be multiplied by 100 for 1956 old francs. National housing series are not assigned to London, Paris, Berlin or Tokyo.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The active crisis of the year was Suez. It created oil-transit disruption, sterling pressure, and a geopolitical rupture among Western allies. The risk that materialized was not a global depression, but a financial confidence shock. It showed that reserve-currency credibility could depend on political alignment and access to emergency support.

**Suez: observed transmission.** The UK reserve position gained $285 million in the first seven months and lost $272 million in the last five, a net +$13 million. September's $177 million oil-company-sale receipts and December's $561 million IMF drawing materially changed the outcome. Removing these gives a hypothetical full-year drain of $725 million, not the actual reserve change. The current account remained in surplus; capital outflows and trade-payment leads and lags explain why a fixed parity could be under pressure without a current-account deficit. BIS reports that late-1956 freight indices rose about 20% above midyear levels, and the U.S. Gulf-Antwerp/Hamburg grain voyage rate reached about $20 per ton late in the year, versus $11.75 in March. Spot tanker quotations covered only a small share of oil transport and are not representative average costs. Petroleum delivery constraints extended into 1957; the January surge in U.S. oil exports and the March recovery are later outcomes, not 1956 observations.

The Hungarian and Polish crises exposed economic weaknesses inside the Soviet bloc. These events showed the political cost of consumer shortages, labor pressure, and heavy-industry-first planning. The uprising and Soviet intervention disrupted production and displaced people; BIS singled Hungary out from general industrial expansion, but a matched national 1956 output-loss estimate is Data not available, and the approximately 200,000 refugee figure is a crisis-period count extending into 1957. Hindsight risk: the socialist growth model could report high investment and output while accumulating welfare and legitimacy problems.

Inflation and monetary tightening were visible risks in market economies. Central banks were trying to slow price pressures without ending growth. Hindsight shows that the 1957 to 1958 downturn would soon test this balance. Commodity exporters also faced recurring vulnerability because primary-product earnings could move against import needs. Indonesia's debt repudiation and external deficit, Canadian investment-related foreign borrowing, and the French and Dutch external deterioration mattered independently of Suez. A global depression did not materialize in 1956, but that does not mean all sectors or countries escaped contraction.

A second hindsight risk was logistics dependence. Suez did not create a 1970s-style oil-price shock, but it revealed that oil routes and shipping constraints could matter macroeconomically. Containerization, while not a crisis, was a black-swan-like productivity seed: a small 1956 transport experiment became a major future driver of globalization. Its productivity importance is a later development, not a measured 1956 result.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1956 vs. 1955)

The main structural delta from 1955 was slower growth in the United States and Western Europe. BIS's contemporary comparison shows the United States and Western Europe both decelerating materially from 1955's rapid expansion: Western European GNP growth slowed from 6.0% to 3.6% and U.S. growth from 7.2% to 2.4%. Later MPD and BEA growth estimates differ in level but also show U.S. deceleration. The 1956 cycle was therefore still positive but less exuberant.

The UN export-quantum index rose from 114 to 124, compared with 105 to 114 a year earlier: growth of 8.77% versus 8.57%, within rounded-index precision and on the same restricted coverage.

The top-20 GDP membership was unchanged in the MPD reconstruction. Within the list, Mexico and Spain moved ahead of Indonesia. Japan continued to close distance with older European economies, while Canada and Italy remained strong. The USSR and China showed high estimated growth, but the confidence level is lower because of non-market accounting.

Policy shifted further toward restraint in several financial centers. The Fed raised the discount rate twice. Britain raised Bank Rate to 5.5% in February and faced Suez-related sterling pressure. West Germany tightened in March and May and then partially eased in September. Japan's tightening belongs to 1957, and Canada changed its operating rule, not merely its rate level. These moves were responses to inflation and external balance, not to a collapse in demand.

External positions shifted. Britain's full-year current balance improved while late-year reserve confidence weakened. France and the Netherlands moved toward deficits, and Canada's current deficit doubled in the contemporary report. Mexico's worker living-cost increase slowed from 16.0% to 4.8%, whereas São Paulo's proxy accelerated and India's 27-town living-cost index reversed a decline; these are basket-specific comparisons, not a global inflation decomposition.

China is an unresolved reconstruction conflict: NBS growth accelerated from 6.9% to 15.0%, while MPD/Wu slowed from 9.06% to 8.49%. Reporting only one as undisputed would conceal a material disagreement.

The development-policy delta was substantial. India began Plan II, Brazil began Kubitschek's development push, and Japan's official narrative changed from recovery to modernization. In microeconomic terms, 1956 also brought the U.S. interstate program, RAMAC, containerization, and TAT-1, which gave the year an outsized long-run innovation footprint. These 1956 laws, authorizations and announcements are not later completion dates or contemporaneous measured productivity gains.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

High-confidence data: U.S., U.K., Canadian, and many Western European monetary and national-account data are relatively reliable. MPD provides structured historical estimates, and central bank sources provide policy-rate data for the United States, United Kingdom, Germany, Japan, Canada, the Netherlands, Belgium and Sweden. WTI oil and official gold-price data are also high-confidence. Contemporary BIS 1957, UN 1957, Bank of Canada 1956 and Banxico 1956 tables, read with their footnotes and publication timing, underpin many country observations. UN and BIS may repeat national, IMF or OEEC origins; the Macrohistory Database and the original French equity documentation share a lineage; and Bank of England and Macrohistory Database UK inflation are not independent corroboration.

Medium-confidence data: Japan, India, Brazil, Mexico, Argentina, Spain, and Indonesia have usable historical reconstructions, but national accounting, exchange-rate controls, and inflation measurement are less comparable than modern series. Country-level real GDP growth from MPD is an estimate, not a single official 1956 national-account release. Germany is graded Medium because its GDP covers the combined territory while its financial indicators describe the Federal Republic, and Australia because its fiscal and currency observations are later reconstructions.

Low-confidence data: USSR, China, Poland, and other non-market economies require special caution. GDP is reconstructed from imperfect official and scholarly sources. Prices were administered, trade was planned, and output targets did not map cleanly onto market value added. The +/- 20 to 30 percent uncertainty rule is appropriate for these economies.

Conflicts across sources: MPD and PWT produce broadly similar top ordering for market economies, but differ in mid-rank economies because they use different PPP bases, national-account concepts, and country coverage; an independent PWT-based ranking for 1956 is Data not available. PWT does not include the USSR as a single 1956 economy, while MPD provides a Former USSR aggregate. Germany is another issue: MPD and PWT report Germany historically, while 1956 politics divided the Federal Republic and the GDR. China's MPD growth (8.49%) and NBS growth (15.0%) are different reconstructions. Sweden's discount-rate date differs between BIS (23 September) and the Riksbank (23 November). The UK public debt ratio is 128.33% in the Bank of England dataset and 143.76% in the Macrohistory Database. The Dow Jones close and the Nikkei 500 milestone rest on secondary compilations.

Indicator treatment: Germany's CPI, unemployment, policy-rate, debt, current-account and budget values refer to West Germany only and appear in the notes, while the DM rate is shown as the value. Parallel exchange quotations from the Macrohistory Database appear only in the notes, with official parities shown as values. City and regional cost-of-living indices (India's 27 towns, São Paulo, Buenos Aires, Mexico's worker basket, Poland's administered index and the Soviet state-retail index) are not presented as national CPIs. Fiscal diagnostics computed from revenue and expenditure are shown in the notes because fiscal years, perimeters and denominators differ. Japan's GNP-derived denominator, Canada's current-account unit conflict, Spain's unemployment source break and Belgium's balance-of-payments perimeter are prominent limitations rather than minor footnotes.

Weakest data: global corporate rankings, exchange-specific equity closes outside New York, copper and agricultural staple annual prices, unemployment rates outside the market economies, current-account balances, budget balances, and debt/GDP for many developing and planned economies. Where those figures are not established, the report states Data not available rather than inferring values. Data not available covers several situations: an actual blank in a published table, a concept that does not apply (such as a market policy rate in a planned economy), a definition conflict that blocks aggregation, or a series for which no comparable 1956 observation is available; it is not proof that nothing was recorded at the time.

Important data that simply does not exist in a modern comparable form for 1956: globally harmonized unemployment, consistent CPI across all top 20, sectoral GDP for every country, current-account-to-GDP for all countries, and corporate market-cap rankings. Healthcare output, patents and R&D, skill-specific wages and several country micro series are also incomplete. For a GitHub repository, the best practice is to store MPD/PWT estimates separately from official national series and to mark non-market economies explicitly.

**Historical polities.** The Federal Republic and the GDR require distinct profiles beneath the combined-Germany ranking; only the FRG has the monetary and fiscal data tabulated above. Whole 1956 Pakistan included both western and eastern wings, and a modern-boundary Pakistan record is not substituted for that polity. Comparable GDP, eight-field macro panels and detailed sector outcomes for the GDR and whole Pakistan are Data not available. Yugoslavia is retained outside the selected twenty with its listed constituents excluded, and no additional GDP row is created for these coverage issues.
