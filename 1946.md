# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 1946

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1946 was the first full postwar year, but not a normal peace economy. The dominant force was reconversion: labor, factories, public finance, and trade shifted away from total war. In the U.S., measured GDP fell as military output collapsed even while civilian demand revived. In Europe and Japan, destruction, broken transport, food shortages, fuel bottlenecks, inflation, and weak currencies dominated. U.S. real GDP fell 9.11% in the Maddison Project Database and 11.61% in the later BEA series, yet civilian employment rose from 51.4 million in the fourth quarter of 1945 to 56.8 million a year later as the armed forces shrank from about 10 million to 2 million.

The international monetary system existed more as blueprint than functioning market order. Bretton Woods had created the IMF and World Bank, and currencies were meant to be fixed but adjustable around the dollar-gold anchor. In practice, exchange controls, dollar rationing, sterling-area constraints, and bilateral finance dominated. Initial IMF par values were announced on 18 December, but the Fund's financial operations began only in 1947.

Food scarcity defined welfare in 1946. FAO warned of a serious grain import gap and shortages of fats, meat, sugar, and essentials. Truman described the world food shortage as an emergency. Germany and Japan relied on rationing and black markets, while the Soviet Union suffered famine in 1946-1947. Coal was the other binding input: BIS records hard-coal output of 189 million tons in Britain and 63 million tons in Western Germany, against prewar benchmarks of 230 and 136 million tons.

The year also embedded the postwar state. The U.S. passed the Employment Act, Britain nationalized the Bank of England and legislated coal nationalization, France built the Monnet planning framework, the Soviet Union launched the Fourth Five-Year Plan, Argentina created IAPI, and Japan’s occupation reforms attacked zaibatsu power.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 1946

**Theme 1: Reconversion and the GDP measurement trap.** GDP changes often reflected composition rather than welfare. U.S. output fell as military procurement ended, while civilian channels revived; in Mexico manufacturing volume rose 13.41% while mining-metallurgy fell 24.53%. Several European economies rebounded from low baselines; Germany and Japan remained trapped by occupation, currency disorder, and input shortages. Forward linkage: the U.S. avoided depression, Western Europe needed aid, and Germany/Japan required later stabilization.

**Theme 2: Bretton Woods before convertibility.** The dollar-gold system was legally important but operationally incomplete. Exchange controls and dollar scarcity mattered more than convertibility. Britain’s sterling system remained important but weak. Forward linkage: this gap helped lead to the Marshall Plan and later convertibility.

**Theme 3: Food, coal, and transport bottlenecks.** Food shortages affected Europe and Asia, while coal constrained European power, steel, rail, and heating. Transport damage made both worse. Key affected areas included Germany, Japan, the Soviet Union, Britain, France, the Netherlands, India, and Eastern Europe. Forward linkage: shortages made reconstruction a security issue.

**Theme 4: Inflation under controls.** Inflation was widespread but hard to measure. Official prices often missed black-market premiums or queues. Japan had extreme inflation, Germany had suppressed inflation and barter, and the U.S. saw post-control price pressure: annual-average CPI inflation was 8.3%, but the December-to-December rise was 18.1%. Forward linkage: later German and Japanese stabilizations addressed 1946-era disorder.

**Theme 5: State-led reconstruction and mixed economies.** State intervention expanded across systems. Soviet planning, French indicative planning, British nationalization, U.S. macro institutions, Argentine trade control, and Japanese occupation reform were different answers to postwar scarcity. Forward linkage: these institutions shaped growth models.

**Theme 6: Early Cold War economic separation.** Blocs were not fully formed, but the split was visible. The Soviet Union prioritized planned heavy industry; Western Europe depended on U.S. finance and imports; Germany’s division deepened. Forward linkage: the Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, German currency reform, and Comecon followed.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

World GDP: Data not available as a complete 1946 total, because the MPD 2020 country-level series has no usable 1946 entry for China and other large economies. The top 20 listed economies sum to about $5.345 trillion in 2011 international PPP dollars, but coverage is incomplete.

World growth rate: Data not available. Available indicators diverge: U.S. measured real GDP contracted, Western Europe rebounded unevenly, Germany and Japan stayed in emergency conditions, and Soviet/developing-country estimates are less comparable.

Global trade volume: Data not available. BIS reports trade of USD 47.9 billion in current dollars for 33 countries in 1946, against USD 26.1 billion in 1938; the sample covered nearly 60% of 1938 world trade, its 1945 figure covers only July-December, and current-dollar values are not volumes. Trade reopened but was constrained by exchange controls, damaged ports, shipping shortages, dollar scarcity, and import licensing.

Reserve currency dynamics: The dollar was the core reserve currency, with official gold convertibility at $35 per troy ounce for foreign monetary authorities. Sterling remained important but weakened.

Global inflation trends: Data not available as a unified global CPI. Inflation pressure was broad, but rationing and black markets weaken comparability.

Capital flows: Relief, loans, and reconstruction finance flowed mainly from the U.S. and other less-damaged exporters toward Europe and Asia, but 1946 flows were insufficient relative to needs. Large bilateral credits, such as Sweden's SKr1,000 million to the Soviet Union and Argentina's loans to Spain and Chile, were authorizations rather than 1946 disbursements.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The U.S. Federal Reserve still maintained wartime yield support: Treasury bills around 3/8 percent and long Treasury bonds around 2.5 percent. The U.K. Bank Rate was 2.00 percent. BIS year-end central-bank discount rates were 1% in the United States, 1.625% in France, 4% in Italy, Spain and Turkey, 1.5% in Canada and Switzerland, 2.5% in the Netherlands and Sweden, 3.5% in Denmark (down from 4%) and 3% in Belgium (up from 1.75%). The Federal Reserve abolished its preferential 0.5% rate on advances secured by government obligations in April-May, which did not change the ordinary 1% rate, and raised minimum stock-market margins to 100% in January. Germany and Japan lacked normal market monetary systems, while France, Italy, India, Australia, Argentina, Brazil, Spain, and Turkey relied heavily on exchange and credit controls. U.S. equities fell in 1946; many other stock markets were closed, thin, controlled, or not comparable.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

There was no GATT yet; trade was governed by controls, relief systems, occupation policy, sterling-area arrangements, and foreign-exchange rationing. The dollar shortage defined European policy debates. The contemporary BIS estimate put Britain's current-account deficit at GBP 400 million (1945: GBP 875 million), much larger than the later Bank of England reconstruction of -1.57% of GDP, and Canada's merchandise surplus narrowed from C$1,672 million to C$448 million. Spain was isolated, colonial conflicts affected Dutch, French, British, and Indian economic settings, and Germany’s occupation zones became more economically distinct.

### 3D. Commodities & Energy

Coal was Europe’s critical energy bottleneck. It constrained electricity, steel, railways, heating, and industrial recovery. BIS hard-coal output for 1946 was 189 million tons in Britain, 63 million in Western Germany, 47 million each in France and Poland, 23 million in Belgium, 15 million in Czechoslovakia and 8 million in the Netherlands; prewar benchmarks were 230, 136, 46, 61, 28, 13 and 13 million tons, with Poland measured on its new boundaries. Mexico's physical indices show crude petroleum up 13.06% and electricity up 12.63%. Oil mattered but was not yet the global macro-shock commodity it became after 1973, and OPEC did not exist. Food commodities were a macroeconomic issue because import gaps affected survival, politics, and external balances.

### 3E. Labor & Demographics

World population was 2,395.7 million in 1946 against 2,377.0 million in 1945 (+0.79%) in the Gapminder reconstruction, which is a smoothed estimate rather than a census count. Demobilization, refugees, displaced persons, and reconstruction needs reshaped labor markets. Modern unemployment rates are often not comparable. In rural economies, hidden underemployment mattered more than registered unemployment. U.S. manufacturing hourly earnings rose from USD 1.00 in December 1945 to USD 1.15 in December 1946, a nominal gain roughly matched by consumer prices. 1946 also began or intensified baby booms in several Western countries, increasing future demand for housing, schools, and consumer goods.

**Economic narrative:**

The United States remained the dominant world economy, but MPD 2020 and BEA-style real-output series show a sharp contraction as war procurement collapsed. This was a composition shock: military output fell faster than civilian production, housing, autos, and household durables could replace it.

Policy moved toward an explicit postwar stabilization state. The Employment Act of 1946 created the Council of Economic Advisers and Joint Economic Committee, while the Federal Reserve still supported Treasury debt with wartime yield pegs.

National-source checks point the same way with different magnitudes. The BEA series gives real GDP of USD 2,209.911 billion in chained 2017 dollars against USD 2,500.057 billion in 1945, and the annual mean of industrial production fell 13.58%. The Federal Reserve recorded civilian employment rising from 51.4 million in the fourth quarter of 1945 to 56.8 million a year later while the armed forces fell from about 10 million to 2 million, so demobilization did not translate one-for-one into unemployment.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Autos, appliances, housing, retailing, banking, and veterans’ services were the main reconversion channels. Labor disputes and price-control removal shaped firm margins and household purchasing power. Member-bank profits after tax were USD 758 million against USD 788 million in 1945 (-3.81%). Manufacturing hourly earnings rose from USD 1.00 in December 1945 to USD 1.15 in December 1946, a nominal gain largely offset by the 18.1% December-to-December rise in consumer prices. Stock-market minimum margins reached 100% in January.

## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

Active crises included Hungarian hyperinflation and currency replacement, China's civil-war inflation, the global food shortage, the Soviet famine, Germany’s black-market and currency breakdown, Japan’s inflation and food emergency, and Europe’s dollar shortage. These were not isolated events; each linked household welfare to foreign exchange, logistics, and state capacity.

Near-miss: U.S. policymakers feared demobilization could recreate Depression-scale unemployment. That risk did not materialize at the feared scale because private demand, savings, housing needs, and reconversion absorbed labor better than pessimists expected.

Hindsight risk: Bretton Woods solved interwar instability partly by creating a dollar-centered order, but the same asymmetry later produced the 1960s dollar-overhang problem. In 1946, however, the immediate issue was shortage of dollars, not excess dollars.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (1946 vs. 1945)

The structural break was the move from victory to reconstruction. In 1945, ending war and restoring administration were the core tasks. In 1946, governments had to allocate scarce food, fuel, dollars, labor, credit, and equipment.

The U.S. shifted from military procurement to civilian demand and created new macroeconomic institutions. Britain moved nationalization from platform to law. France moved planning from idea to apparatus. The Soviet Union began the Fourth Five-Year Plan while entering famine conditions. Germany and Japan moved from defeat to occupation administration but not yet to monetary stabilization. Argentina began the Peronist policy turn and nationalized its central bank; Brazil entered the Dutra period; Mexico moved toward the Alemán development model. U.S. annual-average CPI inflation accelerated by 6.0 percentage points to 8.3%, and Canada's merchandise surplus fell from C$1,672 million to C$448 million. Argentina's narrower goods-income measure rose only 1.76% against 8.94% total GDP growth in the Maddison Project Database. In the ranking, Denmark entered and Venezuela left, although the 1945 and 1946 rankings rest on different Maddison vintages.

New risks included post-control inflation, dollar shortage, food crisis, occupation currency breakdown, and Cold War economic separation. Risks that eased included fears of a U.S. demobilization depression and the total paralysis of trade and shipping seen in the final war phase.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

GDP ranking uses MPD 2020 as primary and Maddison 2010 as cross-check; the figures are real PPP estimates, not current-dollar national accounts. Reliable statistics: MPD-style GDP per capita and population for listed countries; year-end central-bank discount rates, IMF par values, coal output and trade values from the BIS annual report; Banco de México, Banca d'Italia, Argentine central bank and Federal Reserve annual-report tables; broad GDP ranking for the largest economies; Bretton Woods context; U.S. yield peg; U.S. Employment Act; UK nationalizations; Tokyo Stock Exchange closure and 1949 reopening; FAO and Truman food-crisis evidence.

Estimated or weaker data: Soviet GDP and famine interpretation; Japan’s extreme inflation; French planning effects; Argentina’s policy shift; lower-ranked GDP positions around ranks 19-23. Soviet and other non-market estimates may have very wide error bands.

Unavailable data: complete 1946 world GDP and growth; world trade volume; comparable unemployment for the USSR, India, France, Germany, Japan, Italy, Argentina, Brazil, Mexico and Turkey; consumer prices for the USSR, India, Germany, Argentina, Brazil, Mexico, Belgium and Turkey (Japan only as a low-comparability estimate); policy rates for the USSR, India, Germany, Japan, Argentina, Brazil, Australia and Mexico; debt/GDP for the USSR, India, France, Germany, Argentina, Brazil, Mexico and Turkey; current accounts for all but the United States, the United Kingdom, Italy, Spain, Australia, Sweden and Denmark; budget balances for all but the United Kingdom; annual exchange rates for all but the United Kingdom, France and Mexico; corporate rankings; equity returns for Canada, Argentina, Brazil, Turkey and India; national property prices; and most commodity prices.

Vintage break: the 1945 ranking uses Maddison 2010 levels in 1990 international dollars and 1946 uses MPD 2020 in 2011 international dollars, so no level change or rank move is computed across the two years; growth comparators in the indicator tables use the Maddison Project Database 2023 1945 rate. The MPD 2023 and MPD 2020 levels coincide for every listed economy except Spain (93,683.1 against 93,812.7 million). Germany's MPD change of -52.6% compares 1936 borders with present borders and is not reported as growth; the USSR has no usable 1945 observation. Japan's "about 364.5%" inflation is a low-comparability historical estimate: official CPI calculation began only in August 1946, and the Macrohistory full-year reconstruction gives +90.70%. The U.S. debt and current-account series, the UK current account (bullion treatment before 1946) and Swiss debt (central to general government) change definition in 1946, so no change is computed. U.S. real GDP falls 9.11% in MPD and 11.61% in the BEA series, and Argentine total GDP growth of 8.94% contrasts with a 1.76% rise in the central bank's goods income excluding commerce and services. Belgium's Macrohistory current account is a merchandise-trade ratio and its price series crosses a wartime reconstruction, so neither is shown as a value. IMF par values are year-end parities and appear only in Notes unless the rate is otherwise documented for 1946. Licensing: values from the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database and its rate-of-return series are used under the CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 licence, and Zurich rent indices from Historical Statistics of Switzerland under CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 FR, as stated in the references.

Historical data conflict: MPD 2020 and Maddison 2010 agree on the broad top group but differ near the cutoff. MPD 2020 ranks Denmark and Turkey 19-20; Maddison 2010 places Turkey and Venezuela inside the top 20 and Denmark outside. The cutoff is uncertain.

Most important missing case: China. No usable 1946 country-level China GDP entry exists in MPD 2020, so China is not ranked; civil-war inflation and UNRRA shipments of USD 271 million (cumulative to end-1946) are the main quantified Chinese indicators. That is a data gap, not a claim about economic size.
