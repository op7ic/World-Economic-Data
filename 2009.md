# MACRO/MICRO GLOBAL ECONOMIC ASSESSMENT - YEAR: 2009

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

2009 was the year in which the global financial crisis became a synchronized real-economy recession, and then began to bottom out under extraordinary public intervention. World GDP in current U.S. dollars fell from about $64.3 trillion in 2008 to about $61.0 trillion in 2009 on World Bank data. IMF real-output estimates show the first postwar contraction of global activity: the IMF World Economic Outlook database of October 2026 puts PPP-weighted world growth at -0.353 percent and market-exchange-rate-weighted growth at -2.023 percent. The WTO recorded an unprecedented collapse in merchandise trade value and a double-digit fall in trade volume, with merchandise-export volume down 12.2 percent in its March 2010 assessment, a different measure from the 23 percent fall in nominal merchandise exports. The most important economic fact of the year was not only the recession itself, but the degree to which private balance-sheet distress, banking losses, inventory liquidation, and cross-border trade finance stress transmitted the shock across nearly all large economies.

The recession was not symmetrical. The United States, Japan, Germany, the United Kingdom, Italy, Spain, Canada, Mexico, Russia, Turkey, the Netherlands, Switzerland, and Belgium contracted sharply. China, India, Indonesia, and Australia kept growing, helped by domestic demand, bank credit, fiscal stimulus, commodity links, or a smaller direct exposure to structured-credit losses. Brazil and South Korea suffered a severe trade and industrial shock but rebounded quickly as China-led commodity demand, easier monetary policy, and inventory rebuilding returned. Mexico's contraction was among the deepest in the top 20 because it combined U.S. demand dependence, manufacturing exposure, oil-revenue weakness, and the H1N1 shock. India also faced drought and food-price stress alongside external weakness.

Policy regime change defined the year. Major central banks moved to or near the zero lower bound, and the Federal Reserve and Bank of England made large-scale asset purchases a central policy instrument. The European Central Bank cut the main refinancing rate to 1.00 percent and relied on fixed-rate full-allotment liquidity operations and covered-bond purchases. Fiscal policy turned aggressively countercyclical in the United States, China, Japan, Europe, and many emerging markets. Announcements, authorizations, loan flows and cash outlays measure different things, so US fiscal stimulus, China's bank-credit expansion and central-bank asset purchases cannot be added into one spending total. This support stabilized output and financial markets, but it also shifted risk from private balance sheets to public balance sheets, setting the stage for the euro-area sovereign stress that intensified in 2010.

Microeconomically, 2009 was a year of demand destruction followed by selective reflation. Autos, construction, capital goods, luxury goods, shipping, and export manufacturing endured deep contractions. GM and Chrysler restructurings transferred operating assets to new legal entities rather than simply restoring old shareholders, and bank stress tests measured hypothetical losses and capital resilience rather than realized losses. Banking moved from panic toward repair, but credit conditions remained tight for households and small firms. Technology and telecommunications continued to gain share because digital demand was more resilient and because the smartphone, broadband, cloud-computing, and semiconductor cycles were becoming structural rather than purely cyclical. Commodity markets rebounded from late-2008 lows, especially oil, copper, and gold, but the rebound reflected policy liquidity and Chinese restocking as much as final demand, and annual averages for oil and copper were still far below 2008.

## 2. KEY THEMES OF 2009

**1. The first synchronized postwar global recession.** The economic-history significance of 2009 is that it converted a financial-market crisis into a global output and trade contraction. Advanced economies had the deepest output losses, but the trade shock transmitted distress to exporters everywhere. Germany and Japan contracted because their industrial and export models were directly exposed to collapsed external demand. Mexico, Korea, Russia, and Turkey were hit by the combined fall in trade, capital flows, and commodity prices. The forward linkage was the 2010-2011 policy debate about recovery, exit strategies, and public-debt sustainability.

**2. Emergency macro policy became a durable regime shift.** The Fed's 0 to 0.25 percent target range, the Bank of England's 0.5 percent Bank Rate and gilt purchases, the ECB's 1.0 percent main refinancing rate and liquidity allotments, and the Bank of Japan's 0.1 percent policy rate made unconventional policy normal for the next decade. At the time, these measures were framed as emergency stabilization. Support did not expand uniformly: Federal Reserve agency-MBS holdings rose while its dollar-liquidity swap balances fell from US$553.7 billion at end-2008 to US$10.3 billion at end-2009. In hindsight, these measures began the long period of ultra-low rates, expanded central bank balance sheets, and asset-price sensitivity to monetary guidance.

**3. China's stimulus changed the global recovery map.** China's credit and infrastructure stimulus limited the fall in Chinese growth and revived global demand for steel, copper, coal, oil, and construction inputs. New renminbi loans reached RMB 9.59 trillion in 2009, according to the People's Bank of China; these were bank-loan flows, not government outlays. This disproportionately helped Australia, Brazil, Indonesia, Korea, and commodity-linked industrial suppliers. The forward linkage was powerful: by 2010, emerging Asia and commodity exporters were recovering faster than many advanced economies, but China also accumulated local-government debt and property-sector imbalances that became more visible later.

**4. Trade and inventories amplified the cycle.** World merchandise trade value fell about 23 percent in 2009, and trade volume contracted sharply. The shock was larger than the decline in GDP because internationally traded goods are concentrated in durable goods, intermediate inputs, autos, electronics, and capital equipment. Inventory liquidation in late 2008 and early 2009 caused a self-reinforcing production halt. The forward linkage was the rapid 2010 rebound once inventories normalized, but also a lasting reassessment of global supply-chain fragility.

**5. Public balance sheets replaced private balance sheets as the visible risk.** Bank rescues, automatic stabilizers, discretionary stimulus, and recession-driven revenue losses pushed deficits and debt higher. The United States and United Kingdom had double-digit or near-double-digit general government deficits: -13.2 and -10.0 percent of GDP respectively in the IMF WEO October 2026. Japan's already high debt rose further. Euro-area public debt concerns became concrete late in 2009 when Greece's fiscal data problems triggered a loss of confidence. The forward linkage was the euro-area sovereign crisis and a global debate over austerity versus continued stimulus.

**6. Labor markets lagged the financial recovery.** Equity markets rebounded strongly from March 2009 lows, but unemployment kept rising in the United States, Spain, the United Kingdom, Canada, Turkey, Russia, and several euro-area economies. The ILO estimated global unemployment reached a record level. Youth unemployment and informal-sector stress were especially severe. Hours, temporary contracts, discouraged workers, migration and informal activity complicate national comparisons. The forward linkage was a slow household-deleveraging recovery in advanced economies and political pressure for job-focused stimulus.

**7. Emerging-market differentiation became clearer.** Countries with stronger bank balance sheets, less household leverage, policy room, and direct links to China recovered earlier. China, India, Indonesia, Korea, Brazil, and Australia performed better than many advanced economies. Russia and Mexico showed how commodity dependence or U.S. demand dependence could still create deep shocks. Australia raised rates late in the year while the Fed kept its near-zero range, an early sign of uneven normalization. The forward linkage was the post-2009 rise of emerging-market capital inflows and the later concern about overheating and exchange-rate appreciation.

## 3. MACROECONOMIC OVERVIEW

### 3A. Global Aggregates

World GDP in current U.S. dollars was about **$60.98 trillion** in 2009, down from about **$64.31 trillion** in 2008 on World Bank WDI data. Real global output contracted on IMF measures, with advanced economies falling much more sharply than emerging and developing economies. The IMF World Economic Outlook database of October 2026 gives world nominal GDP of US$61,326.5 billion and current-PPP GDP of 82,918.3 billion international dollars, PPP-weighted real growth of -0.353 percent, market-exchange-rate-weighted growth of -2.023 percent, world CPI inflation of 2.670 percent and goods-and-services trade volume growth of -10.213 percent. These are later retrospective estimates rather than contemporaneous releases, and the PPP-weighted and market-weighted growth rates answer different aggregation questions. The contraction was concentrated in late 2008 and early 2009, followed by stabilization in the second half of 2009 as stimulus, inventory restocking, and financial-market normalization took hold.

World merchandise trade experienced one of the sharpest declines in modern records. WTO data show the dollar value of world merchandise trade fell by about **23 percent** in 2009 to roughly **$12.1 trillion** (US$12.15 trillion in the WTO's 26 March 2010 release), down from about **$16.1 trillion** in 2008. Merchandise-export volume fell 12.2 percent and commercial-services exports fell 13 percent to US$3.31 trillion. Trade volume fell less than trade value because commodity prices and manufactured-goods prices also declined, but the volume shock was still severe. The WTO judged collapsing demand, product composition, supply chains and synchronization central to the decline, with trade finance shortages also contributing; the durable-goods composition of trade and inventory liquidation amplified the collapse. The WTO's 9.5 percent rebound for 2010 was a forecast, not a 2009 observation.

Reserve currency dynamics remained dollar-centered. The U.S. dollar was the dominant reserve currency, and the crisis initially strengthened demand for dollar liquidity. The Federal Reserve's swap lines with foreign central banks and the IMF's expanded lending capacity helped relieve dollar funding stress; by end-2009 outstanding Federal Reserve dollar-liquidity swaps had fallen to US$10.272 billion from US$553.728 billion a year earlier, a stock reduction rather than a measure of annual swap lending. The euro remained the second major reserve currency, but late-2009 concerns over Greece began to challenge the perception that euro-area sovereign risk was uniform. Exact 2009 COFER currency shares and a harmonized net international capital-flow total: Data not available.

Global inflation fell sharply as energy prices, capacity utilization, and wage pressure weakened. Several advanced economies recorded very low inflation or temporary deflation. Emerging-market inflation slowed but remained higher in Russia, India, Turkey, Brazil, Mexico, and Indonesia. Oil rebounded from crisis lows but averaged far below 2008. Brent monthly prices began 2009 near the low $40s per barrel and ended the year in the mid-$70s. Gold averaged about **$972 per troy ounce**, supported by safe-haven demand and concerns about monetary expansion. LME copper averaged about **$5,164 per metric ton**, recovering strongly from late-2008 lows as China restocked metals.

International capital flows moved from panic outflows to renewed emerging-market inflows during the year. UNCTAD estimated global FDI inflows fell by about **37 percent** in 2009 to roughly **$1.1 trillion**, reflecting weak corporate profits, scarce credit, and cancelled cross-border projects. Portfolio flows recovered earlier than FDI, especially into emerging-market equities and bonds after global risk appetite returned. Gross FDI, portfolio flows and central-bank swap liquidity are different measures and are not added together.

### 3B. Monetary & Financial Conditions

The interest-rate environment was extraordinarily loose. The Federal Reserve maintained a 0 to 0.25 percent federal funds target range and expanded asset purchases, including agency mortgage-backed securities, agency debt, and longer-term Treasuries. Agency-MBS purchases began on 5 January 2009, not at the November 2008 announcement; the December 2009 FOMC statement retained the target range and expected agency purchases to run through the first quarter of 2010, and they concluded on 31 March 2010 with settlements continuing afterward. At year-end the System Open Market Account held US$908.371 billion of agency MBS at par, with outstanding purchase commitments of US$160.099 billion, including US$32.838 billion of dollar rolls; because holdings are recorded on a settlement-date basis, neither figure equals cumulative gross purchases.

The Bank of England cut Bank Rate to 0.5 percent and launched a £75 billion asset purchase program in March, later expanded; in November the Asset Purchase Facility target was raised to £200 billion. Holdings at 31 December were £190.053 billion: £188.076 billion of gilts, £1.549 billion of corporate bonds and £0.429 billion of commercial paper, measured as settled purchase proceeds less redemptions and sales rather than market value. The ECB cut the main refinancing rate to 1.00 percent by May, effective from the operation settling on 13 May, and supplied liquidity through fixed-rate full-allotment operations; its €60 billion covered-bond purchase programme began buying in July 2009 and was completed in June 2010. The Bank of Japan kept the policy rate around 0.1 percent and used credit-easing measures; on 1 December it introduced a fixed-rate three-month funds-supplying operation at 0.1 percent, a different instrument from its overnight-call target. The Reserve Bank of Australia ended the year at 3.75 percent after late-year increases, still below the December 2008 level.

Exchange rates reflected both safe-haven flows and relative policy expectations. The yen was strong during the crisis, hurting Japanese exporters. The dollar was strong during acute funding stress but weakened against many risk currencies as risk appetite recovered. China kept the renminbi effectively stable against the dollar, which helped stabilize Chinese exporters but also intensified debate over external rebalancing. Many emerging-market currencies recovered in the second half of the year after deep 2008 depreciations.

Credit conditions improved but were not normal. Interbank spreads narrowed from crisis peaks, bank equity issuance resumed, and bond markets reopened for high-grade borrowers. Small firms, commercial real estate borrowers, households with impaired collateral, and leveraged companies still faced tight credit. A policy-rate endpoint does not establish the average bank borrowing cost, and tightening lending standards coexisted with accommodative central-bank rates. Bank balance sheets were supported by public capital, guarantees, liquidity facilities, and accounting changes. The policy success of 2009 was to prevent a complete collapse of the banking system; the policy limitation was that credit repair took years.

Equity markets bottomed in March 2009 and then rallied strongly. The S&P 500, Euro Stoxx, FTSE 100, DAX, Nikkei, Hang Seng, Shanghai Composite, Bovespa, Sensex, and RTS all rose from crisis lows. The rally reflected lower tail risk, liquidity, China's stimulus, inventory restocking, and depressed valuations. Bond markets showed a steepening yield curve in the United States and other economies as short rates were pinned near zero while longer yields rose with recovery expectations and debt supply.

### 3C. Trade & Geopolitics

2009 was not a year of a major global trade agreement; it was a year of crisis-management and protectionism avoidance. G20 leaders repeatedly pledged not to repeat the protectionist spiral of the 1930s. Some tariffs, local-content rules, bank-rescue conditions, and industrial subsidies nevertheless appeared; the WTO's observation that protectionism had not exploded was not a claim that no restrictive measures occurred. The U.S. safeguard tariff on Chinese tires and China's responses signaled that trade tensions would continue even during recession.

The G20 replaced the G7 as the central forum for crisis macro coordination. London and Pittsburgh summits expanded IMF resources, endorsed fiscal stimulus while economies remained weak, and supported financial regulatory reform. The global crisis also strengthened the political case for Basel III, although the final bank-capital framework came later; Basel III and the European sovereign rescue architecture were not completed 2009 reforms.

Geopolitically, economic flows were affected less by war than by credit contraction and policy coordination. Sanctions regimes existed, but the dominant cross-border economic constraint was private financial deleveraging. Regional integration continued: the EU faced internal stress but remained intact; ASEAN and Asian supply-chain integration benefited from China's rebound; Mercosur and Latin American trade were affected by commodity swings and regional demand.

Iron ore, steel, and coal markets were weaker than during the 2007-2008 boom but stabilized as Asia recovered. Agricultural staples were less dramatic than energy and metals: prices generally eased from 2008 food-crisis highs, as the IMF wheat, maize and rice benchmarks show, but food affordability remained a major stress in lower-income economies because incomes and employment were hit. ### 3E. Labor & Demographics

Labor markets deteriorated after output began stabilizing. The United States unemployment rate averaged above 9 percent and peaked at 10 percent in October. The Bureau of Labor Statistics release of 8 January 2010 reported December unemployment at 10.0 percent, with 15.3 million unemployed; construction and manufacturing cut jobs while health care added employment. That monthly seasonally adjusted rate is not the annual average or the ILO modeled estimate, and the release revised seasonal factors back to 2005. Spain's unemployment rate rose toward 18 percent, reflecting the collapse of construction and temporary employment. Turkey, Russia, Mexico, Canada, the United Kingdom, and many euro-area economies saw rising unemployment. Germany was a partial exception because short-time work schemes limited layoffs.

Migration flows were affected by recession. Construction-linked migrant workers in the Gulf, Spain, Ireland, the United Kingdom, and the United States faced weaker demand. Remittances slowed, affecting Mexico, South Asia, Eastern Europe, and parts of Southeast Asia. Demographic trends did not change suddenly, but 2009 reinforced structural divergence: aging in Japan and Europe increased fiscal pressure, while India, Indonesia, Mexico, and Turkey retained labor-force growth but faced job-quality problems. Migration, underemployment and hours adjustment prevent a single unemployment number from capturing the entire welfare loss. **Economic narrative:**

The U.S. economy was the crisis epicenter and the largest policy laboratory. Housing remained weak, household net worth had fallen sharply, and banks were still repairing balance sheets. The American Recovery and Reinvestment Act provided fiscal stimulus, while the Fed used zero-rate guidance, QE, and credit facilities. The policy aim was to stop a debt-deflation spiral, restore mortgage credit, and stabilize employment. The Congressional Budget Office reported US$107.8 billion of ARRA outlays through 30 September, against a classification-adjusted estimate of US$106.3 billion; these are fiscal-year 2009 outlays, not the multi-year US$787 billion cost of the act, and COBRA premium support was classified as lower revenue.

Microeconomically, autos, construction, banking, and commercial real estate were the weakest sectors. General Motors and Chrysler went through government-supported restructurings. Large banks raised capital after stress tests, but community banks and real estate lenders remained vulnerable. By 9 November, nine of the ten firms required to add capital buffers under the Supervisory Capital Assessment Program had met them, with GMAC the exception; the ten had increased Tier 1 common equity by over US$77 billion, including US$39 billion of new issuance, US$23 billion of preferred conversion and US$9 billion of business and asset sales, components that are not all fresh cash or taxpayer injections. Technology was more resilient: smartphones, cloud infrastructure, and internet advertising kept attracting investment. Households deleveraged, raised savings, and reduced discretionary spending, which slowed the consumer-led recovery.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Autos restructured; housing remained depressed; banks stabilized but credit remained tight; technology and online services outperformed; fiscal transfers supported household income. In December, construction and manufacturing cut jobs while health care added employment. In the OCC and OTS mortgage sample, performing first-lien loans fell to 86.4 percent at end-December despite fewer new foreclosure starts.

**Economic narrative:**

Japan's recession was driven by external demand. The collapse in global autos, electronics, and capital goods hit Japanese production more severely than domestic financial losses alone would suggest. The yen's strength worsened the export shock. Policy rates were already near zero, so fiscal stimulus measures and BOJ credit-easing measures carried much of the stabilization burden. The Bank of Japan's 1 December decision introduced three-month funds at a fixed 0.1 percent rate; the rate matched its overnight-call objective, but the maturity and operation differed, and it was not an adoption of the later quantitative-and-qualitative easing framework.

Corporate Japan cut production, employment hours, and investment. Toyota and Honda suffered from the global auto slump, while electronics producers faced weak consumer demand and intense Korean and Taiwanese competition. Deflation returned, reinforcing long-run concerns about nominal GDP stagnation. Japan's debt problem was visible but domestically financed, so it did not trigger the same market panic as later euro-area sovereign stress.

**Micro/sectoral highlights:** Autos and electronics contracted; exporters were squeezed by yen strength; deflation hurt pricing power; aging demographics remained a structural drag; public works and stimulus cushioned demand.

### 5B. Corporate landscape

The largest companies by revenue in or around 2009 were dominated by energy, retail, autos, and finance: Walmart, Royal Dutch Shell, ExxonMobil, BP, Toyota, Sinopec, China National Petroleum, State Grid, Chevron, and Total were among the top global revenue groups. Revenue rankings were distorted by oil-price volatility, so energy companies saw large dollar-revenue swings even when production changed less. Lists published in 2009 often refer to earlier fiscal-year revenue, and fiscal-year ends and currencies differ across firms.

Major corporate events reflected crisis repair. General Motors and Chrysler were restructured with government support in the United States. Chrysler filed on 30 April, and substantially all of its assets were transferred to the new Chrysler Group LLC on 10 June; the GAO reported a US$6.6 billion working-capital commitment to New Chrysler, of which US$4.6 billion had been drawn by 30 September. For GM, the 26 June purchase agreement, the 5 July sale order and the 10 July closing were separate steps: on 10 July substantially all old GM assets were sold under section 363 of the Bankruptcy Code to the distinct General Motors Company (formerly NGMCO), while the old General Motors Corporation became Motors Liquidation Company and continued winding down. Consideration included a credit bid, securities and assumed liabilities rather than a simple cash price, and the US Treasury's 60.8 percent common-equity stake in New GM was not a 60.8 percent share of every liability or of Old GM.

Financial institutions raised capital after stress tests. The Supervisory Capital Assessment Program, published on 7 May, tested 19 bank holding companies under hypothetical 2009-2010 scenarios, not forecasts: it estimated US$599.2 billion of hypothetical losses before US$64.3 billion of purchase-accounting adjustments, and the preliminary US$185 billion capital buffer was reduced to US$74.6 billion by completed or contracted capital actions and first-quarter results. The AIG Credit Facility Trust was formed on 16 January 2009 and convertible preferred shares were issued on 4 March, giving the trust approximately 78 percent of common ownership on conversion, with voting rights exercised by independent trustees rather than the Federal Reserve Bank of New York; the 2008 credit agreement and the 2009 equity issuance were distinct events. Lloyds and RBS remained under UK state influence. Fortis and Dexia remained central to Belgian financial repair. In technology, smartphones and mobile ecosystems accelerated, with Apple's iPhone momentum and Android's expansion reshaping consumer electronics. In China, state banks and infrastructure-linked firms expanded rapidly because stimulus was credit-intensive.

M&A was depressed relative to the pre-crisis boom, but strategic deals and distressed restructurings continued. Mining and energy companies reassessed projects after commodity prices fell, then restarted plans as prices recovered. The corporate winners were firms with liquidity, state backing, domestic-demand exposure, or secular technology demand. The losers were highly leveraged firms, construction developers, banks with weak asset quality, and exporters tied to capital-goods cycles. ### 5C. Innovation & technology

2009 did not look like a technology revolution year at first glance because the macro environment was weak. In hindsight, it was important for digital platforms. Smartphone adoption accelerated, app ecosystems grew, and mobile data became a mass-market business. Cloud computing shifted from experimental to mainstream corporate planning. Semiconductor demand rebounded later in the year as electronics inventories normalized.

R&D spending was pressured in cyclical industries but more resilient in pharmaceuticals, software, defense-related research, and large technology firms. Patent activity continued to internationalize, with Korea, China, Japan, the United States, and Europe all central to global innovation flows. Infrastructure megaprojects were most visible in China, where stimulus financed rail, roads, power, and urban construction; bank project-loan approval identifies the financing channel, but it is not the same as completion of a railway, road or power plant. ### 5D. Labor markets & human capital

The global labor shock was large and uneven. Manufacturing workers, construction workers, young workers, migrants, and temporary workers were hit hardest. Germany's short-time work scheme reduced layoffs, while Spain's temporary-contract structure amplified unemployment. In the United States, long-term unemployment became a major social and macroeconomic concern; the December employment report showed job losses in construction and manufacturing alongside gains in health care. In India, the drought qualified the picture of uniform rural resilience.

Wages generally slowed, but nominal wage rigidity meant employment carried much of the adjustment. Informal labor markets in India, Indonesia, Mexico, Brazil, and Turkey absorbed stress that official unemployment measures often undercounted. Education and skills policy became more important because the crisis accelerated the divide between tradable routine work and higher-skill digital or professional services. ### 6B. Fixed income

Short rates were pinned near zero in the United States, Japan, the United Kingdom, and Switzerland, and at 1 percent in the euro area. Yield curves steepened because investors expected recovery, public borrowing rose, and central banks anchored the front end. Approximate 10-year government yields around year-end were: United States near 3.8 percent, Germany near 3.4 percent, United Kingdom near 4.0 percent, Japan near 1.3 percent, Canada near 3.6 percent, Italy and Spain around 4 percent. Credit spreads narrowed from crisis levels but remained above pre-crisis norms. Sovereign risk became more visible late in the year with Greece. Central-bank holdings were a large part of the story: the Federal Reserve held US$908.371 billion of agency MBS at par at year-end and the Bank of England's Asset Purchase Facility held £188.076 billion of gilts.

### 6C. Currencies

The dollar weakened after the panic phase as risk appetite returned, but it remained the central funding and reserve currency. The yen stayed strong, hurting Japanese exporters. The euro recovered during much of 2009, although Greek fiscal concerns began undermining confidence late in the year. Commodity currencies such as the Australian dollar, Canadian dollar, and Brazilian real rebounded as China demand and risk appetite improved. The Chinese renminbi was effectively stable against the U.S. dollar.

Several emerging-market currencies were still weaker on annual averages than before the crisis. Russia managed a ruble depreciation. Mexico's peso and Korea's won recovered from stressed levels but remained volatile. Capital controls were not the dominant 2009 policy story, but several countries considered or used measures to manage inflows as risk appetite returned. On WDI official annual averages, local currency per dollar rose 27.7 percent for the ruble, 21.4 percent for the peso, 19.1 percent for the lira, 18.0 percent for sterling, 16.1 percent for the won and 5.4 percent for the euro, while it fell 9.5 percent for the yen and 1.7 percent for the renminbi. These annual-average changes do not establish year-end appreciation or depreciation.

### 6D. Alternative assets

Gold was one of the defining alternative assets of 2009. The average gold price was about $972 per troy ounce, and the price moved above $1,000 as investors hedged financial-system risk, dollar weakness, and future inflation concerns. Oil recovered from late-2008 lows, with Brent moving from the low $40s early in the year to the mid-$70s by December. Copper and other industrial metals rallied sharply as China restocked.

Real estate diverged. U.S. housing stabilized but remained weak. The OCC and OTS Mortgage Metrics Report for the fourth quarter covered nearly 34 million first-lien loans with almost US$6 trillion of principal, over 64 percent of outstanding US mortgages; performing loans fell to 86.4 percent at end-December despite fewer new foreclosure starts. Servicer delays and loss-mitigation reviews can lower starts while serious delinquency rises, and HAMP trial plans were not permanent modifications. Spain and parts of the UK and Ireland were still in correction. China's property market revived under credit stimulus, with the NBS 70-city sales-price measure up 1.5 percent and rents down 0.6 percent. Commercial real estate was a major concern in the United States, United Kingdom, and parts of Europe because vacancies rose and refinancing was difficult. ## 7. RISKS, CRISES & BLACK SWANS

The active crisis was the global financial crisis and recession. The main near-miss was a deeper banking collapse, avoided through central-bank liquidity, public guarantees, capital injections, deposit insurance expansion, and fiscal support. The stress tests and asset purchases reduced immediate uncertainty but did not certify solvency under every scenario or normal credit availability, and mortgage delinquency could worsen despite fewer foreclosure starts. Another near-miss was a full protectionist trade spiral; governments used some defensive measures, but they did not recreate the 1930s tariff collapse.

Country shocks layered onto the global downturn. The H1N1 outbreak added a service-sector shock to Mexico's export contraction, and India's drought threatened crops and food costs even as industry recovered. In Egypt, outside the top twenty, the IMF's April 2010 Article IV notice reported growth of 4.7 percent in fiscal year 2008/09 (July-June), supported by accelerated investment, consumption, construction, communications and trade; the Central Bank of Egypt cut rates six times between February and September, and property-tax, VAT and subsidy reforms were postponed rather than implemented in 2009.

Systemic vulnerabilities building in 2009 included public-debt risk, central-bank exit risk, moral hazard in too-big-to-fail banking, China's credit and property imbalances, and the possibility that labor-market scarring would reduce potential output. The Greek fiscal-data crisis late in 2009 was a clear warning that the next phase could shift from private financial risk to sovereign risk.

Hindsight clearly matters. At the time, policymakers were primarily trying to prevent depression. In hindsight, some support measures delayed bank restructuring, encouraged asset-price dependence on monetary support, or shifted risk into public debt. The effectiveness and distributional cost of the emergency measures remain debated, although the prevailing view among major institutions is that aggressive intervention prevented a more catastrophic collapse.

## 8. YEAR-OVER-YEAR DELTA (2009 vs. 2008)

The structural break from 2008 to 2009 was the move from financial panic to real-economy collapse and policy backstop. In 2008 the crisis escalated with Lehman Brothers, AIG, bank rescues, and liquidity panic. In 2009 the crisis showed up in unemployment, trade, industrial output, fiscal deficits, and sovereign balance sheets.

World GDP in current dollars fell, and world trade value fell by about 23 percent. Inflation moved from commodity-driven pressure in 2008 to disinflation or deflation in 2009. Oil moved from a record high above $140 per barrel in mid-2008 to crisis lows and then partial recovery; on annual averages, IMF Brent fell 36.7 percent and the US first-purchase price 40.1 percent despite the within-year rebound. Equity markets moved from collapse in 2008 to a strong rebound in 2009 after March.

The ranking shifts were meaningful but not revolutionary. China nearly caught Japan in nominal GDP and would pass it in 2010. Russia fell from ninth to twelfth because oil prices, the ruble, and output collapsed. India rose as its domestic-demand economy kept growing. Indonesia and Belgium entered the World Bank top 20, while Poland and Saudi Arabia dropped out under the same country coverage.

Policy also pivoted. The Fed, BOE, ECB, BOJ, Bank of Canada, RBA, Banco Central do Brasil, RBI, Bank of Korea, Bank Indonesia, and others were in easing or emergency mode for most of the year. Intervention changed composition rather than growing uniformly: Federal Reserve emergency swap balances contracted sharply while its mortgage holdings expanded. By late 2009, Australia and some emerging economies were already thinking about normalization, while the United States, Japan, the United Kingdom, and euro area remained focused on avoiding relapse; Federal Reserve agency purchases and the ECB covered-bond programme were still unfinished at year-end.

## 9. DATA CONFIDENCE & GAPS

Data confidence is highest for GDP, inflation, exchange rates, central-bank policy rates, and fiscal indicators for the United States, Japan, Canada, Australia, and European OECD economies. It is also high for headline GDP in China, India, Brazil, Mexico, Korea, Turkey, Indonesia, and Russia, but comparability is lower for unemployment, fiscal balance, public debt, and some price measures. India's unemployment data for 2009 are not comparable to OECD-style annual unemployment; the ILO modeled estimate is shown instead. China's registered urban unemployment is not equivalent to a national labor-force survey. Indonesia, Mexico, Turkey, Brazil, and Russia also have labor-market informality that makes headline unemployment incomplete. Monthly US unemployment and Chinese registered urban unemployment are not substitutes for annual modeled rates. Policy-rate values are dated decisions where available and approximate year-end settings otherwise, notably for India, Brazil, Korea, Turkey, Indonesia and Switzerland.

GDP rankings conflict slightly across World Bank, IMF, and UN series because of revisions, exchange-rate conversion, fiscal-year treatment, and coverage of territories or non-sovereign economies. Taiwan is a special case: the IMF includes Taiwan Province of China, while the World Bank country GDP series used here has no Taiwan GDP data. For consistent country coverage, the main top-20 list uses World Bank country series and flags Taiwan separately. The ranking levels are current US dollars at market exchange rates; the Maddison Project Database 2023 levels shown in the country headers are in constant 2011 international dollars and the IMF WEO PPP aggregates are in current international dollars, so they are comparisons only and are not mixed with the WDI current-dollar levels. On those purchasing-power measures the order differs, with China second and India third.

Government debt and deficit estimates are among the weakest cross-country comparisons. General government coverage, public corporation treatment, local-government liabilities, bank-rescue accounting, and off-budget entities differ materially. China's official public debt figure understates the broader public-sector obligations created through local-government financing vehicles; the IMF's specified inclusion of part of new local-government financing vehicle issuance begins only in 2010. Brazil's debt concepts differ across net, gross, and public-sector definitions, and the IMF gross debt measure includes federal securities held by the central bank. Japan's debt is high but domestically financed, making market-risk interpretation different from externally financed sovereign stress. India's IMF fiscal and national-account rows are on an April-March fiscal year. For the Netherlands, Switzerland and Belgium, and for Korean debt and fiscal balance, indicators are approximate values.

Sectoral and corporate data are less standardized than macro data. Revenue rankings depend on fiscal-year reporting and exchange rates. Market-cap rankings changed quickly during the equity rally. M&A, bankruptcies, and nationalizations are well documented for major advanced economies but less uniformly reported in emerging markets. Real-estate data for 2009 are uneven across countries and cities.
